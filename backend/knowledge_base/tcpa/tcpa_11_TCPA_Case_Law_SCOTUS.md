## Scope of this file  
  
This file collects every Supreme Court decision construing the TCPA (47 U.S.C. § 227) or addressing dispositive procedural questions arising under it. The TCPA itself has produced a modest, but doctrinally heavy, set of Supreme Court decisions: jurisdictional gateway (*Mims*); mootness/Rule 68 (*Campbell-Ewald*); First Amendment severability (*Barr/AAPC*); the statutory definition of an automatic telephone dialing system (ATDS) (*Duguid*); and the relationship between the TCPA and the Hobbs Act (*PDR Network*). Each entry is keyed to its public-domain reporter and verified against Justia, Cornell LII, FindLaw, or the slip opinion as posted by the Court.

## Mims v. Arrow Financial Services, LLC, 565 U.S. 368 (2012)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2012 (decided January 18, 2012)  
**Vote:** 9-0  
**Author:** Justice Ginsburg  
  
### Holding  
Federal district courts have federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 over private suits brought under the TCPA. The TCPA's grant of jurisdiction to state courts in § 227(b)(3) is permissive and concurrent, not exclusive; it does not divest federal courts of their § 1331 authority.  
  
### Facts  
Marcus Mims sued Arrow Financial Services in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Florida, alleging that Arrow had repeatedly used an autodialer or prerecorded voice to call his cellphone in connection with a debt-collection effort, in violation of § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). The district court dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, reading § 227(b)(3) ("A person or entity may, if otherwise permitted by the laws or rules of court of a State, bring [an action] in an appropriate court of that State") as vesting exclusive jurisdiction in state courts. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed. Mims sought certiorari to resolve a circuit split.  
  
### Key reasoning  
The Court began with the longstanding presumption that federal courts possess federal-question jurisdiction whenever federal law creates a private right of action and provides the substantive rule of decision. That presumption can be overcome only when Congress has expressly or impliedly divested federal courts of jurisdiction.  
  
The Court found no such divestment. Section 227(b)(3) speaks only of an authorization for state-court suits; nothing in its text, structure, or legislative history "indicates" that Congress intended to make state courts the exclusive arbiters of TCPA claims. The phrase "if otherwise permitted by the laws or rules of court of a State" simply confirms that state procedural rules may govern such state-court actions; it is not a withdrawal of federal jurisdiction. Concurrent jurisdiction was, the Court emphasized, the normal default, and Congress legislates against that default. The Court also observed that the TCPA does not impose a damages cap small enough to suggest Congress wanted only small-claims-court adjudication, and that nationwide TCPA class actions would be unworkable if federal jurisdiction were unavailable.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- Plaintiffs may file private TCPA actions in either federal or state court.  
- *Mims* expressly authorizes removal of TCPA suits to federal court under 28 U.S.C. § 1441 where the requirements of § 1331 are met.  
- For class actions, *Mims* (combined with the Class Action Fairness Act) effectively secured federal-court availability for nationwide TCPA classes.  
- *Mims* did not address Article III standing — that question is governed by *Spokeo*, *TransUnion*, and the post-*TransUnion* circuit cases (see tcpa_12 §G).  
  
### Related statute sections  
47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(3) (private right of action); 28 U.S.C. § 1331 (federal-question jurisdiction); 28 U.S.C. § 1441 (removal).

## Campbell-Ewald Co. v. Gomez, 577 U.S. 153 (2016)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2016 (decided January 20, 2016)  
**Vote:** 6-3  
**Author:** Justice Ginsburg (Roberts, C.J., Scalia, and Alito, JJ., dissenting)  
  
### Holding  
An unaccepted Rule 68 offer of judgment, even one that would provide complete relief to the named plaintiff, does not moot the plaintiff's individual claim or the putative class action. The Court reserved the question of whether actual payment (rather than a mere offer) would moot the case.  
  
### Facts  
The United States Navy contracted with Campbell-Ewald, an advertising agency, to run a recruiting campaign. Campbell-Ewald, in turn, hired Mindmatics, which sent over 100,000 text messages to consumers (allegedly including Jose Gomez) who had not consented. Gomez filed a putative class action under § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). Before class certification, Campbell-Ewald served a Rule 68 offer of judgment offering Gomez treble statutory damages ($1,503 per text) plus an injunction. Gomez allowed the offer to lapse. Campbell-Ewald moved to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, arguing that the complete-relief offer mooted the case. The district court denied the motion. The Ninth Circuit affirmed.  
  
### Key reasoning  
The majority resolved the question using "basic principles of contract law." An unaccepted offer is a legal nullity: it creates no rights or obligations, and once it lapses (under Rule 68's 14-day window) the parties are returned to their pre-offer adverse posture. Citing Justice Kagan's dissent in *Genesis Healthcare Corp. v. Symczyk*, 569 U.S. 66 (2013), the Court endorsed the view that "an unaccepted settlement offer—like any unaccepted contract offer—is a legal nullity, with no operative effect." Adversity therefore persists, and the district court retains Article III jurisdiction.  
  
The Court further held that Campbell-Ewald was not entitled to derivative sovereign immunity merely because it was acting on the Navy's behalf; *Yearsley v. W. A. Ross Construction Co.*, 309 U.S. 18 (1940), immunizes contractors only when the contractor "violated both federal law and the Government's explicit instructions."  
  
The Court explicitly declined to decide whether the result would differ if the defendant deposited the full amount in an escrow account or with the court and the court entered judgment for the plaintiff over the plaintiff's objection — that scenario was "hypothetical."  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- Pick-off attempts via Rule 68 are not a path to mooting TCPA class actions.  
- The "actual tender" loophole reserved in *Campbell-Ewald* has produced a wave of subsequent litigation; lower courts have largely (but not uniformly) rejected actual-tender mootness theories.  
- The decision also clarified the limits of derivative sovereign immunity for government contractors implicated in TCPA conduct.  
- *Campbell-Ewald* is frequently invoked alongside *Mims* to defeat early-stage jurisdictional attacks on TCPA class actions.  
  
### Related statute sections  
47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii); Fed. R. Civ. P. 68; Article III "case or controversy" mootness doctrine.

## PDR Network, LLC v. Carlton & Harris Chiropractic, Inc., 588 U.S. 1 (2019)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2019 (decided June 20, 2019)  
**Vote:** 9-0 (vacated and remanded; Kavanaugh, J., concurring in the judgment, joined by Thomas, Alito, and Gorsuch, JJ.; Thomas, J., concurring in the judgment)  
**Author:** Justice Breyer  
  
### Holding  
The Court vacated the Fourth Circuit's decision holding that the Hobbs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2342, required district courts to defer to the FCC's 2006 Order interpreting "unsolicited advertisement" under § 227(a)(5). The Court remanded for the Fourth Circuit to consider two antecedent questions: (1) whether the 2006 Order is an interpretive or legislative rule, because the Hobbs Act's exclusive-review provision applies only to legislative-style "final orders"; and (2) whether PDR had a "prior, adequate, and exclusive opportunity" for pre-enforcement judicial review of the Order.  
  
### Facts  
PDR Network publishes the Physicians' Desk Reference. It sent unsolicited faxes to healthcare providers offering a "free" electronic copy. Carlton & Harris Chiropractic, a recipient, brought a putative class action under § 227(b)(1)(C), arguing the fax was an "unsolicited advertisement" under the FCC's 2006 Order, which had stated that faxes offering free goods or services constitute advertisements. The district court dismissed, holding the fax was not an advertisement; the Fourth Circuit reversed, holding that the Hobbs Act required deference to the FCC's interpretation.  
  
### Key reasoning  
Justice Breyer's opinion sidestepped the merits of the Hobbs Act question by identifying two preliminary issues. First, an order is reviewable under the Hobbs Act only if it is a "final order"; an interpretive rule that "merely advises the public of an agency's construction" may not qualify. Second, the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 703, requires "any prior, adequate, and exclusive opportunity for judicial review" of an agency action; if no such opportunity existed, a party may challenge the order in a subsequent enforcement proceeding.  
  
Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence (joined by three justices) would have gone further and held that the Hobbs Act never binds district courts to follow FCC interpretations of the TCPA where the agency rule is interpretive in nature. Justice Thomas, separately, would have addressed constitutional avoidance concerns under the non-delegation doctrine.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- The decision left the Hobbs Act question unresolved at the Supreme Court level, prompting a circuit split that continues.  
- On remand, the Fourth Circuit ultimately treated the FCC Order as interpretive and not binding under the Hobbs Act (*Carlton & Harris Chiropractic v. PDR Network*, 982 F.3d 258 (4th Cir. 2020)). The Eleventh Circuit reached a similar conclusion in *Gorss Motels v. Safemark Systems* (see tcpa_12 §F).  
- Bot should advise: where an FCC TCPA order is "interpretive," district courts may consider arguments that the agency interpretation is wrong; where the order is "legislative" and there was an adequate pre-enforcement review opportunity, the Hobbs Act likely bars collateral attack.  
  
### Related statute sections  
47 U.S.C. § 227(a)(5) (unsolicited advertisement); 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(C) (junk faxes); 28 U.S.C. § 2342 (Hobbs Act); 5 U.S.C. § 703 (APA).

## Barr v. American Association of Political Consultants, Inc., 591 U.S. 610 (2020)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2020 (decided July 6, 2020)  
**Vote:** Plurality (6 Justices held the government-debt exception unconstitutional; 7 Justices voted to sever rather than strike the entire restriction)  
**Author:** Justice Kavanaugh (plurality opinion, joined by Roberts, C.J., Alito and Thomas, JJ. as to severability; Sotomayor, J., concurring in the judgment; Breyer, J., concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part, joined by Ginsburg and Kagan, JJ.; Gorsuch, J., concurring in the judgment in part and dissenting in part, joined by Thomas, J.)  
  
### Holding  
The 2015 amendment to § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii) — which created an exception permitting robocalls to cellular phones "made solely to collect a debt owed to or guaranteed by the United States" — is a content-based restriction on speech that violates the First Amendment. The remedy is to sever the government-debt exception; the rest of the TCPA's robocall restriction remains in force.  
  
### Facts  
Plaintiffs were political consultants, pollsters, and non-profits who wished to place automated calls to cellphones to engage in political speech, fundraising, and issue advocacy. They argued that the 2015 government-debt-collection exception meant the cellphone-robocall ban was no longer content-neutral, and that under strict scrutiny the entire ban must fall. The Fourth Circuit agreed that the exception was content-based but severed the exception rather than invalidating the entire robocall ban.  
  
### Key reasoning  
Six Justices agreed that the government-debt exception is content-based: whether the call is permitted depends on the subject matter (collection of federal debt). Under *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155 (2015), content-based restrictions trigger strict scrutiny. The government's asserted interest in collecting its own debts could not justify favoring that speech over political and other speech of equal or greater constitutional weight.  
  
On severability, the plurality applied the "strong presumption of severability." Section 608 of the Communications Act expressly provides that invalidated provisions shall be severed. The plurality concluded that Congress would have preferred the robocall ban without the government-debt carve-out to no robocall ban at all. Justice Sotomayor concurred in the judgment, applying intermediate scrutiny but reaching the same severability result. Justice Breyer (with Justices Ginsburg and Kagan) would have upheld the 2015 amendment as well-tailored to a substantial governmental interest. Justice Gorsuch (with Justice Thomas) would have refused to sever, leaving plaintiffs free to make the calls they wished.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- Section 227(b)(1)(A)(iii) survives — robocalls to cellphones without prior express consent remain illegal.  
- The government-debt exception is dead; calls placed during the 2015–2020 window pursuant to that exception have generated their own line of cases (most courts have held that callers cannot be sued for calls made during the period the exception was on the books, though some judges have disagreed).  
- *AAPC* is a landmark First Amendment case extending beyond the TCPA: it confirms that *Reed* applies vigorously to commercial-adjacent restrictions and that severance, not wholesale invalidation, is the preferred remedy.  
- Bot should explain: political robocalls to cellphones still require prior express consent under the TCPA. *AAPC* did not legalize political robocalls.  
  
### Related statute sections  
47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii); 47 U.S.C. § 608 (severability); First Amendment.

## Facebook, Inc. v. Duguid, 592 U.S. 395 (2021)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2021 (decided April 1, 2021)  
**Vote:** 9-0 (Justice Alito concurring in the judgment)  
**Author:** Justice Sotomayor  
  
### Holding  
To qualify as an "automatic telephone dialing system" (ATDS) under 47 U.S.C. § 227(a)(1), a device must have the capacity either (A) to store a telephone number using a random or sequential number generator, or (B) to produce a telephone number using a random or sequential number generator. A device that stores and dials numbers from a customer-supplied list, without using a random- or sequential-number generator, is not an ATDS.  
  
### Facts  
Noah Duguid received text-message security alerts from Facebook even though he did not have a Facebook account and had never given his number to Facebook. The texts were sent by Facebook's automated login-notification system when someone attempted to access an account associated with his number. Duguid brought a putative class action alleging violations of § 227(b)(1)(A). The Ninth Circuit, applying its earlier *Marks* decision, held that Facebook's system was an ATDS because it had the capacity to store and automatically dial numbers, even though it did not generate them randomly or sequentially.  
  
### Key reasoning  
The Court resolved the long-running circuit split by applying ordinary rules of statutory grammar. The phrase "using a random or sequential number generator" in § 227(a)(1)(A) is a modifying clause that, under the series-qualifier canon, attaches to both "store" and "produce" — not just to "produce." Reading the statute otherwise would untether the term "automatic telephone dialing system" from the harm Congress aimed at: random or sequential dialing of unknown numbers. Justice Sotomayor noted that Duguid's broader reading would "capture virtually all modern cellphones, which have the capacity to store telephone numbers to be called and dial such numbers automatically." That result, the Court held, would render the statute absurd.  
  
The Court also rejected the argument grounded in footnote 7 of the opinion that "random or sequential number generation" could refer to selecting which number to dial from a stored list. The Court held that the generator must operate on the telephone numbers themselves, not on a stored list of pre-existing numbers.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- *Duguid* eliminated the vast majority of ATDS claims premised on modern predictive dialers, "click-to-call" platforms, and CRM-driven dialing systems that work from customer-supplied lists.  
- *Duguid* does not affect § 227(b)(1)(A)'s separate prohibition on artificial or prerecorded voice calls — those remain actionable regardless of dialing technology. (See *Lindenbaum v. Realgy* and other post-*Duguid* prerecorded-voice cases discussed in tcpa_04.)  
- *Duguid* does not affect § 227(c) Do-Not-Call Registry claims, which do not require ATDS use. Plaintiffs have shifted in volume from § 227(b) ATDS theories to § 227(c) DNC and internal-DNC theories.  
- "Footnote 7" arguments — that a system using a random or sequential number generator to *select* numbers from a list is an ATDS — have been rejected by every circuit to reach the question (see *Borden v. eFinancial*, *Beal v. Outfield Brew House* in tcpa_12 §B).  
- For the bot: a defendant's system is likely *not* an ATDS post-*Duguid* if it dials only customer-supplied numbers and lacks the capacity to generate random or sequential phone numbers. The "capacity" inquiry remains relevant, but plaintiffs must plead and prove the existence of random/sequential phone-number-generation functionality.  
  
### Related statute sections  
47 U.S.C. § 227(a)(1) (ATDS definition); 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A) (ATDS-call prohibition); 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(1).
