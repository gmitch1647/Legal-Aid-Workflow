# TCPA Topic 06 — Revocation of Consent  
  
**Statute:** 47 U.S.C. § 227(b), (c)  
**Regulations:** 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(10); 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(d)  
**Key FCC orders:** In re Rules and Regs. Implementing the TCPA, 30 FCC Rcd. 7961 (2015) (2015 Omnibus Order); In re Targeting and Eliminating Unlawful Text Messages, FCC 24-24 (rel. Feb. 16, 2024) (2024 Revocation Order); Bureau extension orders DA 25-312 (Apr. 7, 2025) and DA 26-12 (Jan. 6, 2026)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
---  
  
## 1. Overview  
  
Revocation of consent is the principal "off-switch" in TCPA practice. The TCPA itself does not expressly address revocation — § 227(b) requires "prior express consent" (or "prior express written consent" for marketing under 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(2)–(3)), but is silent on whether and how consent can be withdrawn. The gap has been filled by the FCC and the federal courts, most recently by the FCC's 2024 Revocation Order, which substantially overhauled the framework and codified rules into 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(10).  
  
Two questions dominate revocation litigation:  
  
1. **Can consent be revoked at all?** (Yes, with the narrow contractual exception in the Second Circuit.)  
2. **What counts as a valid revocation?** (Any reasonable method, plus the new presumptive-keyword and presumed-revocation rules in the 2024 Order.)  
  
A third recurring question — **scope** (does revocation as to one program revoke consent for all communications from the seller?) — is now the subject of the FCC's most-litigated and most-delayed rule, with a compliance deadline now extended to January 31, 2027.

## 2. Pre-2024 framework  
  
### 2.1 The 2015 FCC Omnibus Order  
  
In its 2015 TCPA Omnibus Order, the FCC declared that a called party "may revoke consent at any time and through any reasonable means" — either orally or in writing — that clearly expresses a desire not to receive further calls, and that callers "may not designate an exclusive means" of revocation. 30 FCC Rcd. 7961, ¶¶ 55–70 (2015). The D.C. Circuit upheld this portion of the order in *ACA International v. FCC*, 885 F.3d 687, 709–10 (D.C. Cir. 2018).  
  
### 2.2 Majority rule: any reasonable means  
  
Most circuits applied the 2015 standard and pre-existing common-law principles to allow revocation by any reasonable means.  
  
- ***Osorio v. State Farm Bank, F.S.B.***, 746 F.3d 1242 (11th Cir. 2014) — relying on common law of consent, held that "absent any contractual restriction to the contrary," a consumer can orally revoke prior express consent. The court rejected State Farm's argument that revocation must be in writing.  
- ***Gager v. Dell Financial Services***, 727 F.3d 265 (3d Cir. 2013) — earlier circuit decision recognizing oral revocation; held that the TCPA permits revocation, that revocation need not be in writing, and rejected the argument that consent provided on a credit application became irrevocable.  
- ***Schweitzer v. Comenity Bank***, 866 F.3d 1273 (11th Cir. 2017) — extended *Osorio* to recognize **partial** revocation (consumer told bank she could only take calls on certain days/times). Recognized "reasonableness" of partial revocations.  
  
District court decisions consistent with the majority rule include ***Saunders v. Dyck O'Neal, Inc.***, 319 F. Supp. 3d 907 (W.D. Mich. 2018) (telling a collector during a call "stop calling my phone" sufficed as oral revocation; later calls violated § 227(b)).  
  
The Ninth Circuit applied the same general framework but typically litigated through summary-judgment-stage fact questions: ***Pollard v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.***, 720 F. App'x 851 (9th Cir. 2018) (genuine factual dispute over whether plaintiff revoked when she instructed bank not to call her cell phone) [VERIFY citation].  
  
### 2.3 *Reyes v. Lincoln Automotive Financial Services* — the outlier  
  
The Second Circuit broke from the majority rule in ***Reyes v. Lincoln Auto. Fin. Servs.***, 861 F.3d 51 (2d Cir. 2017). When Reyes leased a Lincoln, the lease contract contained a clause providing his consent to receive autodialed calls. After he defaulted and tried to revoke consent, Lincoln continued to call. The court held that **consent given as bargained-for consideration in a binding contract cannot be unilaterally revoked**.  
  
Key reasoning:  
  
- "Consent" under the TCPA carries its common-law meaning.  
- At common law, consent freely given (gratuitous) is revocable, but consent that is part of the consideration in a bilateral contract is not unilaterally revocable.  
- A consumer who wants to escape such consent must either renegotiate the contract or accept that consent travels with the bargain.  
  
The court rejected the FCC's contrary position in the 2015 Omnibus Order, distinguishing it as not directly addressing contract-based consent.  
  
*Reyes* remains good law in the Second Circuit. Other circuits and district courts have largely declined to follow it outside the Second Circuit, and some district courts limit it to contracts where consent was clearly bargained-for consideration (not buried boilerplate). See, e.g., *Few v. Receivables Performance Mgmt.*, 2018 WL 5077013 (N.D. Ala. 2018) (declining to extend *Reyes*).  
  
The 2024 FCC Revocation Order does **not** expressly overrule *Reyes* — the FCC declined to reach the contractual-consent question — leaving the circuit split intact for binding-contract consent.

## 3. The 2024 FCC Revocation Order  
  
### 3.1 Background and adoption  
  
On February 15, 2024, the FCC adopted a Report and Order entitled "Strengthening the Ability of Consumers to Stop Unwanted Robocalls" (released February 16, 2024). FCC 24-24. Published 89 Fed. Reg. 15756 (Mar. 5, 2024). The Order codified its revocation rules into 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(10) and made several significant changes.  
  
### 3.2 Core principles codified  
  
The Order codifies that:  
  
1. **A consumer may revoke prior express consent through any reasonable means** that clearly expresses a desire not to receive further calls or text messages.  
2. **A caller may not designate an exclusive means by which a consumer must revoke consent.** A consumer's revocation is effective even if the caller required revocation only by a specific website, phone number, or address.  
3. **Callers must honor revocation requests within a reasonable time**, not to exceed 10 business days after receipt of the revocation request.  
  
### 3.3 Presumed-revocation keywords  
  
The Order creates a presumption that the following words/phrases, when sent in reply to a text message, constitute revocation:  
  
- "stop"  
- "quit"  
- "end"  
- "revoke"  
- "opt out"  
- "cancel"  
- "unsubscribe"  
  
Any reply that uses one of these standardized keywords is **presumed** to be revocation. A caller may rebut the presumption only by showing that the totality of facts demonstrates the consumer did not intend to revoke. The presumption applies regardless of whether the original text included instructions about which keyword to use.  
  
Other reasonable formulations (e.g., "Please don't text me again," "Take me off your list," voicemails saying "stop calling") remain effective revocation under the "any reasonable means" standard, but lack the automatic presumption.  
  
### 3.4 One-time confirmation message exception  
  
The Order recognizes that after a consumer revokes consent, the caller may send **one final, one-time text message confirming receipt of the opt-out request**, provided the confirmation does not include marketing content and is sent within five minutes of the revocation. This confirmation message is not itself a violation.  
  
### 3.5 Scope: "revoke one = revoke all" rule (delayed)  
  
The most controversial provision: revocation of consent for one type of robocall/robotext from a particular caller applies to **all** robocalls/robotexts from that caller on unrelated matters, unless the caller obtains new consent or the call falls within an exemption.  
  
This provision was originally to take effect April 11, 2025. The FCC has now extended its effective date twice:  
  
- **First extension:** DA 25-312 (Apr. 7, 2025), delaying to April 11, 2026.  
- **Second extension:** DA 26-12 (Jan. 6, 2026), further delaying to **January 31, 2027**.  
  
The delays apply only to the "revoke-all" portion. The remainder of the revocation rule (keyword presumption, 10-business-day deadline, no-exclusive-means rule) took effect April 11, 2025.

## 4. The "reasonable method" standard post-2024  
  
The 2024 Order preserves and codifies the "any reasonable means" standard while supplementing it with the keyword presumption. Examples that courts and the FCC have recognized as reasonable methods of revocation:  
  
- Replying "STOP" or any presumptive keyword to a text message.  
- Saying "stop calling me" or "take me off your list" during a live call. The caller's agent is on notice; the caller is charged with relaying that revocation to the entire enterprise.  
- Sending a written letter to the caller's customer-service or registered-agent address.  
- Clicking an opt-out link in an email or webform.  
- Telephoning the company and asking to be removed.  
- Voicemail messages clearly requesting cessation of calls.  
- Communications through the caller's app, customer portal, or chat interface.  
- Limited recognition: social-media messages (e.g., tweet at the company) — courts split; not strongly endorsed by the FCC.  
  
A caller's attempt to **restrict** revocation to a single method (e.g., "you may only opt out at www.example.com/optout") is invalid under both the 2015 Order and 2024 codified rule.

## 5. Documentation and operational requirements  
  
The FCC's rules and downstream enforcement actions require callers to:  
  
1. **Document revocations.** Record the date, time, method, and content of the revocation request. This is also a 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(d) internal-DNC requirement.  
2. **Stop calls within 10 business days** of receipt of revocation (codified at 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(10)).  
3. **Train staff** to recognize and process revocations made through any channel, including live agents on inbound or outbound calls.  
4. **Maintain an internal Do-Not-Call list** with written policies and training records (§ 64.1200(d)) — see TCPA Topic 09.  
  
For debt collectors and creditors, these requirements stack on top of the FDCPA cease-communication right under 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c). A § 1692c(c) cease-communication letter is also a TCPA revocation, but TCPA revocation extends beyond debt collection to all consumer-facing calls.

## 6. Common litigation patterns  
  
### 6.1 Failure to honor "STOP" reply  
  
The most common modern claim: consumer texts "STOP" (or other keyword), and the sender's platform fails to process the keyword (because it was sent as a reply to a different short code, used in conjunction with other text, or filtered as spam). Post-2024, the keyword presumption shifts the burden of proof, making summary judgment for plaintiffs more accessible.  
  
### 6.2 Calls continuing after written revocation  
  
Plaintiffs send a letter or email; the caller's system continues calls because revocation was logged in one queue (e.g., collections) but not propagated to others (e.g., marketing). Pre-2027, this gives rise to litigation about scope; post-2027 (assuming the rule takes effect), revocation in one program automatically revokes all.  
  
### 6.3 Oral revocation during a call  
  
Plaintiff tells the caller's agent "stop calling" on a recorded line; calls continue. Where the recording is preserved, summary judgment for plaintiff is routine. Discovery should target call recordings, agent notes, and the caller's policies for processing oral revocation. See *Saunders v. Dyck O'Neal*, 319 F. Supp. 3d 907.  
  
### 6.4 *Reyes*-based defenses (Second Circuit and persuasively elsewhere)  
  
Where consent was provided in a bargained-for contract clause, defendants invoke *Reyes*. Plaintiff counter-arguments include:  
  
- Consent was not in fact bargained-for consideration (it was boilerplate in adhesion contract).  
- Calls fell outside the scope of the contract.  
- Calls continued after termination of the contract (post-payoff, post-cancellation).  
- New consent was required for marketing under § 64.1200(a)(2) and was not validly obtained.  
  
### 6.5 Scope-of-revocation disputes (pre-Jan. 31, 2027)  
  
Until the "revoke-all" provision takes effect, courts apply pre-2024 case law on scope. The common-sense rule applied by most courts: revocation as to one specific type of call (e.g., collections) does not necessarily revoke consent for unrelated communications (e.g., account alerts), but a revocation phrased broadly ("don't call me ever") is enterprise-wide. See *Schweitzer*, 866 F.3d at 1276–77 (partial revocation valid).

## 7. Litigation tips  
  
- **Preserve the revocation evidence.** Send revocation in writing, via certified mail and email, and follow up. If oral, identify the agent and date/time so it can be matched to call recordings in discovery.  
- **Specify the scope.** Until 2027 in revoke-all jurisdictions, "stop calling about this debt and any other matter" forecloses defenses that revocation was program-specific.  
- **In texts, use a presumptive keyword.** "STOP" carries the strongest presumption post-2024.  
- **Track calls after revocation.** Each subsequent call is a separate § 227(b) violation at $500 statutory damages (or $1,500 if willful).  
- **Combine TCPA with FDCPA/state UDAP claims** where applicable; the same conduct often violates multiple statutes.
