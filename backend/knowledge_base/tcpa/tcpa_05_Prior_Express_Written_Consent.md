# Prior Express Written Consent (PEWC) Under the TCPA  
  
**Topic:** The heightened "prior express written consent" standard for marketing/telephone-solicitation calls  
**Primary statute:** 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A), (B)  
**Implementing regulation:** 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(2), (a)(3), (f)(9)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
---  
  
## 1. The PEWC requirement in context  
  
"Prior express written consent" (PEWC) is the heightened consent standard required for marketing communications under the TCPA. It applies to:  
  
- Autodialed or prerecorded *telemarketing* calls to cell phones — 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(2);  
- Prerecorded *telemarketing* calls to residential lines — § 64.1200(a)(3); and  
- (As applied by the 2024 FCC declaratory ruling) AI-voice marketing calls — see § 5 below.  
  
For non-marketing calls to cell phones, the lower "prior express consent" standard (§ 227(b)(1)(A)(iii); 2008 FCC Ruling) applies — see `tcpa_04_Prior_Express_Consent.md`. PEWC is more demanding: it requires a written agreement, a clear and conspicuous disclosure, and a signature.  
  
---  
  
## 2. The PEWC definition — 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(f)(9)  
  
The FCC's definition is the core of PEWC compliance:  
  
> "The term *prior express written consent* means an agreement, in writing, bearing the signature of the person called that clearly and conspicuously authorizes the seller to deliver or cause to be delivered to the person called advertisements or telemarketing messages using an automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice, and the telephone number to which the signatory authorizes such advertisements or telemarketing messages to be delivered." — 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(f)(9).  
  
The agreement must include "a clear and conspicuous disclosure informing the person signing" that:  
  
- **(A)** By executing the agreement, the person authorizes the seller to deliver, or cause to be delivered, advertisements or telemarketing messages "using an automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice"; and  
- **(B)** The person is not required to sign the agreement (directly or indirectly), or agree to enter into such an agreement as a condition of purchasing any property, goods, or services.  
  
These two disclosures are sometimes called the "ATDS authorization" and the "no-condition-of-purchase" disclosures. Both are mandatory.  
  
### 2.1 Required elements — checklist  
  
1. **In writing.** Includes electronic writings (see § 3 below).  
2. **Signed by the person called.** Electronic signatures permitted.  
3. **Clearly and conspicuously discloses** authorization to be contacted using an ATDS or artificial/prerecorded voice.  
4. **Specifically identifies** the telephone number(s) at which the person may be contacted.  
5. **Discloses** that signature is not a condition of purchase.  
6. **Identifies the seller** — the entity authorized to deliver the messages.  
  
### 2.2 "Clear and conspicuous"  
  
The FCC has defined "clear and conspicuous" as "a notice that would be apparent to the reasonable consumer, separate and distinguishable from the advertising copy or other disclosures." 2012 TCPA Order, 27 FCC Rcd 1830, ¶ 33 n.113.  
  
Practical implications:  
- Burying the PEWC language in dense terms of service is non-compliant.  
- Pre-checked consent boxes are disfavored and risky.  
- The consent language must be visually distinct (separate paragraph, contrasting background, distinct font weight) from surrounding marketing copy.

## 3. E-SIGN Act and electronic consents  
  
The FCC has confirmed that PEWC may be obtained electronically under the federal E-SIGN Act, 15 U.S.C. § 7001 et seq. 2012 TCPA Order ¶ 34. Acceptable electronic signature methods include:  
  
- A web form with an "I agree" or "Submit" button immediately following the disclosure.  
- A SMS double-opt-in confirmation, where the consumer texts a keyword in response to a disclosure and then confirms by replying YES.  
- A typed name in a signature field, with appropriate metadata captured.  
- A click-through agreement in a mobile app, where the disclosure is clearly visible above the agreement button.  
  
The defendant must be able to produce the underlying record. E-SIGN compliance requires:  
  
- Disclosure to the consumer of the right to receive the agreement in paper form;  
- Demonstration that the consumer can access the electronic record;  
- Retention of the electronic record in a form that can be accurately reproduced.

## 4. Specificity — identifying recipient and purpose  
  
PEWC must identify the *seller* and the type of communications authorized. Generic consent to "third-party marketing partners" or "affiliated businesses" historically posed two problems:  
  
1. **Identity.** The consumer must know which entity is authorized to call.  
2. **Subject matter.** The consent must reasonably encompass the type of marketing communication later sent.  
  
The Eleventh Circuit's 2025 decision in *Insurance Marketing Coalition Ltd. v. FCC*, 127 F.4th 303 (11th Cir. 2025), addressed these issues in the context of the FCC's one-to-one consent rule (see § 6 below).  
  
---  
  
## 5. The 2012 FCC TCPA Order — PEWC takes effect  
  
The PEWC framework was established by the FCC's 2012 TCPA Order, *In re Rules and Regulations Implementing the Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991*, 27 FCC Rcd 1830 (Feb. 15, 2012). The order:  
  
1. Required PEWC for all autodialed or prerecorded telemarketing calls to wireless numbers (eliminating reliance on the lower "prior express consent" standard for marketing);  
2. Required PEWC for prerecorded telemarketing calls to residential numbers (eliminating the prior EBR-based exemption);  
3. Required an automated, interactive opt-out mechanism within each prerecorded telemarketing call;  
4. Adopted call-abandonment limits for prerecorded calls.  
  
The PEWC rules took effect October 16, 2013. Communications made before that date under the prior "prior express consent" standard were grandfathered for the original transactional purpose, but companies that wished to continue telemarketing had to obtain new PEWC.

## 6. The 2023 "one-to-one consent" rule and the *Insurance Marketing Coalition* decision  
  
### 6.1 The 2023 FCC rule  
  
In December 2023, the FCC adopted a rule that purported to further interpret "prior express consent" to add two new restrictions:  
  
- **One-to-one:** Consent must be given to a single seller at a time; bundled consents covering multiple unaffiliated sellers were invalid; and  
- **Logically and topically associated:** The subject matter of the marketing call had to be logically and topically associated with the interaction that prompted the consent.  
  
The rule's stated effective date was January 27, 2025 (originally scheduled for earlier implementation; delayed by the FCC). The rule targeted the lead-generation industry, where a single consumer form on a comparison-shopping website typically generated consents to dozens of "marketing partners."  
  
### 6.2 *Insurance Marketing Coalition Ltd. v. FCC*, 127 F.4th 303 (11th Cir. Jan. 24, 2025)  
  
On January 24, 2025—just days before the rule's effective date—the Eleventh Circuit vacated the one-to-one consent rule and the logically-and-topically-related requirement. The court held that the FCC's restrictions were "inconsistent with the ordinary statutory meaning of" "prior express consent," which under common-law principles means "a willingness for certain conduct to occur, clearly and unmistakably stated before the conduct."  
  
The court granted IMC's petition for review, vacated Part III.D of the FCC's 2023 Order, and remanded. The decision is published and binding within the Eleventh Circuit; because it sets aside the rule on Hobbs Act review, the rule does not take effect anywhere.  
  
### 6.3 Status as of May 2026  
  
The FCC's one-to-one consent rule is vacated. PEWC continues to be governed by 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(f)(9) without the one-to-one and logical-relatedness gloss. However:  
  
- Underlying TCPA principles still require that PEWC clearly identify the seller and unambiguously authorize the type of communication later made.  
- Consent obtained for "marketing partners" without identifying them, or for unrelated subject matter, may still fail the existing § 64.1200(f)(9) requirements as a matter of contract and statutory interpretation.  
- Industry practice has not fully reverted to pre-2023 norms; many lead-generation platforms have adopted enhanced consent disclosures voluntarily.  
  
[VERIFY — the regulatory landscape on this point is in flux; counsel should check the FCC's most recent guidance and any post-*IMC* rulemaking before relying on bundled-seller consents.]

## 7. STIR/SHAKEN and TCR — related compliance context  
  
PEWC compliance does not exist in isolation. Marketing callers must also comply with:  
  
### 7.1 STIR/SHAKEN  
  
The STIR/SHAKEN caller-ID authentication framework, mandated by the TRACED Act (Pub. L. No. 116-105) and implemented in 47 C.F.R. § 64.6300 et seq., requires originating voice service providers to authenticate caller-ID information. Calls that cannot be authenticated may be blocked or labeled as "Likely Spam" by terminating carriers. PEWC alone does not guarantee delivery; the originating provider must also be STIR/SHAKEN-compliant.  
  
### 7.2 Trusted Communications Registry / Campaign Registry  
  
For text-message marketing, the wireless carriers' Campaign Registry (TCR) imposes additional registration and disclosure requirements. Brands and campaigns must be registered, and consent records may be requested by carriers as a condition of message throughput. While the TCR is not a TCPA requirement, its consent documentation requirements substantially overlap with PEWC.

## 8. Lead-generation industry implications  
  
The lead-generation industry — where consumer information is collected on one site (comparison-shopping pages, "free quote" pages) and resold to multiple sellers — has been the principal target of PEWC enforcement.  
  
### 8.1 Typical structure  
  
- Consumer enters information on a comparison page (insurance, mortgage, solar, education, etc.).  
- The page displays a list of "marketing partners" (often dozens, with a hyperlinked list).  
- Consumer submits the form. The lead is sold to one or more "purchaser" companies, who then call.  
  
### 8.2 Vulnerabilities  
  
- **Identifiability of the seller.** If the consumer cannot reasonably identify which company will call, the consent may fail § 64.1200(f)(9)'s "seller" requirement.  
- **Scope of authorized communications.** Where the consumer signed up for a "mortgage quote" but the marketing call is about life insurance, scope may be exceeded.  
- **Updating the partner list.** When the partner list changes after consent, the new partners are typically not covered.  
- **Proof of presentation.** The lead-generation vendor must be able to produce the exact disclosure shown to the consumer on the consent date, in its then-current configuration.  
  
### 8.3 Post-*IMC* practice  
  
After the Eleventh Circuit's vacatur of the one-to-one rule, lead-generation operators retain greater flexibility, but the underlying § 64.1200(f)(9) requirements still demand specific identification and clear authorization. Best practices include:  
  
- Display a *visible* (not hyperlinked) seller list with a reasonable maximum number;  
- Use a dual-button structure (consent vs. no consent), not pre-checked boxes;  
- Capture and retain the exact disclosure HTML, IP address, timestamp, and user agent.

## 9. Sample compliant PEWC language  
  
For a mortgage refinance lead-generation page:  
  
> **Consent to Be Contacted (Optional)**  
> By checking the box below and clicking "Submit," you authorize **Acme Mortgage Co.** and its affiliates and service providers to contact you at the telephone number you provided, including via prerecorded voice messages and automatic telephone dialing systems, for marketing purposes related to mortgage products and services. Your consent is **not** a condition of purchase. You may revoke your consent at any time by [describe revocation method]. Standard message and data rates may apply.  
> ☐ Yes, I agree.  
  
Key features:  
- Clear identification of the seller ("Acme Mortgage Co.");  
- Specific reference to "prerecorded voice messages and automatic telephone dialing systems";  
- "Not a condition of purchase" disclosure;  
- Unchecked opt-in checkbox separate from the form submission button (some courts have held that this dual-affirmation structure is best practice);  
- Reference to the telephone number provided;  
- Description of revocation method.  
  
For marketing texts, the disclosure should also reference the program name, message frequency, and HELP/STOP keywords (as required by the CTIA messaging principles and TCR).

## 10. Burden of proof and common deficiencies  
  
### 10.1 Defendant bears the burden  
  
As with general prior express consent, the defendant bears the burden of proving PEWC. *Van Patten v. Vertical Fitness Group, LLC*, 847 F.3d 1037, 1044 (9th Cir. 2017). Where PEWC is required (marketing context), the defendant must produce:  
  
- The signed agreement;  
- The exact disclosure language as displayed to the consumer;  
- Evidence of the consumer's affirmative signature/click;  
- Metadata supporting authenticity (IP, timestamp, device).  
  
### 10.2 Common deficiencies that produce liability  
  
1. **Marketing call placed under non-marketing "prior express consent."** Number provided for account servicing used for marketing without separate PEWC.  
2. **Buried disclosure.** Consent language hidden in long terms of service.  
3. **Pre-checked box.** Many courts treat pre-checked consent as inadequate "express" consent.  
4. **No "ATDS or prerecorded" disclosure.** Generic consent to "be contacted" without referencing ATDS or prerecorded voice.  
5. **No "not a condition" disclosure.** Consent obtained as part of a forced flow.  
6. **Vendor failure.** Lead-gen vendor cannot produce the original consent record.  
7. **Out-of-scope call.** Subject matter of the call does not reasonably correspond to the consented purpose.  
8. **Stale consent.** Consent obtained years before the call, after the seller's identity or product line has materially changed.  
9. **Revoked consent honored too slowly.** Consent revoked but call placed because revocation was not propagated in time.  
10. **Affiliate or third-party seller not properly identified.** Consent named one entity; call came from an affiliate not within the consenting language.  
  
Each deficiency exposes the caller to statutory damages of $500 per call (or $1,500 per willful or knowing violation) under § 227(b)(3).

## 11. Practical compliance recommendations  
  
1. Treat the PEWC capture page as a regulated form. Subject every change to compliance review.  
2. Maintain a consent vault with full audit trails (snapshot of disclosure HTML, IP, timestamp, device, signature method).  
3. Map each consent to specific authorized sellers and product categories.  
4. Implement immediate revocation propagation (see `tcpa_06_Revocation_of_Consent.md`).  
5. For lead generation, contractually require vendors to preserve consent records for at least 5 years and to provide them on demand within a defined SLA.  
6. Run regular internal audits: pull a random sample of marketing calls and verify the underlying PEWC record.  
7. Track FCC and circuit developments — the PEWC regime has been unstable since 2023.
