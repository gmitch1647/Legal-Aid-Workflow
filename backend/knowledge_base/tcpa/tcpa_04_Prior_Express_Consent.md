# Prior Express Consent Under the TCPA  
  
**Topic:** "Prior express consent" for non-marketing calls to cell phones under § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii)  
**Primary statute:** 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii)  
**Implementing regulation:** 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(1), (l) (reassigned numbers database safe harbor)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
---  
  
## 1. The statutory framework  
  
47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A) prohibits any person from making a "call" to a cell phone using an ATDS or an artificial or prerecorded voice "other than a call made ... with the *prior express consent* of the called party." The "prior express consent" defense is the primary defense available for non-marketing calls placed to cell phones. (For marketing calls and prerecorded marketing calls to residential numbers, the higher "prior express *written* consent" standard applies — see `tcpa_05_Prior_Express_Written_Consent.md`.)  
  
Two foundational points govern every consent analysis:  
  
1. **Express, not implied.** Implied consent is generally insufficient. The consent must be affirmatively communicated and unambiguous.  
2. **Defendant bears the burden.** The defendant must prove prior express consent. *In re Rules and Regulations Implementing the Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991*, 23 FCC Rcd 559, ¶ 10 (2008) ("2008 FCC Declaratory Ruling") ("Should a question arise as to whether express consent was provided, the burden will be on the creditor to show it obtained the necessary prior express consent.").  
  
---  
  
## 2. What constitutes "prior express consent" for non-marketing calls  
  
### 2.1 The 2008 FCC Declaratory Ruling  
  
The seminal FCC interpretation is the 2008 ACA International Declaratory Ruling, 23 FCC Rcd 559. The FCC held that:  
  
> "[T]he provision of a cell phone number to a creditor, e.g., as part of a credit application, reasonably evidences prior express consent by the cell phone subscriber to be contacted at that number regarding the debt." — 23 FCC Rcd at 564 ¶ 9.  
  
The ruling addressed debt-collection calls, but its rationale extends to any transactional, non-marketing call: by voluntarily providing one's cell number in connection with a transaction, the consumer has expressly consented to autodialed and prerecorded calls about that transaction.  
  
### 2.2 Scope: consent is transactional, not topical  
  
The 2008 ruling and subsequent FCC guidance confirm that consent obtained when a number is provided is *limited in scope* to communications about the transaction in connection with which the number was provided. A consumer who provides her cell number to her bank for account-servicing purposes has not consented to receive marketing calls from the bank or its affiliates.  
  
This scope limitation has been reinforced repeatedly:  
  
- *Van Patten v. Vertical Fitness Group, LLC*, 847 F.3d 1037, 1045–46 (9th Cir. 2017): consent given in one context does not extend to materially different communications. The Ninth Circuit held that providing a cell number when joining a gym was consent to gym-related communications; whether a "We Want You Back" reactivation text three years after cancellation exceeded that consent was a close question (the court ultimately held it did not, on the facts).  
- *Reardon v. Uber Technologies, Inc.*, 115 F. Supp. 3d 1090 (N.D. Cal. 2015): driver-applicant consent to receive recruiting texts did not cover later marketing texts.  
  
### 2.3 Common transactional contexts in which "consent by provision" is recognized  
  
- **Credit applications and account opening.** Number provided on a credit-card, loan, or deposit application is consent to servicing calls about the account.  
- **Healthcare intake.** Number provided in healthcare intake forms is consent to appointment reminders and similar healthcare-treatment communications. See 2015 FCC Declaratory Ruling on HIPAA-compliant communications, 30 FCC Rcd 7961 ¶¶ 144–47.  
- **Utility, telecom, and insurance accounts.** Number provided to obtain service is consent to service-related calls (outage notifications, billing).  
- **Employment / hiring.** Number provided in a job application is consent to call about that application.  
- **E-commerce.** Number provided to receive shipping notifications is consent to shipping and order-status notifications.  
  
In each case, the scope is limited to the transactional purpose.

## 3. Marketing versus non-marketing — the consent dichotomy  
  
The TCPA and FCC rules draw a sharp line between two consent levels:  
  
| Call type | Consent level required | Source |  
| --- | --- | --- |  
| Non-marketing call to cell phone via ATDS or prerecorded voice | "Prior express consent" | § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii); 2008 FCC Ruling |  
| Telemarketing / advertising call to cell phone via ATDS or prerecorded voice | "Prior express *written* consent" | 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(2)–(3); 2012 FCC Order |  
| Prerecorded telemarketing call to residential line | "Prior express written consent" | 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(3) |  
| Non-marketing prerecorded call to residential line | Generally permitted under EBR or specified exemptions | § 64.1200(a)(3)(ii)–(v) |  
  
The 2012 FCC TCPA Order (FCC 12-21) eliminated the prior EBR-based exemption for prerecorded telemarketing calls to residential lines and imposed the PEWC requirement for all autodialed or prerecorded marketing calls to cell phones. The order took effect October 16, 2013.  
  
This dichotomy is the most common source of inadvertent TCPA liability. A company that lawfully obtained "consent by provision" of a cell number for account servicing cannot use that consent for marketing calls without also obtaining separate PEWC.

## 4. Implied consent — generally not recognized  
  
The FCC and courts have been consistent: TCPA consent must be *express*, not implied. Mere existence of a customer relationship does not constitute consent to autodialed or prerecorded calls.  
  
Limited exceptions:  
  
- **Emergency purposes.** § 227(b)(1)(A) excepts calls "made for emergency purposes." 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(f)(4) defines this narrowly: "calls made necessary in any situation affecting the health and safety of consumers." The FCC has interpreted this term narrowly. See *Roberts v. PayPal, Inc.*, 621 F. App'x 478 (9th Cir. 2015) (denying emergency exception for service alerts).  
- **Specified exemptions in § 64.1200(a)(9).** The FCC has carved out narrow exemptions for free-to-end-user financial-account, healthcare-treatment, package-delivery, and utility-outage calls, each subject to volume and opt-out limits.  
  
Importantly, *implied consent from a prior relationship* does not satisfy the TCPA. A bank that has a 20-year deposit relationship with a consumer but has never obtained the consumer's cell number must still obtain express consent before placing autodialed or prerecorded calls to that number.

## 5. The recycled-number / reassigned-number problem  
  
When a cell number is reassigned from a consenting consumer to a new, non-consenting subscriber, the caller faces a difficult problem: the original consent is no longer effective, and the caller has no consent from the new subscriber.  
  
### 5.1 "Called party" means current subscriber  
  
The leading authority is the Seventh Circuit's decision in *Soppet v. Enhanced Recovery Co.*, 679 F.3d 637 (7th Cir. 2012) (Easterbrook, J.). The court held that "called party" in § 227(b)(1)(A) means the current subscriber, not the intended recipient. The Eleventh Circuit agreed in *Osorio v. State Farm Bank, F.S.B.*, 746 F.3d 1242 (11th Cir. 2014).  
  
### 5.2 The 2015 FCC "one-call" safe harbor  
  
In its 2015 Omnibus Order, the FCC adopted a one-call safe harbor: a caller would not be liable for the first call after reassignment, on a "reasonable reliance" theory. The D.C. Circuit vacated this approach in *ACA International v. FCC*, 885 F.3d 687 (D.C. Cir. 2018), as arbitrary.  
  
### 5.3 The Reassigned Numbers Database — 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(l)  
  
After *ACA International*, the FCC adopted the Reassigned Numbers Database (RND) framework in 2018 (33 FCC Rcd 12024). The database, operated by Somos under FCC oversight, became operational for paid subscribers on November 1, 2021.  
  
Under § 64.1200(l):  
  
- Callers can query the RND with a phone number and a "consent date" (the date on which they obtained consent or the date of last contact).  
- If the RND returns "No" (i.e., the number has not been reassigned since the consent date), the caller is eligible for a **safe harbor** from TCPA liability for inadvertent calls to a reassigned number.  
- If the RND returns "Yes" or "No Data," there is no safe harbor.  
  
The safe harbor applies only to liability for the "called party" element—it does not cure other defects (e.g., absence of consent in the first place, ATDS issues, DNC violations).  
  
### 5.4 Practical effect  
  
- Counsel for callers: require RND queries before any autodialed or prerecorded call campaign. Document each query.  
- Counsel for consumers: at the pleading stage, allege facts establishing that the plaintiff is the current subscriber and that the prior subscriber (if any) was not the source of consent. In discovery, demand the defendant's RND query records.

## 6. Family-member and third-party consent — agency issues  
  
The 2008 Declaratory Ruling addressed only consent given by the cell-phone subscriber. When a third party (spouse, parent, employer) provides the number, the question becomes whether that third party had actual or apparent authority to consent on the subscriber's behalf.  
  
- *Gager v. Dell Financial Services, LLC*, 727 F.3d 265 (3d Cir. 2013): consent is governed by common-law agency principles.  
- *Osorio*, 746 F.3d at 1252–55 (11th Cir. 2014): consent by a co-resident who shared the cell phone with the subscriber raised disputed fact issues about whether the co-resident had authority to provide the number.  
  
Practical rule: a third party's consent binds the subscriber only if that party had actual, apparent, or ratified authority. The mere fact that the number was on a joint account or that a spouse provided it is not sufficient.

## 7. Documentation and proof of consent  
  
Because the defendant bears the burden of proof, documentation is essential.  
  
### 7.1 What defendants should maintain  
  
- The form or screen capture on which the consumer provided the number.  
- The date of provision.  
- The transactional context (account application, intake form, e-commerce checkout, etc.).  
- The IP address or device identifier (for electronic consents).  
- For oral consent: a recording or contemporaneous note.  
- Any post-provision communications confirming the number.  
  
### 7.2 Common evidentiary problems  
  
- **Lost call recordings.** Many TCPA cases turn on whether the defendant can produce the original consent document. Standard records-retention periods (often 18–36 months) may not cover the four-year TCPA statute-of-limitations period (28 U.S.C. § 1658).  
- **Vendor consents.** Where consent was obtained through a lead-generation vendor, the defendant must be able to produce vendor records. Lack of vendor cooperation is fatal.  
- **Updated terms / consent clauses.** Where consent language was later updated, the defendant must show the version in effect when the consumer provided the number.  
  
### 7.3 Sample compliant consent language (non-marketing, account-servicing)  
  
> "By providing your phone number, you authorize [Company] and its servicers to contact you at that number, including by autodialed and prerecorded calls or text messages, for purposes related to your account. Standard message and data rates may apply. Your consent is not a condition of purchase."  
  
For marketing calls, this language is insufficient — separate PEWC language is required (see `tcpa_05_Prior_Express_Written_Consent.md`).

## 8. Common pleading and litigation issues  
  
### 8.1 Plaintiff-side  
  
- Allege that the plaintiff never provided the cell number to the defendant.  
- Allege the absence of any transactional relationship that would support consent by provision.  
- Where the plaintiff did provide the number, allege facts establishing that the call exceeded the scope of consent (e.g., marketing call after consent only for servicing).  
- Allege facts about reassignment if the plaintiff is the new subscriber on a recycled number.  
  
### 8.2 Defendant-side  
  
- Plead consent as an affirmative defense.  
- Produce contemporaneous consent records early.  
- Where consent was provided through a vendor, secure vendor cooperation immediately.  
- For reassigned-number defense, produce RND query logs.  
- For scope arguments, document the transactional context in which the number was provided.  
  
### 8.3 Class certification  
  
Consent issues are the principal battleground in TCPA class certification. Defendants routinely argue that individualized consent inquiries defeat predominance under Rule 23(b)(3). Plaintiffs counter that where the defendant's consent practice was uniform across the class (e.g., the same web form or call script), predominance is satisfied.  
  
The Ninth Circuit in *True Health Chiropractic, Inc. v. McKesson Corp.*, 896 F.3d 923 (9th Cir. 2018), held that consent issues can defeat predominance where the defendant has produced evidence of individualized consent for substantial portions of the class. By contrast, where the defendant's evidence is sparse or formulaic, predominance is often satisfied.

## 9. Practical compliance checklist  
  
1. Maintain a consent management system that records source, date, channel, and version of consent language.  
2. Map consent scope to call type (servicing vs. marketing).  
3. Query the RND before each autodialed or prerecorded campaign.  
4. Update consent language whenever the FCC issues new guidance.  
5. Train CSRs to confirm consent and to honor revocation immediately (see `tcpa_06_Revocation_of_Consent.md`).  
6. Retain consent documentation for at least 5 years (one year beyond the 4-year statute of limitations).  
7. For lead-generation campaigns, require vendor contractual representations that PEWC was obtained, and require vendors to preserve consent records.
