## How to use this file  
  
Cases are organized by doctrinal topic, not by circuit, because TCPA litigation is dominated by a small number of issues (ATDS, consent, revocation, called party, faxes, standing, vicarious liability, and class certification). Each entry contains: (1) the verified citation; (2) the issuing court and year; (3) the operative facts; (4) the holding; and (5) the doctrinal significance. Citations are verified against Justia, Cornell LII, FindLaw, CourtListener, or the issuing court's published opinions. Where a citation could not be confirmed against a primary public-domain source, the entry carries a `[VERIFY CITATION]` flag.

## A. ATDS definition before *Facebook v. Duguid* (historical context)  
  
The pre-*Duguid* ATDS jurisprudence remains relevant for three reasons: (i) understanding agency rulemaking history (the FCC 2003, 2008, and 2015 Orders); (ii) interpreting calls and texts placed before April 1, 2021 (the *Duguid* decision date); and (iii) framing the rejected "broad-capacity" reading that *Duguid* foreclosed.  
  
### *ACA International v. FCC*, 885 F.3d 687 (D.C. Cir. 2018)  
**Court:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit  
**Year:** 2018  
**Facts:** Industry petitioners challenged the FCC's 2015 Omnibus Declaratory Ruling and Order, which had interpreted "capacity" in the ATDS definition broadly to encompass devices with the potential capacity to dial randomly generated numbers, and had adopted rules for revocation of consent, reassigned numbers, and time-sensitive healthcare calls.  
**Holding:** The D.C. Circuit set aside the FCC's expansive ATDS interpretation, vacated the agency's reassigned-number "one-call safe harbor" as arbitrary and capricious, but upheld the FCC's revocation-by-any-reasonable-means rule and the healthcare exemption.  
**Significance:** *ACA International* created the doctrinal vacuum that *Duguid* later filled. Its rejection of the "potential capacity" reading foreshadowed the Supreme Court's narrower textual construction.  
  
### *Marks v. Crunch San Diego, LLC*, 904 F.3d 1041 (9th Cir. 2018) (now abrogated by *Duguid*)  
**Court:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit  
**Year:** 2018  
**Facts:** Crunch Fitness used the "Textmunication" platform to send promotional text messages to current and prospective gym members from a stored list. Marks received three texts and sued under § 227(b).  
**Holding:** The Ninth Circuit read the ATDS definition broadly to include any device that has the capacity to store numbers and dial them automatically, even without random or sequential generation.  
**Significance:** *Marks* was the high-water mark for the plaintiffs' bar pre-*Duguid*. It was directly abrogated by *Facebook v. Duguid*, 592 U.S. 395 (2021). Treat *Marks* as overruled for ATDS purposes, but it remains cited for human-intervention principles.  
  
### *Glasser v. Hilton Grand Vacations Co.*, 948 F.3d 1301 (11th Cir. 2020)  
**Court:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2020  
**Facts:** Plaintiffs received calls from Hilton's debt-collection and timeshare-marketing operations placed via a system requiring an employee click to dial each call.  
**Holding:** The Eleventh Circuit held that an ATDS must use a random or sequential number generator and that systems requiring meaningful human intervention to initiate a call are not "automatic." "An employee's choice initiates every call" — that is not automated.  
**Significance:** *Glasser* aligned the Eleventh Circuit with the Third (*Dominguez v. Yahoo*, 894 F.3d 116 (3d Cir. 2018)) and Seventh (*Gadelhak v. AT&T Services, Inc.*, 950 F.3d 458 (7th Cir. 2020) (Barrett, J.)) Circuits and foreshadowed *Duguid*.

## B. ATDS definition after *Facebook v. Duguid*  
  
*Duguid* answered the central question but left several second-order issues: (i) whether the random/sequential generator must generate phone numbers or merely "numbers"; (ii) what footnote 7 of the opinion means; (iii) whether "using" an ATDS requires deployment of the generator function; and (iv) how to plead ATDS use post-*Duguid*.  
  
### *Borden v. eFinancial, LLC*, 53 F.4th 1230 (9th Cir. 2022)  
**Court:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit  
**Year:** 2022  
**Facts:** Borden provided his phone number to an insurance referral website; eFinancial subsequently sent marketing texts. Borden alleged the marketer used a "sequential number generator" to determine the *order* in which numbers from its database would be dialed.  
**Holding:** Affirming dismissal, the Ninth Circuit held that an ATDS must generate and dial random or sequential *telephone numbers*; merely generating sequence numbers to select from a stored list does not qualify. The court explicitly rejected the "footnote 7" reading.  
**Significance:** *Borden* is the leading circuit-level interpretation of *Duguid*'s "number generator" language and the principal authority foreclosing plaintiffs' attempts to revive expansive ATDS theories.  
  
### *Beal v. Outfield Brew House, LLC*, 29 F.4th 391 (8th Cir. 2022)  
**Court:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
**Year:** 2022  
**Facts:** Outfield Brew House used a marketing platform to send promotional texts to customers who had opted in by texting a keyword to a short code.  
**Holding:** The Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the defendant: the system did not "produce" telephone numbers; it merely selected which pre-stored phone number to text. "A 'random or sequential number generator' does the producing, and a generator produces by generating." The system was not an ATDS.  
**Significance:** *Beal* adopted the same number-generation-of-phone-numbers reading later embraced by *Borden*. **Note:** The user's prompt referenced "Beal v. Truist Bank, 89 F.4th 426 (4th Cir. 2024)" — that citation could not be verified against Justia, CourtListener, or the Fourth Circuit's published opinions. `[VERIFY CITATION]`. The verified controlling Eighth Circuit case on the predictive-dialer/list-mode point is *Beal v. Outfield Brew House*.  
  
### *Panzarella v. Navient Solutions, Inc.*, 37 F.4th 867 (3d Cir. 2022)  
**Court:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit  
**Year:** 2022  
**Facts:** Navient's dialer had the technical capacity to generate random ten-digit numbers, but Navient never used that feature for the calls at issue. Instead, the system pulled phone numbers from a stored list of student-loan accounts and dialed those numbers. Plaintiffs argued *capacity* was enough.  
**Holding:** The Third Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Navient on the alternative ground that even if Navient's system had random-generator capacity, Navient did not "use" an ATDS within the meaning of § 227(b)(1)(A) when it placed the calls. "Using" requires deployment of the autodialing functionality, not mere possession of latent capacity.  
**Significance:** *Panzarella* opened a powerful "use vs. capacity" defense, separating dialing systems' technical specifications from how a defendant actually placed the calls at issue. The opinion has been adopted by district courts in multiple circuits.  
  
### *Soliman v. Subway Franchisee Advertising Fund Trust, Ltd.*, 999 F.3d 828 (2d Cir. 2021)  
**Court:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
**Year:** 2021 (with later proceedings reported at 22-1726, 2d Cir. May 10, 2024)  
**Facts:** Soliman texted a Subway short code to receive promotions and later requested they stop. Subway moved to compel arbitration based on terms incorporated by reference on an in-store sign.  
**Holding:** The Second Circuit affirmed denial of arbitration, holding that the in-store advertisement did not provide inquiry notice of the arbitration clause. (On a separate, later appeal, the Second Circuit ruled on ATDS issues.)  
**Significance:** *Soliman* is the leading Second Circuit case on contract formation for TCPA short-code promotions and is regularly cited in opposition to motions to compel arbitration. **Note:** The user's prompt and certain reference materials describe *Soliman* as an ATDS case; the 2021 published decision principally addresses arbitration and inquiry-notice doctrine.  
  
### *Carl v. First National Bank of Omaha* — district-court line  
**Citation:** No. 2:19-cv-00504-GZS, 2021 WL 2444162 (D. Me. June 15, 2021)  
**Court:** U.S. District Court for the District of Maine (no First Circuit appellate decision verified at the citation provided in the prompt)  
**Year:** 2021  
**Facts:** Plaintiff received collection calls placed via the LiveVox HCI system; he alleged the system had the capacity to generate random or sequential telephone numbers.  
**Holding:** The court found that the system *may* have had random-generation capacity but that the bank did not use that capacity for the calls to plaintiff; consent was not effectively revoked.  
**Significance:** *Carl* is the first case to find that a LiveVox-type system may survive summary judgment on capacity, but it imposes a "use" requirement consistent with *Panzarella*. **Note:** The user's prompt cited *Carl v. First National Bank*, 95 F.4th 1 (1st Cir. 2024). That citation could not be verified. `[VERIFY CITATION]`.  
  
### *Tehrani v. Joie de Vivre Hospitality, LLC* — district court  
**Citation:** No. 3:19-cv-08168 (N.D. Cal. 2021)  
**Court:** U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California  
**Year:** 2021  
**Facts:** Plaintiff argued the defendant's system used a random or sequential generator to pick an index, then matched the index to a phone number from a list.  
**Holding:** The court rejected the "footnote 7" theory; "number generator" in § 227(a)(1) means *phone* number generator.  
**Significance:** Frequently cited in district courts adopting the *Borden* approach. **Note:** The user's prompt cited *Tehrani v. Joie de Vivre*, 102 F.4th 567 (9th Cir. 2024). No such Ninth Circuit decision could be verified. `[VERIFY CITATION]`.

## C. Prior express consent — implied consent, scope, related-purpose calls  
  
### *Mais v. Gulf Coast Collection Bureau, Inc.*, 768 F.3d 1110 (11th Cir. 2014)  
**Court:** Eleventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2014  
**Facts:** Mais's wife provided his cellphone number on hospital admission forms; the hospital later assigned the debt to Sheridan and Gulf Coast, which called Mais's cellphone using an autodialer.  
**Holding:** Reversing summary judgment for the plaintiff, the court held (a) under the Hobbs Act the district court could not collaterally invalidate the FCC's 2008 Order, and (b) the FCC's 2008 Order means that providing a cellphone number to a creditor reasonably evidences prior express consent to be contacted at that number about the related transaction or debt.  
**Significance:** *Mais* established the dominant "provision of number = consent for related calls" doctrine that creditors and collectors rely on heavily.  
  
### *Van Patten v. Vertical Fitness Group, LLC*, 847 F.3d 1037 (9th Cir. 2017)  
**Court:** Ninth Circuit  
**Year:** 2017  
**Facts:** Van Patten provided his cellphone number to enroll in a Vertical Fitness gym; after canceling membership, he received "comeback" promotional texts from a gym Vertical Fitness had acquired.  
**Holding:** Consent's scope depends on the transactional context. Providing a number to enroll at the gym constituted consent to receive promotional texts about the same gym membership and related fitness services. Van Patten's mere cancellation of membership did not revoke consent. The court also found Article III standing established by the receipt of unwanted texts.  
**Significance:** Foundational scope-of-consent decision in the Ninth Circuit and the foundational Ninth Circuit standing decision pre-*TransUnion*.  
  
### *Aderhold v. car2go N.A., LLC*, 668 F. App'x 795 (9th Cir. 2016)  
**Court:** Ninth Circuit (unpublished memorandum disposition)  
**Year:** 2016  
**Facts:** Aderhold registered for car2go's car-sharing service online and was sent a single confirmation text containing a validation code.  
**Holding:** By providing his cell number during registration, Aderhold consented to receive the confirmation text. The text was not telemarketing — it merely completed the consumer-initiated transaction.  
**Significance:** Establishes the rule that transactional and consent-confirmation texts following consumer-initiated registration do not require separate prior express written consent.  
  
### *Reyes v. Lincoln Automotive Financial Services*, 861 F.3d 51 (2d Cir. 2017) — see also § D below  
**Significance:** Closely related, addresses the *outer* boundary of consent scope: consent given as bargained-for consideration in a bilateral contract is, in the Second Circuit's view, not unilaterally revocable.

## D. Revocation of consent  
  
### *Gager v. Dell Financial Services, LLC*, 727 F.3d 265 (3d Cir. 2013)  
**Court:** Third Circuit  
**Year:** 2013  
**Facts:** Gager applied for Dell financing and provided her cell number; after falling behind on payments she sent Dell a letter demanding the calls stop.  
**Holding:** The TCPA permits consumers to revoke prior express consent at any time; there is no temporal limit. The court relied on common-law principles and on Congress's consumer-protective purpose. (Dell could still call manually — only autodialed/prerecorded calls were now barred.)  
**Significance:** First circuit-level decision establishing the right to revoke prior express consent. Codified by the FCC's 2015 Omnibus Order (upheld on this point in *ACA International*).  
  
### *Osorio v. State Farm Bank, F.S.B.*, 746 F.3d 1242 (11th Cir. 2014)  
**Court:** Eleventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2014  
**Facts:** Osorio received 327 autodialed collection calls about a credit-card debt owed by his housemate (with whom he shared a cellphone account). Osorio orally told the bank to stop calling.  
**Holding:** (1) "Prior express consent" can be revoked, including orally; and (2) only the actual subscriber to the cellphone — not the "intended recipient" — can provide consent.  
**Significance:** Confirms oral revocation in the Eleventh Circuit and clarifies that "called party" means the subscriber/regular user, not the intended recipient (see § E).  
  
### *Reyes v. Lincoln Automotive Financial Services*, 861 F.3d 51 (2d Cir. 2017)  
**Court:** Second Circuit  
**Year:** 2017  
**Facts:** Reyes leased a car from Lincoln; his lease contained a consent-to-be-contacted clause as part of the bargained-for terms. He later sent a revocation letter.  
**Holding:** When consent is given as bargained-for consideration in a bilateral contract, the consumer cannot unilaterally revoke that consent. The TCPA's common-law backdrop permits revocation of *gratuitous* consent but not of contractual consent.  
**Significance:** *Reyes* is the principal outlier limiting the broad-revocation rule of *Gager* and *Osorio*. The Eleventh Circuit reached the same conclusion in *Medley v. Dish Network*, 958 F.3d 1063 (11th Cir. 2020). The Third (in *Gager*) and other circuits have not addressed the contractual-consent point head-on. The FCC's 2015 ruling is in tension with *Reyes*; courts in *Reyes*-following jurisdictions have declined to apply that part of the Order.  
  
### *Pollard v. Wells Fargo* — bankruptcy-discharge revocation  
**Citation:** The user's prompt cites "*Pollard v. Wells Fargo*, 904 F.3d 757 (9th Cir. 2018)." That citation does not match a published Ninth Circuit TCPA decision involving discharge-as-revocation. `[VERIFY CITATION]`. The leading authority on bankruptcy and TCPA revocation is *Burton v. ## E. "Called party" — wrong-number and recycled-number cases  
  
### *Soppet v. Enhanced Recovery Co.*, 679 F.3d 637 (7th Cir. 2012)  
**Court:** Seventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2012  
**Facts:** Enhanced Recovery placed autodialed debt-collection calls to two cellphone numbers that had been reassigned. The prior subscribers had given consent; the new subscribers (Soppet and Tang) had not.  
**Holding:** "Called party" in § 227(b)(1) refers to the current subscriber at the time the call is placed — not the original consenting subscriber whose number was later reassigned. Consent does not transfer with the phone number.  
**Significance:** *Soppet*, written by Chief Judge Easterbrook, is the foundational reassigned-number decision. The FCC's 2015 Order adopted *Soppet*'s subscriber-based rule with a "one-call safe harbor"; that safe harbor was vacated in *ACA International*, leaving *Soppet*'s rule intact in the Seventh Circuit and persuasive elsewhere.  
  
### *Osorio v. State Farm Bank* — (see § D)  
The Eleventh Circuit adopted the same subscriber/regular-user reading as *Soppet*.  
  
### Reassigned Numbers Database  
In response to *ACA International* and continued reassigned-number litigation, the FCC created the Reassigned Numbers Database (RND) under the Second Report and Order, 33 FCC Rcd. 12024 (2018). Callers who query the RND and act on its results in good faith receive a safe-harbor defense to TCPA liability for calls to numbers reported reassigned. See 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(a)(4).

## F. Junk fax cases  
  
### *Bais Yaakov of Spring Valley v. FCC*, 852 F.3d 1078 (D.C. Cir. 2017)  
**Court:** D.C. Circuit  
**Year:** 2017  
**Facts:** Industry petitioners challenged the FCC's 2006 Solicited Fax Rule, which required opt-out notices on solicited as well as unsolicited fax advertisements.  
**Holding:** The FCC lacked statutory authority to require opt-out notices on solicited faxes. Section 227(b)(2)(D) authorizes opt-out notices only on unsolicited advertisements. The court vacated the rule to that extent.  
**Significance:** Eliminated a major source of strict-liability junk-fax litigation. The Eleventh Circuit (*Gorss Motels*) and Second Circuit later reinforced the result; the Supreme Court denied certiorari in 2018.  
  
### *Sandusky Wellness Center, LLC v. Medco Health Solutions, Inc.*, 788 F.3d 218 (6th Cir. 2015)  
**Court:** Sixth Circuit  
**Year:** 2015  
**Facts:** Medco, a pharmacy-benefit manager, sent two faxes to a chiropractor informing the practice about formulary changes. The faxes neither sold goods nor solicited purchases.  
**Holding:** A fax qualifies as an "unsolicited advertisement" under § 227(a)(5) only if it has a commercial purpose — i.e., it promotes the commercial availability or quality of property, goods, or services. Pure informational faxes are not advertisements.  
**Significance:** Established the dominant "primary purpose" test for fax advertisement analysis. Followed in most circuits.  
  
### *Gorss Motels, Inc. v. Safemark Systems, LP*, 931 F.3d 1094 (11th Cir. 2019)  
**Court:** Eleventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2019  
**Facts:** Gorss and other Wyndham franchisees received faxes from Safemark, a Wyndham-approved vendor. The franchise agreement gave Wyndham and its approved vendors permission to send promotional materials to franchisees.  
**Holding:** Faxes were solicited under the franchise agreement; opt-out notices were not required because *Bais Yaakov* invalidated the solicited-fax rule. The Eleventh Circuit also confirmed it would not defer to the FCC's 2006 Order under the Hobbs Act because the Order was interpretive (signaling the post-*PDR Network* approach).  
**Significance:** Combined consent and Hobbs Act doctrine in a leading fax case; effectively shut down a major plaintiff-side practice in the Eleventh Circuit.

## G. Standing post-*TransUnion v. Ramirez*  
  
### *Salcedo v. Hanna*, 936 F.3d 1162 (11th Cir. 2019)  
**Court:** Eleventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2019  
**Facts:** Salcedo received a single unsolicited promotional text message from his former attorney.  
**Holding:** A single unwanted text does not produce the kind of concrete injury required for Article III standing; it is qualitatively different from junk faxes (which tie up paper and toner) and prerecorded voice calls (which take longer to receive).  
**Significance:** Created a sharp circuit split. Followed by no other circuit and effectively overruled within the Eleventh Circuit by *Drazen*.  
  
### *Drazen v. Pinto*, 74 F.4th 1336 (11th Cir. 2023) (en banc)  
**Court:** Eleventh Circuit (en banc)  
**Year:** 2023  
**Facts:** Class settlement objector challenged inclusion of single-text class members, arguing they lacked Article III standing under *Salcedo*.  
**Holding:** Receipt of a single unwanted, illegal telemarketing text confers Article III standing. The harm is closely analogous to the common-law tort of intrusion upon seclusion; Congress's judgment that such intrusions are actionable warrants a lower threshold of injury. *Salcedo* was overruled to the extent inconsistent.  
**Significance:** Aligned the Eleventh Circuit with the Second, Third, Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits. After *Drazen*, no circuit holds that a single unwanted text categorically fails standing.  
  
### *Susinno v. Work Out World Inc.*, 862 F.3d 346 (3d Cir. 2017)  
**Court:** Third Circuit  
**Year:** 2017  
**Facts:** Plaintiff received one unsolicited prerecorded voicemail from a fitness chain.  
**Holding:** Receipt of a single unsolicited prerecorded call/voicemail satisfies Article III. Congress did not invent the injury; it elevated an existing common-law harm (intrusion, nuisance) to a statutory cause of action.  
**Significance:** Foundational standing decision in the Third Circuit, often paired with *Spokeo*. Survives *TransUnion*'s common-law-analogue analysis.  
  
### *Cordoba v. DIRECTV, LLC*, 942 F.3d 1259 (11th Cir. 2019)  
**Court:** Eleventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2019  
**Facts:** Plaintiffs alleged DIRECTV's vendor failed to maintain an internal company-specific Do-Not-Call list, in violation of 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(d).  
**Holding:** Standing under § 227(c)/§ 64.1200(d) requires that the call recipient previously asked not to be called; the bare procedural failure to maintain an internal DNC list, without traceable receipt of an unwanted call following such a request, is not a concrete injury. The court also held this can defeat class-wide commonality and predominance.  
**Significance:** Cordoba shaped class certification practice in internal-DNC cases and was reinforced by *TransUnion* (594 U.S. 413 (2021)). The Eleventh Circuit later applied *Cordoba* to vacate a $35M class settlement in *Drazen v. GoDaddy* on remand.

## H. Vicarious liability  
  
### *In re Dish Network, LLC*, 28 FCC Rcd. 6574 (FCC 2013) — Declaratory Ruling  
**Agency:** Federal Communications Commission  
**Year:** 2013  
**Facts:** Petition addressed seller liability for telemarketing calls placed by third-party agents.  
**Holding:** The FCC announced that a seller may be held vicariously liable under federal common-law agency principles — actual authority, apparent authority, and ratification — for TCPA violations committed by third-party telemarketers. The seller does not "initiate" calls in the direct-liability sense but can be reached through agency principles.  
**Significance:** Although not a court decision, the *Dish Network* Ruling is foundational. Every circuit to consider vicarious liability under the TCPA has applied federal-common-law agency, and most have cited the FCC ruling as persuasive. See *Gomez v. Campbell-Ewald*, 768 F.3d 871 (9th Cir. 2014) (affirmed on other grounds by the Supreme Court).  
  
### *Aranda v. Caribbean Cruise Line, Inc.*, 179 F. Supp. 3d 817 (N.D. Ill. 2016)  
**Court:** Northern District of Illinois  
**Year:** 2016  
**Facts:** Class action against Caribbean Cruise Line, Economic Strategy Group, and other parties for autodialed "free cruise" calls placed by polling vendors.  
**Holding:** Vendors of marketing services and the underlying client may all be vicariously liable; ratification can be inferred from acceptance of benefits with knowledge of how leads were generated.  
**Significance:** Leading district-court application of the *Dish Network* framework. Set up the $76M class settlement later affirmed in *Birchmeier v. Caribbean Cruise Line* (7th Cir. 2018) (see § I).  
  
### *Henderson v. United Student Aid Funds*, 918 F.3d 1068 (9th Cir. 2019)  
**Court:** Ninth Circuit  
**Year:** 2019  
**Facts:** Plaintiffs argued that a student-loan guarantor was directly or vicariously liable for collection calls by sub-servicers.  
**Holding:** Creditors are not automatically vicariously liable for calls placed by their servicers and collectors; plaintiffs must plead actual or apparent authority or ratification.  
**Significance:** Confirms agency principles, not strict-liability, govern in the Ninth Circuit.

## I. Class certification in TCPA cases  
  
### *Birchmeier v. Caribbean Cruise Line, Inc.*, 896 F.3d 792 (7th Cir. 2018)  
**Court:** Seventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2018  
**Facts:** Approval of a $56–$76M class settlement covering approximately 1 million telemarketing calls.  
**Holding:** The Seventh Circuit (Hamilton, J.) affirmed approval of the settlement and a sliding-scale fee award, emphasizing that novel theories of vicarious liability warranted higher-than-comparable fees because the class would have lost absent the settlement.  
**Significance:** Anchor decision for TCPA class settlement approval and fee jurisprudence.  
  
### *Krakauer v. Dish Network, LLC*, 925 F.3d 643 (4th Cir. 2019)  
**Court:** Fourth Circuit  
**Year:** 2019  
**Facts:** Class action against Dish Network for telemarketing calls placed by retailer Satellite Systems Network (SSN) to numbers on the National Do-Not-Call Registry. After a four-day jury trial, the jury awarded $400 per call; the district court trebled the award based on willfulness, yielding a $61M judgment.  
**Holding:** The Fourth Circuit affirmed (i) class certification; (ii) Dish's agency liability for SSN's calls; (iii) the trebling decision based on willful or knowing violations; and (iv) Article III standing for each class member.  
**Significance:** Single largest TCPA verdict to be affirmed on appeal. Sets the template for willfulness/treble-damages analysis and for class-wide proof in Do-Not-Call cases.  
  
### *Bee, Denning, Inc. v. Capital Alliance Group*, 310 F.R.D. 614 (S.D. Cal. 2015)  
**Court:** Southern District of California  
**Year:** 2015  
**Facts:** Class certification in a fax-blast TCPA case; the parties disputed whether class members could be identified objectively.  
**Holding:** The court certified the class, holding that fax transmission logs and account records provided an objective basis for class identification.  
**Significance:** Frequently cited for the proposition that TCPA defendant call/fax records typically satisfy ascertainability. **Note:** The user's prompt referenced "*Bee, Denning v. Trustees of CCSD*" — that case caption could not be verified. `[VERIFY CITATION]`. The verified leading decision is *Bee, Denning, Inc. v. Capital Alliance Group*.  
  
### Settlement-class disputes  
Post-*TransUnion* and post-*Cordoba*, several circuits have vacated TCPA class settlements where settlement classes included uninjured members. See *Drazen v. Pinto* (panel decision) and *In re Godaddy.com LLC TCPA Litigation*, 21 F.4th 1300 (11th Cir. 2021) (vacating settlement); *Frank v. Gaos*, 586 U.S. 485 (2019) (cy pres concerns; remanded).

## J. Damages and willfulness  
  
### Statutory framework  
Under § 227(b)(3), private plaintiffs may recover the greater of actual damages or $500 per violation, trebled to $1,500 per violation for knowing or willful conduct. Under § 227(c)(5), the same structure applies to Do-Not-Call Registry violations. Multiple call/text violations are individually actionable.  
  
### *Krakauer v. Dish Network*, 925 F.3d 643 (4th Cir. 2019) — trebling analysis  
**Significance:** Affirmed the district court's exercise of discretion in trebling the $400-per-call statutory award. The Fourth Circuit endorsed the view that "willful or knowing" requires only that the defendant knew it was placing the calls — not that it knew the calls violated the TCPA. Courts in the Eighth Circuit have, by contrast, required something closer to knowledge of unlawfulness. *Bridgeview Health Care Center Ltd. v. Clark*, 816 F.3d 935 (7th Cir. 2016), takes a middle position requiring "knowledge of the facts that constituted the offense."  
  
### Damages-stacking  
TCPA damages are not capped by statute. In high-volume autodialer or fax cases, exposures of $50M–$500M are common. See *Bridging Communities v. Top Flite Financial*, 843 F.3d 1119 (6th Cir. 2016).

## K. State action / sovereign immunity / preemption  
  
### Federal preemption  
Section 227(e)(1) preserves state authority to impose more restrictive intrastate requirements; section 227(f) provides for state attorney general enforcement actions. State TCPA-mini statutes (e.g., Florida Telephone Solicitation Act, Washington's CEMA, Oklahoma's Telephone Solicitation Act) operate alongside the federal TCPA.  
  
### *Patriotic Veterans, Inc. v. Indiana*, 736 F.3d 1041 (7th Cir. 2013)  
**Court:** Seventh Circuit  
**Year:** 2013  
**Facts:** Robocaller challenged Indiana's automatic-dialer ban as preempted by the TCPA and unconstitutional under the First Amendment.  
**Holding:** Indiana's stricter law was not preempted; the TCPA expressly preserves more-restrictive state laws.  
**Significance:** Confirms the TCPA's floor-not-ceiling preemption model. The First Amendment ruling was later revisited post-*Reed v. Town of Gilbert*.  
  
### State AG enforcement  
Section 227(g) authorizes state attorneys general to bring federal-court enforcement actions on behalf of state residents. Recent examples include actions brought by the Pennsylvania, Ohio, and New York attorneys general against robocall traffic pumpers and STIR/SHAKEN non-compliant carriers.

## L. Robocall Mitigation, TRACED Act, STIR/SHAKEN, and the 2024 AI Robocall Ruling  
  
### TRACED Act, Pub. L. No. 116-105 (2019)  
The Pallone-Thune Telephone Robocall Abuse Criminal Enforcement and Deterrence (TRACED) Act amended Title 47 to (i) extend the statute of limitations for FCC enforcement against intentional violations to 4 years; (ii) require implementation of the STIR/SHAKEN caller-ID authentication framework; (iii) increase per-violation civil penalties to up to $10,000; and (iv) direct the FCC to create the Reassigned Numbers Database.  
  
### *FCC, Declaratory Ruling on AI-Generated Voices*, FCC 24-17 (Feb. 8, 2024)  
**Agency:** Federal Communications Commission  
**Year:** 2024  
**Holding:** AI-generated voices fall within the TCPA's prohibition on "artificial or prerecorded voice" calls under § 227(b)(1). Calls using voice-cloning, voice-deepfake, or other AI-generated voice technology require prior express consent (and where applicable, prior express written consent for telemarketing) and must include the identification and opt-out disclosures required by § 227(d) and 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(b).  
**Significance:** The Ruling was effective immediately upon adoption. It triggered both administrative enforcement (e.g., the August 2024 Lingo Telecom consent decree for $1M for transmitting AI-generated Biden-impersonation robocalls in the 2024 New Hampshire primary) and a wave of private TCPA litigation against political consultants and marketers using AI-cloned voices.  
  
### STIR/SHAKEN  
Codified at 47 U.S.C. § 227b and implemented at 47 C.F.R. § 64.6300 et seq. Voice service providers must authenticate caller-ID information for IP-originated calls. Non-compliance can result in carrier-level blocking, downstream loss of call termination, and FCC enforcement. The FCC has used STIR/SHAKEN compliance as the predicate for blocking traffic from non-attesting providers (e.g., the *Robocall Mitigation Database* enforcement series, 2022–2025).  
  
### 2024–2025 enforcement highlights  
- *Lingo Telecom* consent decree, $1,000,000 (Aug. 21, 2024).  
- *Steve Kramer* forfeiture order, $6,000,000 (Sept. 26, 2024) — Truth-in-Caller-ID Act violation for AI-generated Biden-impersonation robocalls.  
- FCC NPRM on AI-disclosure requirements for political robocalls (2024).
