# 12 C.F.R. Part 1006 — Regulation F (Debt Collection Practices)

**Topic:** CFPB's Implementing Regulation for the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)
**Primary Statute:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692–1692p (FDCPA)
**Primary Regulation:** 12 C.F.R. Part 1006 (Regulation F)
**Regulatory Authority:** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau
**Effective Date:** November 30, 2021
**Last Updated:** May 2026

---

## Overview and Regulatory Framework

Regulation F, codified at 12 C.F.R. Part 1006, is the CFPB's comprehensive implementing regulation for the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act. The CFPB finalized Regulation F in two stages: the October 2020 Final Rule (85 Fed. Reg. 76734, Nov. 30, 2020) addressing communications, harassment, and other debt collection practices, and the December 2020 Final Rule (86 Fed. Reg. 5766, Jan. 19, 2021) addressing validation notices and time-barred debt provisions. Both rules took effect November 30, 2021.

Regulation F represents the first comprehensive federal rulemaking under the FDCPA since its enactment in 1977. It modernizes the statute's application to electronic communications, establishes quantitative call-frequency limits, restructures the validation notice, codifies the prohibition on time-barred-debt lawsuits, and creates the "passive collection" prohibition against credit report "parking."

**A violation of Regulation F is simultaneously a violation of the FDCPA** under 15 U.S.C. § 1692l(d) and is enforceable through the FDCPA's private right of action under § 1692k (statutory damages up to $1,000 per individual action; actual damages without cap; attorney's fees and costs; and class damages up to the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of net worth).

## § 1006.2 — Definitions

### Key Definitions for Litigation

**(b) "Attempt to communicate"** — Any act to initiate a communication or other contact about a debt with any person through any medium, including by soliciting a response from such person. An attempt to communicate includes leaving a limited-content message. This definition is critical for call-frequency counting: even unanswered calls and voicemail-only attempts count toward the 7-in-7 threshold.

**(d) "Communication"** — The conveying of information regarding a debt directly or indirectly to any person through any medium. This expansive definition encompasses oral, written, electronic, and any other medium. A communication triggers third-party disclosure rules, the mini-Miranda requirement, and time/place restrictions.

**(e) "Consumer"** — A natural person obligated or allegedly obligated to pay any debt. For communication provisions, also includes the consumer's spouse, parent (if consumer is a minor), guardian, executor, or administrator.

**(h) "Debt"** — Any obligation or alleged obligation of a consumer to pay money arising out of a transaction in which the money, property, insurance, or services that are the subject of the transaction are primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, whether or not such obligation has been reduced to judgment. Tracks 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(5).

**(i) "Debt collector"** — Any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another. This definition explicitly encompasses:
- Third-party collection agencies
- Debt buyers (who purchase defaulted debt and collect in their own name but on accounts "owed another" at the time of default)
- Collection law firms
- Servicers who acquire accounts in default

**The debt buyer inclusion is particularly significant for litigation.** The CFPB confirmed in the Regulation F preamble (85 Fed. Reg. at 76746–52) that debt buyers who regularly collect debts that were in default when acquired qualify as "debt collectors" under the FDCPA and Regulation F, regardless of whether they collect in their own name as owner. This codifies *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA LLC*, 582 U.S. 79 (2017)'s dicta and resolves circuit splits regarding default-status debt purchasers.

**(j) "Limited-content message"** — A voicemail message for a consumer that includes only:
- Required: (i) a business name that does not indicate debt collection; (ii) a request to reply; (iii) name of one or more natural persons to contact; (iv) a telephone number
- Optional: a statement the call relates to "an account"; a generic salutation; suggested call-back times

A limited-content message is NOT a "communication" (so it does not trigger third-party disclosure liability) but IS an "attempt to communicate" (so it counts toward call-frequency limits).

## § 1006.6 — Communications in Connection with Debt Collection

### Time and Place Restrictions (§ 1006.6(b))

A debt collector may not communicate with a consumer at a time or place known or that should be known to be inconvenient. The regulation establishes:
- **Presumptively inconvenient times:** Before 8:00 a.m. and after 9:00 p.m. local time at the consumer's location
- **Workplace prohibition:** Communication at the consumer's place of employment is prohibited if the collector knows or has reason to know the employer prohibits it
- The presumptions are rebuttable but function as bright-line rules in practice

### Attorney Representation (§ 1006.6(b)(4))

Once a debt collector knows the consumer is represented by an attorney with regard to the debt, the collector must communicate only with that attorney (unless attorney fails to respond within a reasonable time or consents to direct contact).

### Cease-Communication Rights (§ 1006.6(c))

If a consumer notifies a debt collector in writing to cease communication:
- The debt collector must cease all communications except to advise (i) collection efforts are terminated; (ii) specific remedies may be invoked; or (iii) the collector intends to invoke a specific remedy
- Electronic opt-out under § 1006.6(e) applies per-channel (email address or phone number), not globally

### Electronic Communications Framework (§ 1006.6(d)–(e))

**Email — Permissible Procedures (§ 1006.6(d)(4)):**
The collector must obtain the email address through one of three "Bedrock Procedures":
1. **Direct provision:** Consumer provided the email directly to the debt collector
2. **Creditor-source with opt-out:** Obtained from the prior creditor, with notice to consumer and reasonable opt-out opportunity before first use
3. **Prior-collector carry-forward:** Obtained from a prior debt collector after the prior collector satisfied Procedure 2

**Text Messages — Permissible Procedures (§ 1006.6(d)(5)):**
- Must verify the number has not been reassigned (using FCC Reassigned Numbers Database or other reasonable procedures)
- Consumer must have used the number to communicate about the debt, or provided it to the current or prior creditor

**Opt-Out Requirements (§ 1006.6(e)):**
- Every electronic communication must contain a clear and conspicuous opt-out method
- The method must be "reasonable and simple" (reply STOP, hyperlink, dedicated phone line)
- Cannot require a fee or additional information beyond opt-out preferences
- Must be honored within a reasonable time (no later than the next business day is best practice)
- Opt-out applies per-channel: opting out of email does not opt out of text, and vice versa

### Litigation Implications of § 1006.6

Pleading a § 1006.6 violation in a complaint:
- **Inconvenient time claims:** Allege specific call times (from phone records) outside 8am–9pm
- **Electronic communication claims:** Allege collector used email/text without satisfying a Bedrock Procedure; allege collector continued contact after opt-out
- **Third-party disclosure:** Allege voicemail content exceeded limited-content-message parameters, exposing debt information to household members

## § 1006.14 — Harassing, Oppressive, or Abusive Conduct

### The 7-in-7 Call Frequency Rule (§ 1006.14(b))

**Presumption of violation:** A debt collector is presumed to violate the harassment prohibition if it places telephone calls to a person in connection with the collection of a particular debt:
1. More than **seven (7) times** within any **seven (7) consecutive-day period**; OR
2. Within **seven (7) days** after engaging in a **telephone conversation** with the person about the particular debt

**Presumption of compliance:** A debt collector is presumed NOT to violate if it stays at or below these thresholds.

**Critical calculation rules:**
- **Per-debt, not per-consumer:** The 7-in-7 count is calculated for each "particular debt." A collector holding 3 accounts for one consumer could theoretically make 21 calls in 7 days (7 per account)
- **"Telephone call" includes:** Completed calls, unanswered calls, calls to voicemail, limited-content messages — ALL count toward the cap
- **"Telephone conversation" trigger:** Only an actual two-way conversation triggers the second prong (7-day cooldown after conversation)
- **Rolling window:** The 7-day period rolls continuously; it is not a calendar-week reset
- **Multiple numbers:** Calls to different numbers belonging to the same consumer count in aggregate for a particular debt

**Rebuttal:** Both presumptions are rebuttable. A collector at 6 calls could still violate if circumstances show harassment (e.g., calling elderly consumer repeatedly despite request to stop). A collector at 8 calls could potentially rebut if it shows legitimate operational reason and absence of harassing intent, though this is extremely difficult in practice.

### Other Harassment Provisions (§ 1006.14(a))

The general prohibition against harassment, oppression, and abuse remains, encompassing:
- Threats of violence or criminal means
- Use of obscene or profane language
- Publishing lists of consumers who refuse to pay (except to CRAs)
- Advertising debt for sale to coerce payment
- Causing a telephone to ring continuously to annoy
- Placing calls without meaningful disclosure of caller's identity

## § 1006.18 — False, Deceptive, or Misleading Representations

### Prohibited Representations (§ 1006.18(b)–(e))

A debt collector must not use any false, deceptive, or misleading representation or means in connection with collection. Specific prohibitions:

1. **False character/amount/legal status:** Misrepresenting the character, amount, or legal status of any debt (§ 1006.18(b)(1))
2. **False threat of action:** Threatening to take any action that cannot legally be taken or that is not intended to be taken (§ 1006.18(b)(2))
3. **False government affiliation:** Falsely representing or implying affiliation with the government (§ 1006.18(b)(3))
4. **Misrepresenting consequences:** Representing that nonpayment will result in arrest, imprisonment, garnishment, attachment, or seizure unless such action is lawful AND the collector intends to take it (§ 1006.18(b)(4))
5. **Threat to communicate credit information known to be false:** Including failure to communicate that a disputed debt is disputed (§ 1006.18(b)(5))
6. **False document authority:** Using or distributing any written communication that simulates a legal process or government document (§ 1006.18(b)(9))
7. **False credit report threats:** Falsely representing that accounts have been or will be turned over to innocent purchasers for value (§ 1006.18(b)(12))
8. **False attorney involvement:** Representing that documents are from an attorney when they are not (§ 1006.18(b)(13))

### Mini-Miranda Disclosure (§ 1006.18(e))

The initial communication must disclose:
- That the debt collector is attempting to collect a debt
- That any information obtained will be used for that purpose

Subsequent communications must disclose that the communication is from a debt collector. Failure to include either disclosure is a per-se § 1006.18 violation.

### Litigation Implications

In NDGA practice, § 1006.18 violations commonly arise from:
- Collection letters demanding amounts exceeding what is owed (interest miscalculations, unauthorized fees)
- Threats of litigation when the collector has no intention or authority to sue
- Threat of credit reporting consequences for time-barred debt
- Misidentification of the creditor or incorrect account information in collection communications
- Letters from law firms that were not meaningfully reviewed by an attorney

## § 1006.22 — Unfair or Unconscionable Means

### Prohibited Collection Methods

**(b) Unauthorized amounts:** Collecting any amount (including interest, service charge, fee, or expense) not expressly authorized by the agreement creating the debt or permitted by law. This provision is the basis for the CFPB's 2022 Advisory Opinion on "pay-to-pay" convenience fees.

**(f) Communication restrictions:**
1. **Envelope/postcard rules:** No language or symbol indicating debt collection on any envelope or postcard
2. **Workplace email:** Must not use an email address known to be employer-provided, unless the consumer gave it directly or used it to communicate about the debt
3. **Social media:** Must not communicate via social media in any manner viewable by the general public or the consumer's contacts. Private/direct messaging is permitted.

### Pay-to-Pay Fees (CFPB Advisory Opinion, 87 Fed. Reg. 39733)

The CFPB's July 2022 advisory opinion clarifies that convenience fees charged for making payments by phone or online violate § 1006.22(b) unless:
- The original credit agreement expressly authorizes such fees; OR
- State law specifically permits the fee

Most consumer credit agreements do not authorize pay-to-pay fees, making this a fertile source of FDCPA claims.

## § 1006.26 — Collection of Time-Barred Debt

### Core Prohibition

A debt collector must not bring or threaten to bring a legal action against a consumer to collect a time-barred debt. "Time-barred debt" means debt for which the applicable statute of limitations has expired.

### Key Regulatory Principles

- **Strict liability standard:** The CFPB's May 2023 advisory opinion (88 Fed. Reg. 26475) confirms this prohibition applies without regard to the collector's knowledge of the time-barred status. A debt collector's "mistake" about the SOL is not a defense under Regulation F (though bona fide error under § 1692k(c) may still apply in litigation).
- **"Threaten" broadly defined:** Includes any communication that would lead the least sophisticated consumer to believe legal action might be taken
- **State SOL governs:** The applicable limitations period is determined by state law (in Georgia, generally 4 years for open accounts under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-25 or 6 years for written contracts under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-24)
- **Collection without suit is permitted:** The prohibition applies only to bringing or threatening legal action; a debt collector may still contact the consumer to collect time-barred debt through non-litigation means (though state laws may impose additional restrictions)

### Revival/Tolling Issues

Regulation F's preamble acknowledged the complexity of SOL revival (partial payment restarting the clock in some states) but declined to impose disclosure requirements. In Georgia, a partial payment on a time-barred debt may revive the debt under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-96, creating a trap for consumers. Collectors who accept partial payments on Georgia time-barred debt without disclosure face potential § 1006.18 liability for misleading the consumer about the legal status of the debt.

## § 1006.34 — Validation Notice Requirements

### Timing

Validation information must be provided:
- In the initial communication with the consumer; OR
- In writing or electronically within five (5) days after the initial communication

### Required Content (§ 1006.34(c))

The validation notice must include:

**1. Debt collector identification:**
- Name and mailing address of the debt collector
- Mini-Miranda disclosure

**2. Debt information:**
- Consumer's name and mailing address
- Name of the creditor to whom the debt is currently owed
- Account number (or partial account number)
- **Itemization table:**
  - Itemization date (one of five permissible dates: last statement, last payment, charge-off, transaction, or judgment date)
  - Amount as of the itemization date
  - Itemization of interest, fees, payments, and credits since the itemization date
  - Current amount of the debt

**3. Consumer rights information:**
- Validation period end date (30 days from receipt)
- Statement of right to dispute
- Statement of right to request original-creditor information
- Consequences of disputing/not disputing

**4. Consumer response form:**
- Tear-off or response section with dispute options
- Checkboxes for common dispute reasons (not my debt, wrong amount, other)

### Model Forms (Appendix B)

- **Form B-1:** Standard validation notice (primary safe harbor)
- **Form B-2:** Mortgage debt variant with periodic statement reference
- **Form B-3:** Debt-buyer variant

Substantial compliance with a model form creates a safe harbor. Deviations from the model form that are more than minor risk losing safe-harbor protection.

### Litigation Implications

Validation notice deficiencies are among the most commonly litigated Regulation F violations:
- Missing or incorrect itemization tables
- Wrong itemization date selection
- Failure to include consumer response form
- Required content not on the front of the first page
- Delivery more than 5 days after initial communication
- Inconsistency between validation notice amounts and other collection communications

## § 1006.38 — Disputes and Verification

### Consumer Dispute Rights

If a consumer disputes the debt (or any portion) or requests original-creditor information within the 30-day validation period, the debt collector must:
1. **Cease collection** until verification is mailed to the consumer
2. **Obtain verification** — the debt is verified (evidence establishing the debt's validity)
3. **Mail verification** or original-creditor information to the consumer

### Duplicative Disputes

A debt collector need not re-investigate a dispute that is "substantially the same" as a prior dispute already responded to, but must:
- Notify the consumer the dispute is duplicative
- Provide a copy of the prior response
- Identify why the current dispute is duplicative

### Interaction with FCRA

A disputed debt must be reported as disputed to CRAs under FCRA § 1681s-2(a)(3) and Reg F § 1006.18(c)(2). Failure to note the dispute in credit reporting is independently actionable under both the FDCPA (false/misleading representation) and the FCRA (furnisher duties).

## § 1006.42 — Electronic Delivery of Required Disclosures

### Requirements for Electronic Delivery

A debt collector may deliver required disclosures (validation notice, opt-out notice, etc.) electronically only if:
1. The delivery method is reasonably expected to provide actual notice
2. The disclosure is in a form the consumer may keep and access later
3. E-SIGN Act § 101(c) consent requirements are satisfied (15 U.S.C. § 7001(c))

### Undeliverable Electronic Communications

If a debt collector receives a notice of undeliverability (bounce-back, delivery failure), the communication was NOT sent in a manner reasonably expected to provide actual notice. The collector must attempt alternative delivery.

### Hyperlink Delivery

A required disclosure may be delivered via hyperlink in an electronic message only if the collector follows specific safe-harbor procedures (secure messaging portal or direct-access hyperlink that does not require account login).

## How Regulation F Differs from and Expands the FDCPA Statute

| Issue | FDCPA Statute | Regulation F Addition |
|---|---|---|
| Call frequency | General "harassment" standard | Quantitative 7-in-7 presumption |
| Electronic communications | Not addressed | Full email/text framework with Bedrock Procedures |
| Validation notice content | Minimal requirements | Detailed itemization table, model forms, response section |
| Time-barred debt | Case law split | Explicit prohibition on suit/threat of suit |
| Credit report parking | Not addressed | 14-day anti-parking rule (§ 1006.30(a)) |
| Social media | Not addressed | Public-viewable communication prohibited |
| Voicemail | Case law split | Limited-content message safe harbor |
| Opt-out mechanisms | Written cease-and-desist | Per-channel electronic opt-out |
| Pay-to-pay fees | General "unfair means" | Specific prohibition via advisory opinion |
| Record retention | Not addressed | 3-year retention requirement |

## Litigation Implications of Regulation F Provisions

### Pleading Reg F Violations

In NDGA complaints, Regulation F violations are pled as FDCPA violations (since Reg F violations are FDCPA violations under § 1692l(d)):

**Sample allegation structure:**
> "Defendant violated 15 U.S.C. § 1692d and 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(1)(i) by placing [number] telephone calls to Plaintiff in connection with the collection of the subject debt within a seven-consecutive-day period, exceeding the seven-call presumption of violation established by the CFPB's Regulation F."

### Damages Framework

- **Statutory damages:** Up to $1,000 per action under § 1692k(a)(2)(A)
- **Actual damages:** No cap; includes emotional distress damages supported by Reg F violations as evidence of egregiousness
- **Attorney's fees:** Mandatory under § 1692k(a)(3) for prevailing plaintiffs
- **Class damages:** Up to lesser of $500,000 or 1% of net worth

### Evidentiary Value

Regulation F provisions are particularly valuable in litigation because:
1. They establish bright-line rules (7-in-7, 5-day validation timing, specific content requirements) that eliminate factual disputes about what the law requires
2. The CFPB's Official Interpretations (Supplement I) receive deference under *Kisor v. Wilkie*, 588 U.S. 558 (2019)
3. CFPB Supervisory Highlights and enforcement actions provide persuasive evidence of industry-standard compliance expectations
