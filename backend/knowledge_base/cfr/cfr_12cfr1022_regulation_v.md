# 12 C.F.R. Part 1022 — Regulation V (Fair Credit Reporting)

**Topic:** CFPB's Implementing Regulation for the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)
**Primary Statute:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1681–1681x (FCRA)
**Primary Regulation:** 12 C.F.R. Part 1022 (Regulation V)
**Regulatory Authority:** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (transferred from FTC/banking agencies by Dodd-Frank § 1088)
**Last Updated:** May 2026

---

## Overview and Regulatory History

Regulation V implements the Fair Credit Reporting Act as transferred to the CFPB by the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010. The regulation was originally promulgated by various federal agencies (FTC, OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC, OTS, NCUA) under their respective pre-Dodd-Frank FCRA authorities. Effective July 21, 2011, rulemaking authority for most FCRA provisions transferred to the CFPB, which recodified the relevant regulations at 12 C.F.R. Part 1022.

Regulation V is the single most important regulatory source for FCRA litigation because it defines the specific duties of furnishers (creditors, debt collectors, and other data suppliers who report consumer information to consumer reporting agencies), the duties of users of consumer reports, and the procedures that operationalize statutory rights such as the right to dispute inaccurate information.

## Subpart A — General Provisions (§§ 1022.1–1022.3)

### § 1022.1 — Purpose, scope, and model forms and disclosures

Regulation V's stated purpose is to implement the FCRA and prescribe regulations concerning consumer report accuracy, identity theft protections, furnisher duties, user obligations, and affiliate marketing restrictions. The scope covers all persons subject to the FCRA, including consumer reporting agencies (CRAs), furnishers of information, and users of consumer reports.

**Model Forms and Disclosures:** Use of model forms (where provided in appendices) constitutes compliance with the applicable regulatory requirement. This safe-harbor principle is important in litigation: a defendant who substantially complies with a model form can assert the model-form defense.

### § 1022.2 — Definitions

Key definitions for litigation purposes:

- **"Accuracy"** — means that information a furnisher provides to a CRA about an account or other relationship with the consumer correctly reflects the terms of and liability for the account or relationship and correctly reflects the consumer's performance and other conduct with respect to the account or relationship. (§ 1022.41(a))
- **"Integrity"** — means that information a furnisher provides to a CRA about an account or relationship is substantively complete so as not to be likely to be materially misleading (with reference to Appendix E guidelines). (§ 1022.41(d))
- **"Direct dispute"** — a dispute submitted by a consumer directly to a furnisher (as opposed to through a CRA). (§ 1022.41(c))
- **"Furnisher"** — an entity that furnishes information relating to consumers to one or more CRAs for inclusion in a consumer report. (§ 1022.41(b))
- **"Consumer report"** — has the same meaning as in 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(d).
- **"Consumer reporting agency"** — has the same meaning as in 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(f).

### § 1022.3 — Disclosures

Establishes that disclosures required by Regulation V must be clear and conspicuous. When a regulation provision requires written notice, electronic delivery may satisfy the requirement if consistent with E-SIGN Act requirements (15 U.S.C. § 7001 et seq.).

## Subpart B — Disposal of Consumer Report Information (§§ 1022.20–1022.22)

### § 1022.20 — Scope

Applies to any person who maintains or otherwise possesses consumer information or compiled consumer reports for a business purpose. This includes creditors, insurers, employers, landlords, debt collectors, and any other user of consumer reports.

### § 1022.21 — Proper disposal of records

Implements FCRA § 1681w. Any person who possesses or maintains consumer report information for a business purpose must properly dispose of such information by taking reasonable measures to protect against unauthorized access to or use of the information in connection with its disposal. Reasonable measures include:

1. Burning, pulverizing, or shredding papers containing consumer report information
2. Erasing or destroying electronic media containing consumer report information
3. Contracting with a third party engaged in the business of record destruction to dispose of material
4. Implementing and monitoring compliance with policies and procedures that address disposal

**Litigation relevance:** Failure to properly dispose creates a private right of action under § 1681n (willful) or § 1681o (negligent). Particularly relevant when debt collectors or servicers improperly discard files containing consumer credit information.

## Subpart C — Affiliate Marketing (§§ 1022.20–1022.27)

### § 1022.21 — Affiliate marketing opt-out

Implements FCRA § 1681s-3. When an affiliate of a person receives eligibility information (consumer report information or other information communicated among affiliates) about a consumer, the person may not use that information to make a solicitation for marketing purposes to the consumer unless:

1. The consumer is provided a clear, conspicuous, and concise opt-out notice; AND
2. The consumer has not opted out within a reasonable opportunity period (not less than 30 days from mailing or electronic delivery)

**Key provisions:**
- The opt-out must be effective for at least five years (renewable)
- If consumer opts out, the person may not use eligibility information from affiliates for marketing solicitations during the opt-out period
- Exceptions exist for pre-existing business relationships, responses to consumer inquiries, and certain communications within affiliates that do not use eligibility information

**Litigation relevance in NDGA:** Affiliate marketing violations create potential claims under § 1681s-3, particularly where large financial holding companies share consumer information among subsidiaries for cross-marketing without proper notice and opt-out.

## Subpart D — Medical Information (§§ 1022.30–1022.32)

### § 1022.30 — Obtaining or using medical information in connection with credit

Implements FCRA § 1681b(g). A creditor may not obtain or use medical information pertaining to a consumer in connection with any determination of the consumer's eligibility or continued eligibility for credit, except in limited circumstances:

1. To determine whether a medical expense is covered by insurance
2. In connection with the consumer's eligibility for a particular type of credit product specifically designed for consumers with medical expenses (medical credit cards)
3. Where the consumer specifically consents in writing for the information to be considered

### § 1022.31 — Limits on sharing medical information for affiliate marketing

Medical information or medically-derived information received from an affiliate may not be used for marketing solicitations unless the consumer provides specific, separate, written consent.

### § 1022.32 — Sharing medical information with affiliates

Restricts sharing medical information with affiliates except as necessary for insurance underwriting or as specifically permitted by HIPAA and state law.

**Litigation relevance:** Medical debt reporting violations are increasingly significant given the March 2023 CFPB rule removing medical collections under $500 from credit reports (later vacated in part) and the ongoing scrutiny of medical debt furnishing practices.

## Subpart E — Duties of Furnishers of Information (§§ 1022.40–1022.43)

**This is the most critical subpart for FCRA litigation in the Northern District of Georgia.** Subpart E establishes the regulatory framework for furnisher obligations that give rise to private causes of action under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b).

### § 1022.40 — Scope

Subpart E applies to any furnisher that furnishes information to a CRA about a consumer. This includes banks, credit unions, finance companies, mortgage servicers, auto lenders, student loan servicers, utility companies, telecommunications companies, debt collectors, and debt buyers.

### § 1022.41 — Definitions

Four critical definitions anchor Subpart E litigation:

**(a) "Accuracy"** — Information is accurate if it correctly reflects:
- The terms of and liability for the account or other relationship
- The consumer's performance and other conduct with respect to the account or relationship

This definition means that technically correct but misleading information (e.g., reporting a paid collection as still owing, reporting an incorrect balance after settlement, reporting the wrong date of first delinquency) is "inaccurate" for Regulation V purposes even if some subset of the reported data points are literally true. Courts in the Eleventh Circuit have held that a furnisher's obligation to report accurately encompasses the duty to not report misleading information. See *Felts v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 893 F.3d 1305 (11th Cir. 2018).

**(b) "Furnisher"** — An entity that furnishes information relating to consumers to one or more CRAs for inclusion in a consumer report. Critically, this includes debt collectors and debt buyers who furnish tradeline data or collection accounts.

**(c) "Direct dispute"** — A dispute submitted by a consumer directly to the furnisher of information, rather than through the CRA dispute process under § 1681i. The direct dispute mechanism was added by the FACT Act of 2003 and implemented by § 1022.43.

**(d) "Integrity"** — Information has integrity if it is substantively complete so as not to be likely to be materially misleading. The integrity standard is measured against the Interagency Guidelines in Appendix E. This means a furnisher violates its duty even when individual data fields are "accurate" if the overall reported tradeline is misleading because key information is omitted or reported in a misleading context.

**Practical application:** In NDGA litigation, the accuracy/integrity definitions support claims where:
- A debt collector reports a collection account without noting the consumer's dispute
- A creditor reports a balance that does not reflect a settlement agreement
- A mortgage servicer reports delinquency that occurred during a COVID-19 forbearance
- A furnisher reports an account as "open" when it was closed by the consumer
- A debt buyer reports without the name of the original creditor

### § 1022.42 — Reasonable policies and procedures

**(a) Requirement:** Each furnisher must establish and implement reasonable written policies and procedures regarding the accuracy and integrity of the information relating to consumers that it furnishes to a CRA.

**(b) Guidelines:** In developing its policies and procedures, each furnisher must consider the Interagency Guidelines for Furnisher Accuracy and Integrity set forth in Appendix E to Part 1022.

**(c) Specific elements the policies must address:**
1. Establishing and implementing a system for furnishing information to CRAs that is designed to ensure accuracy and integrity
2. Procedures for updating information it furnishes as necessary to reflect the current status of the consumer's account
3. Procedures for investigating disputes received from CRAs under § 1681s-2(b) in a timely manner
4. Procedures for investigating direct disputes under § 1022.43
5. Procedures for correcting and updating information found to be inaccurate or incomplete after investigation
6. Procedures for preventing re-furnishing of information determined to be inaccurate
7. Addressing identified systemic errors in furnishing

**Litigation relevance:** While § 1022.42 itself may not create a standalone private right of action (courts are split), the failure to maintain reasonable policies is powerful evidence of willfulness under § 1681n when proving a § 1681s-2(b) claim. Discovery requests targeting the furnisher's written policies and procedures are standard in NDGA FCRA cases. A furnisher that has no written policies, or that has written policies it systematically ignores, faces significant exposure for statutory and punitive damages.

**Key discovery targets flowing from § 1022.42:**
- Written furnisher policies and procedures manual
- Training materials for employees who investigate disputes
- Audit/compliance reports regarding furnishing accuracy
- Error logs and correction histories
- Internal metrics on dispute resolution rates and timelines

### § 1022.43 — Direct disputes

**(a) Scope:** A furnisher must conduct a reasonable investigation of a direct dispute if it relates to:
1. The consumer's liability for a credit account or other debt with the furnisher
2. The terms of a credit account or other debt with the furnisher
3. The consumer's performance or other conduct concerning an account or other relationship with the furnisher
4. Any other information contained in a consumer report regarding an account or other relationship with the furnisher that bears on the consumer's creditworthiness, credit standing, credit capacity, character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living

**(b) Exceptions (disputes the furnisher need NOT investigate):**
1. The consumer did not provide sufficient information to investigate
2. The dispute is frivolous or irrelevant (same standard as for CRA disputes under § 1681i(a)(3))
3. The dispute relates to the consumer's identifying information (name, address, SSN) unless from an identity-theft report
4. The furnisher has a reasonable belief that the dispute is submitted by a credit repair organization (as defined in 15 U.S.C. § 1679a(3))

**(c) Notice requirements for non-investigation:** If the furnisher determines it will not investigate, it must notify the consumer within the investigation period and explain why the direct dispute was determined to be frivolous, irrelevant, or otherwise exempt.

**(d) Investigation procedures:** The furnisher must:
1. Conduct a reasonable investigation with respect to the disputed information
2. Review all relevant information provided by the consumer
3. Complete the investigation within 30 days of receiving the dispute (may extend to 45 days if consumer provides additional information within the initial 30-day period)
4. Report the results to the consumer

**(e) Results of investigation:** If the investigation finds the disputed information is inaccurate, incomplete, or cannot be verified, the furnisher must:
1. Modify the information as appropriate
2. Delete the information as appropriate
3. Permanently block the reporting of the information as appropriate
4. Notify each CRA to which it furnished the information of the correction

**(f) Furnisher's obligations upon determining information is inaccurate:**
- Must promptly notify CRAs
- Must not re-furnish information found to be inaccurate
- Must update its records to reflect the correction

**Critical litigation point:** The direct dispute mechanism under § 1022.43 creates a private right of action under § 1681s-2(b). The Eleventh Circuit has held that a consumer's direct dispute to a furnisher triggers the § 1681s-2(b) duties just as effectively as a CRA-forwarded dispute (ACDV). See *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142, 149–50 (4th Cir. 2008) (widely followed). In the Northern District of Georgia, the direct dispute route is particularly valuable because:
- It puts the furnisher on direct notice of the specific inaccuracy
- It establishes the furnisher's actual knowledge (relevant to willfulness)
- It eliminates the CRA as an information bottleneck
- It preserves the consumer's detailed dispute letter as evidence
- The consumer controls the content and specificity of the dispute

### Appendix E — Interagency Guidelines for Furnisher Accuracy and Integrity

Appendix E provides the framework against which furnisher policies are measured. Key guidelines:

**I. Nature, Scope, and Objectives:**
- Policies must be appropriate to the nature, size, complexity, and scope of the furnisher's activities
- Must be designed to ensure the accuracy and integrity of information furnished

**II. Establishing and Implementing Policies and Procedures:**
The guidelines identify specific practices that furnishers should address:

1. **Technology controls:** Using standardized data reporting formats (Metro 2 format for credit reporting)
2. **Internal controls:** Implementing edit checks, validation routines, and quality control procedures
3. **Training:** Providing adequate training to personnel responsible for furnishing
4. **Monitoring:** Conducting periodic reviews and audits of furnished information
5. **Complaint tracking:** Maintaining records of consumer complaints and dispute outcomes
6. **Vendor management:** Overseeing service providers who furnish on the furnisher's behalf

**III. Specific Accuracy Requirements:**
- Furnishing information after it has been determined to be inaccurate (violates guidelines)
- Furnishing information without first establishing reasonable procedures to ensure accuracy
- Furnishing information that omits material qualifying information likely to affect a consumer's creditworthiness negatively (integrity violation)
- Furnishing information based on records that the furnisher knows or has reasonable cause to believe are outdated or incomplete

**IV. Responding to Direct Disputes:**
- Promptly investigating disputes
- Not requiring consumers to use a particular form or format for disputes
- Reviewing all information submitted by the consumer
- Reporting results within regulatory timeframes

**Litigation application of Appendix E:** In NDGA FCRA cases, Appendix E functions as the standard of care. A plaintiff can establish unreasonableness by showing that the furnisher's investigation deviated from Appendix E guidelines. Expert witnesses on credit reporting industry standards often testify by reference to Appendix E. The guidelines also support class certification by establishing common policies applicable to all class members.

## Subpart F — Duties of Users Regarding Obtaining and Using Consumer Reports (§§ 1022.50–1022.54)

### § 1022.54 — Duties of users making adverse action decisions

Implements FCRA § 1681m. When a user of a consumer report takes adverse action based in whole or in part on information in a consumer report, the user must provide the consumer with:

1. **Adverse action notice** containing:
   - Notice that adverse action was taken
   - The name, address, and telephone number of the CRA that furnished the report
   - A statement that the CRA did not make the adverse action decision and cannot explain why it was taken
   - A statement of the consumer's right to obtain a free copy of the report from the CRA within 60 days
   - A statement of the consumer's right to dispute accuracy or completeness of information in the report

2. **Credit score disclosure** (if a credit score was used):
   - The actual credit score used
   - The range of possible scores
   - Key factors (up to 4) that adversely affected the score
   - The date the score was created
   - The name of the entity that provided the score

**Litigation relevance:** Adverse action notice failures are common FCRA claims in NDGA, particularly in employment screening (background check) cases, auto lending denials, and insurance underwriting. The failure to provide adequate adverse action notices is often willful because the requirement is clear and well-established.

## Subpart H — Duties Regarding Risk-Based Pricing (§§ 1022.70–1022.75)

### § 1022.72 — General requirements for risk-based pricing notices

When a creditor uses a consumer report in connection with setting material terms of credit and the consumer receives terms materially less favorable than the most favorable terms available to a substantial proportion of consumers from that creditor, the creditor must provide a risk-based pricing notice.

**Content requirements:**
- Statement that a consumer report was used in setting the terms
- Name, address, and phone number of the CRA
- Statement that the CRA did not make the credit decision
- Consumer's right to obtain a free report within 60 days
- Consumer's right to dispute inaccurate information

**Exceptions:**
- § 1022.74(a): Credit score disclosure exception — creditor may provide a credit score disclosure to all applicants instead of identifying which received less favorable terms
- § 1022.74(d): Application for specific terms — when consumer applies for specific terms (e.g., specific advertised rate) and receives those terms

**Litigation relevance:** Risk-based pricing violations support FCRA claims in auto lending and credit card cases where consumers received higher rates without proper notice. These claims are increasingly paired with ECOA and UDAP claims in NDGA.

## Application to FCRA Litigation in NDGA

### How Regulation V Supports Complaint Allegations

**1. Establishing the furnisher's duty:** Cite § 1022.42 (reasonable policies) and § 1022.43 (direct dispute investigation) to establish that the defendant had specific regulatory duties beyond the general statutory text of § 1681s-2(b).

**2. Defining "reasonable investigation":** Regulation V's requirement of a "reasonable investigation" in § 1022.43(d) provides the standard against which the furnisher's conduct is measured. The Appendix E guidelines flesh out what "reasonable" means.

**3. Proving willfulness:** A furnisher that violates its own written policies (required by § 1022.42) demonstrates the "conscious disregard" or "unjustifiably high risk" standard for willfulness under *Safeco Ins. Co. of Am. v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007). Discovery of policies and deviations therefrom is critical.

**4. Accuracy and integrity standards:** The regulatory definitions of "accuracy" (§ 1022.41(a)) and "integrity" (§ 1022.41(d)) expand the universe of actionable inaccuracies beyond what a narrow reading of the statute might suggest. A tradeline can be "inaccurate" under Regulation V even if individual fields contain literally true data, if the overall presentation is materially misleading.

**5. Direct dispute as trigger:** § 1022.43 confirms that the consumer's direct dispute to the furnisher triggers investigation duties. This eliminates the defense that § 1681s-2(b) duties arise only upon CRA notification.

**6. Timeliness:** The 30-day investigation deadline in § 1022.43(d) provides a bright-line standard for timeliness claims.

**7. Remedial obligations:** § 1022.43(e)'s requirement to correct, delete, or permanently block inaccurate information — and to notify all CRAs — provides specific relief standards that the court can enforce.

### Key Regulatory Provisions for Specific Claim Types

| Claim Type | Regulation V Provisions |
|---|---|
| Failure to investigate | § 1022.43(d), Appendix E § IV |
| Inaccurate reporting | § 1022.41(a), § 1022.42(a) |
| Misleading/incomplete reporting | § 1022.41(d), Appendix E § III |
| Re-furnishing inaccurate info | § 1022.43(f), Appendix E § III.1 |
| No written policies | § 1022.42(a)–(b), Appendix E § II |
| Inadequate investigation | § 1022.43(d), Appendix E § IV |
| Failure to report dispute | § 1022.42(c)(2) (updating obligations) |
| Adverse action notice failure | § 1022.54, Appendix H (model forms) |
| Improper disposal | § 1022.21 |

### Eleventh Circuit and NDGA Interpretive Framework

Courts in the Northern District of Georgia and the Eleventh Circuit apply Regulation V as binding regulatory authority that gives content to the FCRA's statutory mandates. Key principles:

1. **Regulation V defines "reasonable"** — The Eleventh Circuit looks to Regulation V to determine whether a furnisher's investigation was "reasonable" under § 1681s-2(b)(1)(A). *Felts v. Wells Fargo*, 893 F.3d 1305 (11th Cir. 2018).

2. **Appendix E sets the standard of care** — District courts in NDGA regularly permit expert testimony that evaluates furnisher conduct against Appendix E guidelines.

3. **Direct disputes are fully actionable** — NDGA follows the majority rule that direct disputes under § 1022.43 trigger the full range of § 1681s-2(b) duties and remedies.

4. **Policy failures support willfulness** — A furnisher's failure to maintain required policies, or systematic deviation from its own policies, supports the "objectively unreasonable" willfulness standard from *Safeco*.

5. **Metro 2 compliance is relevant but not dispositive** — Technical compliance with Metro 2 format does not immunize a furnisher from accuracy claims if the information reported is substantively inaccurate or lacks integrity under § 1022.41.
