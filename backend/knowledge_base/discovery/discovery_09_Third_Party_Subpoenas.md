## 1. Rule 45 Mechanics and FCRA-Specific Constraints  
  
### 1.1 Notice and service  
  
Under Rule 45(a)(4), **before service** on the non-party, the issuing party must serve a notice and a copy of the subpoena on every other party. This requirement is non-waivable and routinely violated; opposing counsel should object to non-compliance.  
  
Service on the recipient must be in accordance with Rule 45(b). Many courts accept proof of service by certified mail or process server; some require personal service for compliance subpoenas.  
  
### 1.2 Permissible purpose under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(a)  
  
A consumer reporting agency (CRA) responding to a subpoena for a consumer report relies on the "court order or subpoena issued in connection with proceedings before a Federal grand jury" provision of § 1681b(a)(1), or — much more commonly in civil discovery — on (i) consumer written instructions under § 1681b(a)(2) or (ii) a court order. A pure Rule 45 subpoena, without consumer written instructions or a court order, may be refused by a CRA because § 1681b(a)(1) covers only orders of a court of competent jurisdiction and federal grand-jury subpoenas. *See Mone v. Dranow*, 945 F.2d 306 (9th Cir. 1991); *Stewart v. Credit Bureau, Inc.*, 734 F.2d 47 (D.C. Cir. 1984). Best practice: obtain a court order or the consumer's written authorization.  
  
### 1.3 Geographic limits — Rule 45(c)  
  
A subpoena may command a non-party to produce documents at a place within 100 miles of where the person resides, is employed, or regularly transacts business. Most CRAs and large furnishers will produce documents to a location near their corporate offices; counsel should accept production at the recipient's headquarters or by ESI transmission.  
  
### 1.4 Subpoena cost-shifting  
  
Rule 45(d)(1) requires the issuing party to take reasonable steps to avoid imposing undue burden or expense. CRAs and large furnishers routinely request cost reimbursement; defense counsel should negotiate costs in advance to preserve later sanction arguments.

## 2. Subpoenas to Non-Party CRAs  
  
When only one CRA is a defendant, the plaintiff's file at the other two nationwide CRAs (and at specialty CRAs) is often critical to:  
  
- alternate-cause analysis (the same tradeline appears at all three; was the same dispute submitted at all three?);  
- mixed-file evidence (whether the matching error was unique to defendant or systemic);  
- damages causation (whether a third-party lender pulled the defendant's report or another CRA's report);  
- assessment of "reasonable procedures" comparisons.  
  
### 2.1 Sample subpoena schedule — non-party CRA  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
> **Schedule A — Documents to be Produced by [Non-Party CRA] regarding [Consumer Name, DOB last four 19__, SSN last four ____]:**  
>  
> 1. The complete consumer disclosure file for the consumer, in any format maintained, for the period [date] through [date], including all archival, "purged," and "soft-deleted" data.  
> 2. All consumer reports issued concerning the consumer to any third party during the same period, including the recipient's identity, date, and stated permissible purpose.  
> 3. All dispute records, ACDV requests, ACDV responses, reinvestigation results, and "results of investigation" notices concerning the consumer.  
> 4. All correspondence (letters, emails, online-portal submissions) between the consumer (or anyone purporting to act on the consumer's behalf) and the CRA.  
> 5. All audit logs and access logs reflecting users who accessed, modified, or deleted any data in the consumer's file.  
> 6. All "frivolous" or "irrelevant" determinations under 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(3) concerning any dispute submitted by or on behalf of the consumer.  
> 7. All scoring records (FICO, VantageScore, Educational, or other) generated for the consumer during the period.  
> 8. All security freezes, fraud alerts, active-duty alerts, and identity-theft block records concerning the consumer.  
> 9. All "matching logic," "partial match," "near match," or similar algorithm output associated with the consumer's identifiers during the period.  
> 10. All "permissible purpose" certifications received from third parties that obtained consumer reports concerning the consumer during the period.  
  
### 2.2 Common objections  
  
- **Permissible purpose / FCRA.** Resolve with consumer authorization or court order. Defense counsel typically negotiates a stipulated authorization signed by the plaintiff.  
- **Overbreadth.** Tailor the temporal scope to the FCRA two-year/five-year statute of limitations under 15 U.S.C. § 1681p and the alleged damages period.  
- **Trade secret / confidentiality.** Address with a stipulated protective order (Rule 26(c)).  
- **Burden.** Most CRAs maintain dispute and disclosure files in standardized electronic formats; burden objections rarely succeed for routine production but may succeed for "all algorithm output" requests without further refinement.

## 3. Subpoenas to Creditors and Lenders That Denied Credit  
  
These subpoenas establish (or rebut) the damages claim that the alleged inaccuracy caused a denial or worse terms.  
  
### 3.1 Relevance  
  
Each denial is a discrete damages event. The defense must verify: (a) that the denial occurred; (b) which report the lender actually pulled; (c) the precise reason codes cited in the adverse-action notice; (d) whether the disputed item drove the decision or whether the lender would have denied based on non-disputed factors (e.g., DTI ratio, prior bankruptcy, income shortfall). Plaintiffs use these same subpoenas to prove the same elements affirmatively.  
  
### 3.2 Sample subpoena schedule — denying lender  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
> **Schedule A — Documents to be Produced by [Lender] regarding application of [Consumer Name, DOB last four 19__, SSN last four ____] dated [Date]:**  
>  
> 1. The complete application file, including the application, all underwriting notes, all internal credit-decision documents, the score-card output, and the final adverse-action notice.  
> 2. Every consumer report pulled in connection with the application, including a copy of the report as the lender received it, identification of the issuing CRA, and the date and time of the pull.  
> 3. The credit score(s) used in the decision and the corresponding score-cutoff or score-band criteria applicable to the requested product on the application date.  
> 4. The reason codes (and their narrative descriptions) cited in the adverse-action notice under 15 U.S.C. § 1681m(a) and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (Regulation B, 12 C.F.R. § 1002.9).  
> 5. The lender's written underwriting guidelines, score cutoffs, and overlay rules in effect on the application date for the product requested.  
> 6. All documents reflecting any subsequent reconsideration, appeal, or "second-look" review of the application.  
> 7. All approved applications from the consumer at the lender during the past five years and the terms of each.  
> 8. All documents reflecting any counteroffer made to the consumer and the terms thereof.  
> 9. All risk-based pricing notices issued to the consumer.  
> 10. All correspondence between the lender and the consumer concerning the application.  
  
### 3.3 Practice notes  
  
- Subpoenaing the lender for the **actual report** the lender received is critical because it captures what was disseminated to the third party at the time of the alleged injury — central to *TransUnion v. Ramirez* analysis.  
- Some lenders treat scoring as a trade secret; expect partial redactions and negotiate a protective order.

## 4. Subpoenas to Employers — Employment-Screening FCRA Cases  
  
In employment FCRA cases (improper disclosure under § 1681b(b); inaccuracy in employment-purpose reports), the employer's file is central.  
  
### 4.1 Sample subpoena schedule — employer  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
> **Schedule A — Documents to be Produced by [Employer] regarding [Consumer Name]:**  
>  
> 1. The complete personnel file, including the application, the offer letter, all background-check authorizations, and all separation documents.  
> 2. The complete background-check or consumer-report file, including the report as received from the consumer reporting agency.  
> 3. All "FCRA disclosure" and "FCRA authorization" forms signed by the consumer under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(2)(A).  
> 4. All "pre-adverse action" notices and "adverse action" notices issued to the consumer under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3).  
> 5. The "summary of rights" provided to the consumer with any pre-adverse action notice.  
> 6. All internal correspondence concerning the consumer's application, hire, or termination.  
> 7. The position description, qualifications, and any "matrix" applied to background-check results for the position at issue.  
> 8. All payroll records, performance reviews, and disciplinary records.  
> 9. Records reflecting the employer's contract with the consumer reporting agency and the certifications under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(f).  
> 10. Records reflecting any policy on use of background-check information in hiring decisions.  
  
### 4.2 Relevance and common objections  
  
- The "stand-alone disclosure" jurisprudence (e.g., *Walker v. Fred Meyer, Inc.*, 953 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 2020); *Syed v. M-I, LLC*, 853 F.3d 492 (9th Cir. 2017); *Long v. Se. Pa. Transp. Auth.*, 903 F.3d 312 (3d Cir. 2018)) makes the actual disclosure form pivotal.  
- Personnel-file objections (state law) are typical; the federal court usually orders production with a protective order.  
- Privacy concerns for co-workers' information are routinely addressed by redaction.

## 5. Subpoenas to Landlords and Property Managers — Tenant-Screening Cases  
  
Tenant-screening FCRA cases (e.g., *Smith v. HireRight Sols., Inc.*, 711 F. Supp. 2d 426 (E.D. Pa. 2010); *Williams v. First Advantage LNS Screening Sols., Inc.*, 947 F.3d 735 (11th Cir. 2020) — for procedures principles) often require subpoenas to the property manager that denied the rental.  
  
### 5.1 Sample subpoena schedule — landlord / property manager  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
> **Schedule A — Documents to be Produced by [Property Manager] regarding rental application of [Consumer Name]:**  
>  
> 1. The complete tenant file, including the application, all addenda, and all signed authorizations.  
> 2. The complete tenant-screening report received from any consumer reporting agency, including the report as delivered.  
> 3. All adverse-action notices issued to the consumer and the "summary of rights" provided therewith.  
> 4. The property's written tenant-screening criteria in effect on the application date.  
> 5. All correspondence between the property manager and the consumer.  
> 6. All correspondence between the property manager and the consumer reporting agency, including any account agreement and any "permissible purpose" certifications.  
> 7. All records reflecting subsequent rental of the property and the terms (rent, deposit, lease length).  
> 8. The unit-availability and waitlist log for the period [application date] through [date].  
> 9. Any "second-look" or "appeal" records concerning the consumer's application.  
> 10. Any records reflecting any other application by the consumer to any property managed by the recipient in the past five years.

## 6. Subpoenas to Credit Repair Organizations (Defense-Side)  
  
CROs are critical defense targets — they typically generate the boilerplate dispute correspondence that animates the litigation. Under 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43(b)(2), a furnisher may decline to investigate a direct dispute prepared by or on behalf of a CRO. A subpoena to the CRO often produces the very letters the plaintiff disclaims authorship of.  
  
### 6.1 Sample subpoena schedule — CRO  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
> **Schedule A — Documents to be Produced by [CRO Name] regarding services to [Consumer Name]:**  
>  
> 1. The complete client file for the consumer, including the engagement agreement, retainer, terms of service, and CROA disclosures under 15 U.S.C. § 1679c.  
> 2. All dispute letters, dispute forms, and online-portal submissions drafted, prepared, transmitted, or otherwise generated on behalf of the consumer.  
> 3. All templates, form letters, and "letter library" content from which any consumer-specific letter was generated.  
> 4. All communications between the consumer and the CRO (mail, email, portal messages, text, recorded calls).  
> 5. All communications between the CRO and any CRA or furnisher purporting to act on behalf of the consumer.  
> 6. All invoices, receipts, and payment records reflecting the consumer's engagement.  
> 7. All software logs reflecting auto-generated correspondence concerning the consumer.  
> 8. All marketing materials provided to the consumer.  
> 9. All complaints filed against the CRO with any state or federal regulator concerning the services provided to the consumer.  
> 10. The deposition transcript or testimony of any CRO employee in any prior FCRA or CROA matter, if accessible.  
  
### 6.2 Objections  
  
CROs frequently assert that records are "confidential" or "trade secret." There is no privilege protecting CRO-consumer communications — they are not attorney-client, and the work-product doctrine does not extend to non-litigation commercial dispute mills. *See Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495 (1947) (work-product doctrine limited to litigation). Move to compel.

## 7. Subpoenas to Medical and Mental-Health Providers — Limited, Contested  
  
Where the plaintiff claims emotional distress, the defense may seek records from treating providers. The scope is sharply circumscribed.  
  
### 7.1 Scope  
  
Following *Prado v. Equifax Info. Servs. LLC*, 331 F.R.D. 134 (N.D. Cal. 2019), and similar cases, the appropriate subpoena scope for a treating-provider records request is:  
  
- records that **specifically reference** the condition placed at issue (anxiety, sleep, depression, panic, etc.);  
- for the **temporal period from the first alleged inaccuracy** through the present;  
- with patient-controlled redaction for unrelated treatment.  
  
### 7.2 Sample subpoena schedule — treating provider  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
> **Schedule A — Documents to be Produced by [Provider] regarding [Patient Name, DOB, last four SSN]:**  
>  
> Limited to records dated [first alleged inaccuracy] through the present, and limited to records that reference any of: anxiety, depression, panic, sleep disturbance, insomnia, somatic complaint attributed to stress, or any condition for which the patient has alleged causation by the events giving rise to litigation styled [Case caption]:  
>  
> 1. Office and progress notes;  
> 2. Diagnostic codes (ICD-10, DSM-V) and treatment plans;  
> 3. Prescriptions and medication-administration records limited to medications prescribed for the conditions listed above;  
> 4. All intake and history forms reflecting reported causation of the conditions listed above;  
> 5. All referrals to and from other providers concerning the conditions listed above.  
  
### 7.3 Notice and authorizations  
  
HIPAA-qualified protective orders (45 C.F.R. § 164.512(e)) are typically required. Best practice: obtain a HIPAA-compliant authorization from the plaintiff and a federal court protective order before serving the subpoena.  
  
### 7.4 Authority  
  
- *Prado v. Equifax Info. Servs. LLC*, 331 F.R.D. 134 (N.D. Cal. 2019).  
- *Fitzgerald v. Cassil*, 216 F.R.D. 632 (N.D. Cal. 2003).  
- *Turner v. Imperial Stores*, 161 F.R.D. 89 (S.D. Cal. 1995).

## 8. Subpoenas to Prior Employers — Limited, Defense-Side  
  
Prior-employer subpoenas in non-employment FCRA cases are disfavored. They are typically appropriate only where (a) employment loss is part of the claimed damages, (b) the prior employer pulled the consumer report at issue, or (c) the consumer alleges prior identity-theft events of which the employer has records.  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
> **Schedule A — Limited Production by [Prior Employer]:**  
>  
> 1. The dates of the consumer's employment and the position held.  
> 2. The reason for separation, limited to documents memorializing the stated reason.  
> 3. Any consumer report pulled by the employer concerning the consumer, with permissible-purpose authorizations and adverse-action notices.  
> 4. Any documents reflecting any complaint by the consumer of identity theft, fraud, or credit-related issue during employment.

## 9. Subpoenas to AnnualCreditReport.com and CDIA  
  
### 9.1 AnnualCreditReport.com (operated by the Central Source LLC, a CDIA joint venture)  
  
A subpoena to the Central Source LLC can confirm whether and when a consumer obtained a free annual disclosure under 15 U.S.C. § 1681j. This evidence pins down (a) the consumer's actual notice of the alleged inaccuracy and (b) the start of the FCRA limitations clock under § 1681p ("the date of discovery by the plaintiff of the violation").  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
> Produce all records reflecting requests, deliveries, and access events by [Consumer Name, DOB, last four SSN] through annualcreditreport.com for the period [date] through [date], including the date and time of each request, the CRAs from which a disclosure was requested, the delivery method (online, mail, telephone), and any failed-authentication events.  
  
### 9.2 CDIA (Consumer Data Industry Association)  
  
Subpoenas to CDIA are rare but available for industry-standard records: Metro 2 reporting format documentation, e-OSCAR system specifications, and industry guidance. CDIA typically opposes on relevance grounds; the issuing party must show a particularized need beyond what is publicly available in the Credit Reporting Resource Guide.

## 10. Subpoenas to the CFPB — Rare and Procedurally Heavy  
  
A subpoena to the CFPB for consumer-complaint records is procedurally difficult. The CFPB's Touhy regulations at 12 C.F.R. §§ 1070.30–.34 govern. Key features:  
  
- All subpoenas for testimony or documents must be directed to the General Counsel.  
- The CFPB may decline to produce based on a defined set of factors (mission impairment, deliberative-process, ongoing supervisory or enforcement interest, etc.).  
- If the CFPB declines, judicial review is available only under the APA (because the CFPB is not a "party" to the underlying case). *See United States ex rel. Touhy v. Ragen*, 340 U.S. 462 (1951); *In re Bankers Trust Co.*, 61 F.3d 465 (6th Cir. 1995); *COMSAT Corp. v. Nat'l Sci. Found.*, 190 F.3d 269 (4th Cir. 1999).  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE — Touhy request letter (NOT a subpoena alone)**  
  
> Pursuant to 12 C.F.R. § 1070.32, the undersigned requests that the Bureau produce non-public records concerning consumer complaints filed by or against [parties] for the period [date] through [date]. The requested records are described in the attached schedule. [State summary of the case, the relevance of the records, why the records cannot reasonably be obtained from another source, and the limited use to which they will be put.]  
  
Defense counsel typically pairs the Touhy letter with a Rule 45 subpoena, then negotiates with the General Counsel's office.

## 11. FTC Touhy Regulations  
  
The Federal Trade Commission's Touhy regulations are codified at 16 C.F.R. § 4.11(e). They govern:  
  
- Demands on FTC personnel for testimony or records concerning non-public information.  
- The General Counsel's authority to authorize disclosure.  
- The factors weighed: the public interest, conservation of agency resources, demands of fair administration of justice, and statutory restrictions.  
  
**Sample combined letter / subpoena structure:**  
  
1. **Touhy request letter** to FTC General Counsel under 16 C.F.R. § 4.11(e) describing the case, the records sought, and the justifications.  
2. **Rule 45 subpoena** to the Custodian of Records at the FTC, with notice to all parties under Rule 45(a)(4).  
3. **Protective order proposal** for any non-public information that the FTC may produce.  
  
Note: a Rule 45 subpoena alone, without the predicate Touhy letter, will be refused. *See Houston Bus. Journal, Inc. v. Office of Comptroller of Currency*, 86 F.3d 1208 (D.C. Cir. 1996).

## 12. Cross-Cutting Procedural Issues  
  
### 12.1 Rule 45(a)(4) notice  
  
Every Rule 45 subpoena for documents in FCRA litigation must be served on all parties **before** service on the non-party recipient. Opposing counsel should track all subpoenas and object promptly to defective notice — many courts treat compliance with Rule 45(a)(4) as a prerequisite to enforcing the subpoena.  
  
### 12.2 Privilege logs and inadvertent production  
  
Non-party recipients are unlikely to maintain privilege logs of their own. The issuing party should be prepared to address claw-back under Fed. R. Evid. 502(d) and Rule 26(b)(5)(B) when CROs, prior-employer counsel, or treating providers inadvertently produce privileged material.  
  
### 12.3 Motions to quash — Rule 45(d)(3)  
  
Common bases:  
  
- Failure to allow reasonable time to comply.  
- Requires non-party to travel beyond Rule 45(c) limits.  
- Requires disclosure of privileged or protected matter.  
- Subjects person to undue burden.  
  
Standing to move to quash typically belongs to the recipient. A party has standing to move to quash a subpoena to a non-party only when the party claims a personal right or privilege in the records — common when the plaintiff seeks to quash a defense subpoena to her treating physician or to a CRO. *See In re REMEC, Inc. Sec. Litig.*, 2008 WL 2282647 (S.D. Cal. 2008); *Hertenstein v. Kimberly Home Health Care, Inc.*, 189 F.R.D. 620 (D. Kan. 1999).  
  
### 12.4 Protective orders  
  
Almost every FCRA case warrants a stipulated protective order before subpoenas issue. Key categories:  
  
- "Confidential" — standard restriction.  
- "Confidential — Attorney Eyes Only" — for trade-secret matching algorithms, CRA processing logic, lender underwriting rules.  
- "Highly Confidential — Source Code" — rarely invoked but should be addressed for any algorithm code production.  
  
A HIPAA-qualified protective order under 45 C.F.R. § 164.512(e) is required for medical-records subpoenas; the plaintiff's signed HIPAA authorization is the cleaner mechanism.  
  
### 12.5 Cost-shifting under Rule 45(d)(2)(B)(ii)  
  
For burdensome non-party productions, the court may shift "significant" costs to the issuing party. Defense counsel should pre-negotiate reasonable-cost reimbursement to avoid later disputes.

## 13. Scope and Timing Best Practices  
  
1. **Sequence party discovery first.** Most courts require an attempt to obtain documents from a party before resorting to non-party subpoenas, especially for the same records.  
2. **Tailor temporal scope** to the limitations period (15 U.S.C. § 1681p — two years from discovery / five years from violation) and the alleged damages period.  
3. **Use the consumer's HIPAA and FCRA authorizations** to avoid permissible-purpose objections.  
4. **Coordinate subpoenas to the same recipient** (e.g., do not serve three sequential subpoenas on Experian; serve one comprehensive subpoena and meet and confer).  
5. **Preserve costs** for an eventual fee application or Rule 11 motion if the subpoena uncovers fabricated dispute history (a common defense outcome in CRO-driven litigation).

## 14. Common Objections and Defense Responses  
  
| Objection | Typical Response |  
|---|---|  
| "Permissible purpose" (CRA) | Provide consumer authorization or court order. |  
| "Overbreadth — all consumer files" | Tailor to identifier last-four, temporal scope, and disputed accounts. |  
| "Trade secret — matching logic" | Negotiate AEO protective order. |  
| "Personnel-file privilege (state law)" | Federal-law subpoena; state-law privileges yield in federal-question cases. |  
| "HIPAA / medical privacy" | Limit scope per *Prado*; HIPAA-qualified protective order; authorization. |  
| "Touhy — no agency consent" | File Touhy letter; if denied, APA review. |  
| "Lack of notice under Rule 45(a)(4)" | Re-issue with proper notice; cure is usually permitted. |  
| "Undue burden / cost" | Pre-negotiate cost reimbursement; offer narrowed schedule. |  
| "Privilege — work product" | Argue non-party CRO is not a litigation actor; no work-product privilege attaches. |
