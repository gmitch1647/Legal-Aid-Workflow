# File 21 — Defense-Side Deposition of the Consumer Plaintiff  
  
**Topic:** Strategy and outlines for the defense-side deposition of the consumer plaintiff in an FCRA action — and, from the plaintiff side, how to prepare and defend that deposition.  
  
**Primary FRCP rules:** 26 (scope), 30 (depositions), 30(c) (objections), 30(d) (duration; sanctions), 30(e) (review and errata), 32 (use at trial), 37 (sanctions).  
  
**Primary FCRA / Reg V cross-references:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681i (consumer disputes); § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duties); § 1681n / § 1681o (damages); § 1681o(a)(1) (actual damages); 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43(b)(2) (exclusion of certain credit-repair-organization disputes from direct-dispute regime).  
  
**Federal Rules of Evidence:** 401–403 (relevance), 404(b) (other acts), 608, 613 (impeachment), 612 (refreshing recollection), 801 (hearsay), 901 (authentication).  
  
**Key cases cited:** *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021); *Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins*, 578 U.S. 330 (2016); *Safeco Ins. Co. v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007); *Sloane v. Equifax Information Services*, 510 F.3d 495 (4th Cir. 2007); *Cousin v. Trans Union Corp.*, 246 F.3d 359 (5th Cir. 2001); *Bach v. First Union Nat. Bank*, 149 F. App'x 354 (6th Cir. 2005); *Wantz v. Experian*, 386 F.3d 829 (7th Cir. 2004); *Bryant v. TRW*, 689 F.2d 72 (6th Cir. 1982); *Hall v. Clifton Precision*, 150 F.R.D. 525 (E.D. Pa. 1993).  
  
**Last updated:** 2026-05-16

## Overview  
  
The plaintiff's deposition is the single most important event for the defense in an FCRA case. The deposition typically determines (i) the credibility of the damages narrative, (ii) the standing posture under *TransUnion v. Ramirez*, (iii) the strength of the dispute history (and whether procedural prerequisites under § 1681i and § 1681s-2(b) are met), and (iv) whether a credit repair organization is the real driver of the dispute (with downstream Reg V § 1022.43(b)(2) implications). For plaintiff's counsel, preparing the client and defending the deposition skillfully is equally critical.  
  
This file is written primarily from the defense perspective with parallel notes for plaintiff's counsel.  
  
---  
  
## A. Pre-Deposition Discovery Review  
  
Before the deposition, the defense should assemble and cross-reference:  
  
- Plaintiff's interrogatory responses (especially those describing damages, dispute history, and prior credit problems).  
- Plaintiff's document production: every dispute letter, every credit report, every denial notice, every CRO contract, every medical record (if emotional distress is claimed and authorizations were obtained).  
- Plaintiff's tax returns, W-2s, pay stubs, and any business records (if economic damages claimed).  
- Credit reports from all three nationwide CRAs spanning a window (typically 3–7 years pre- and post-event).  
- Bank and credit card statements (if applicable to the alleged tradeline).  
- Social media review (public posts only) on emotional distress and lifestyle.  
- Public records: prior bankruptcies, prior FCRA lawsuits, prior consumer protection lawsuits.  
- The PACER docket on plaintiff's prior litigation.  
- Authorizations executed by plaintiff (employment, medical, financial).  
  
Cross-reference inconsistencies between interrogatory answers, dispute letter narratives, and credit report data. Identify gaps in the timeline. Build a master chronology with citations to source documents.  
  
Identify possible **credit-repair-organization involvement**: form-letter language, paid-service receipts, identical phrasing across multiple disputes, payment to a third party in the bank records, references to a "credit specialist."

## B. Deposition Outline Structure  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Plaintiff's Deposition: Master Outline  
  
I. Preliminaries and Background (30 min)  
II. Education, Employment, Financial Sophistication (30 min)  
III. Pre-Event Credit History (30 min)  
IV. Credit Repair Organization Inquiry (30 min)  
V. The Disputed Item: Factual Narrative (60 min)  
VI. Dispute History (60 min)  
VII. Damages — Out of Pocket (20 min)  
VIII. Damages — Credit Denials (30 min)  
IX. Damages — Emotional Distress (45 min)  
X. Damages — Lost Opportunities (20 min)  
XI. Standing — TransUnion v. Ramirez Analysis (45 min)  
XII. Mitigation (15 min)  
XIII. Closing (15 min)  
```  
  
### B.1 Preliminaries and Background  
  
- Confirm name, all prior names, all addresses for 7–10 years, SSN (off-record on confidentiality protocols), date of birth.  
- Walk through residences chronologically — household members, mortgage/rent payer.  
- Marital history and children.  
- Identification of relatives with similar names (mixed-file precursor).  
  
### B.2 Education, Employment, Financial Sophistication  
  
- Educational background and degrees.  
- Current employment, prior employers (10 years).  
- Job duties — particularly anything involving finance, credit, banking, collections, or consumer reporting.  
- Income history.  
- Use of credit: number of accounts open, types, history of late payments, prior bankruptcies, prior collections.  
- Self-rated financial sophistication.  
  
### B.3 Pre-Event Credit History  
  
- The condition of plaintiff's credit *before* the alleged inaccuracy. Critical for both standing and damages: a plaintiff with poor pre-event credit faces challenges showing that the disputed item *caused* harm. *See Wantz v. Experian Information Solutions*, 386 F.3d 829 (7th Cir. 2004) (lack of evidence that disputed item caused denial supports defense judgment).  
- Prior derogatories, late payments, charge-offs, collections, bankruptcies.  
- Existing FICO range and how known.  
- Prior dispute history (separate disputes for other items).  
- Identity-theft history.  
  
### B.4 Credit-Repair-Organization Inquiry  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — CRO Discovery Questions  
  
Q. Have you ever used a credit repair service or company?  
Q. Which one(s)?  
Q. When did you sign up?  
Q. How much did you pay?  
Q. Are you still a customer?  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 14 — is this a contract with [CRO]?  
Q. What did [CRO] do for you?  
Q. Did [CRO] draft any of the dispute letters that were sent in  
 your name?  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 20 — this dispute letter dated June 10, 2024.  
 Did you draft this letter?  
Q. Did you sign it?  
Q. Where did the language in this letter come from?  
Q. Did [CRO] tell you what to write?  
Q. Did [CRO] mail the letter for you?  
Q. Did [CRO] give you any training on disputing tradelines?  
Q. Did [CRO] tell you to dispute accurate information?  
```  
  
This sequence has both substantive and impeachment value. Under 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43(b)(2), direct disputes "submitted by, or prepared on behalf of, the consumer by a credit repair organization" or similar third party are excluded from the furnisher's mandatory direct-dispute investigation regime — defense leverage if applicable. (Note: this exclusion applies to *direct disputes to furnishers*; CRA disputes routed through § 1681i still trigger furnisher duties under § 1681s-2(b) when the CRA forwards them.)  
  
### B.5 The Disputed Item: Factual Narrative  
  
- Origin of the account or item. Did plaintiff open the account? When? Where?  
- All communications about the account before any dispute.  
- First awareness that the item was on the credit report.  
- Plaintiff's specific factual claim: the item is wrong because ___.  
- Documentation supporting that claim.  
- Who else knows the underlying facts (witnesses).  
  
### B.6 Dispute History  
  
Every dispute: letter or online portal or phone call. For each:  
- Date sent and method.  
- Recipient.  
- Whether plaintiff drafted it.  
- Attachments included.  
- Response received.  
- Plaintiff's reaction to response.  
  
Pin the plaintiff to a precise factual account that can be tested against produced records.

## C. Damages Questioning  
  
### C.1 Emotional Distress  
  
Emotional-distress damages under § 1681o(a)(1) require some showing of genuine harm. The Fourth Circuit's framework in *Sloane v. Equifax*, 510 F.3d 495 (4th Cir. 2007), is the leading articulation: courts look at the duration and severity of distress, corroborating evidence, and the nexus to the FCRA violation. Earlier cases such as *Bryant v. TRW*, 689 F.2d 72 (6th Cir. 1982), and *Cousin v. Trans Union*, 246 F.3d 359 (5th Cir. 2001), establish that emotional distress is recoverable but generalized claims are insufficient.  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Emotional Distress Sequence  
  
Q. You claim that the inaccurate credit report caused you emotional  
 distress, correct?  
Q. Describe that distress.  
Q. When did it start?  
Q. When did it end? Or is it ongoing?  
Q. How often do you experience it?  
Q. Have you sought any professional treatment for this distress?  
Q. Have you seen a doctor, therapist, counselor, psychiatrist,  
 psychologist?  
Q. Have you taken any medication?  
Q. Have you told anyone about the distress — family, friends,  
 coworkers, clergy?  
Q. Who? When?  
Q. Aside from this credit report situation, have you experienced  
 any other stressful events during this period? (death,  
 divorce, job loss, medical issues)  
Q. Have you ever been diagnosed with any anxiety or depression  
 disorder?  
Q. Before this credit report situation, had you ever experienced  
 emotional distress?  
Q. What were the symptoms then?  
Q. How do you distinguish that prior distress from the current  
 distress?  
```  
  
The questions identify (i) duration and severity, (ii) corroborating witnesses and treatment, (iii) confounding stressors that may explain the distress without the FCRA violation, and (iv) pre-existing conditions that complicate causation.  
  
### C.2 Credit Denials  
  
For each claimed denial:  
- Date.  
- Lender.  
- Product applied for (mortgage, auto loan, credit card, etc.).  
- Stated reason on adverse action notice.  
- Credit score communicated.  
- The credit report used (which CRA, what date).  
- Whether the disputed item appears in that report and whether the adverse action notice references it.  
- Application terms — was the loan one plaintiff could afford on the merits?  
  
A plaintiff claiming a denial caused by a disputed tradeline must connect the dots. *Wantz v. Experian*, 386 F.3d 829 (7th Cir. 2004), and similar cases reject damages claims where the plaintiff fails to tie the alleged inaccuracy to the actual lending decision.  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Credit Denial Sequence  
  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 32, an adverse action notice from [Bank] dated  
 March 15, 2024. Is this a denial you're claiming as damages?  
Q. Identify the stated reasons on the notice.  
Q. The notice lists "delinquent past or present credit obligations  
 with others" — does it list anything about [the disputed item]?  
Q. Which credit report did [Bank] use?  
Q. What was your score at the time?  
Q. If [the disputed item] had not been on your report, what would  
 your score have been?  
Q. Do you know?  
Q. Did [Bank] tell you that the loan would have been approved if  
 not for [the disputed item]?  
Q. Did you reapply elsewhere?  
Q. What was the outcome?  
```  
  
### C.3 Lost Opportunities  
  
- Jobs lost or not obtained. Employer name, position, application date, stated reasons, plaintiff's qualification.  
- Housing denials. Landlord, property, application date, screening process.  
- Business deals. Counterparty, terms, lost profit calculation.  
- Insurance: policies declined, premium increases.  
  
### C.4 Out-of-Pocket  
  
- Postage, certified-mail fees.  
- Phone calls.  
- Time spent (with rough hours per task).  
- Mileage to and from disputes, lenders.  
- Credit-monitoring subscriptions.  
- Attorney fees pre-suit (often non-recoverable as damages but still asked).  
- CRO fees.  
  
### C.5 Mitigation  
  
- What did plaintiff do to fix the problem?  
- Disputes (already covered).  
- Alternative credit applications.  
- Identity-theft remediation (if applicable).  
- Sworn statements, police reports, FTC affidavits.

## D. Standing Questioning Post-*TransUnion v. Ramirez*  
  
In *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), the Supreme Court held that to recover damages under Article III, a plaintiff must show a concrete injury — and that the mere existence of an inaccurate credit file, *without dissemination to a third party*, generally does not suffice. The case dramatically changed FCRA litigation: defense counsel routinely test standing at deposition.  
  
### D.1 Dissemination  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Dissemination Sequence  
  
Q. Do you know whether your credit report containing [the  
 disputed item] was ever provided to anyone outside the CRA?  
Q. What's the basis for that belief?  
Q. Identify each entity that you contend received your credit  
 report containing [the disputed item].  
Q. For each, on what date?  
Q. What is your evidence that they received the report?  
Q. Did anyone at [Lender] tell you they had seen [the disputed  
 item]?  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 36, your credit report inquiry log. The  
 inquiries listed here — do these represent each time the  
 report was sent to a third party?  
```  
  
### D.2 Concrete Harm Tied to Dissemination  
  
The Court in *TransUnion* distinguished between class members whose inaccurate reports *were* disseminated (who had standing) and those whose reports *were not* disseminated (who did not). The deposition should probe concrete consequences flowing from each instance of dissemination.  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Concrete-Harm Sequence  
  
Q. You testified that your report was sent to [Lender] on  
 May 1, 2024.  
Q. What specific harm did you experience from that transmission?  
Q. Was credit denied?  
Q. Was credit offered on worse terms?  
Q. Did anyone at [Lender] discuss [the disputed item] with you?  
Q. Were you embarrassed or humiliated?  
Q. With whom?  
Q. Were you turned away in person? Did your spouse / coworker /  
 family member see this happen?  
```  
  
### D.3 Distinguishing Risk-of-Harm From Materialized Harm  
  
Under *TransUnion*, the mere *risk* of future harm does not support damages standing. Probe whether each claimed injury actually materialized.  
  
```  
Q. You testified that you were worried the report might affect  
 future credit decisions. Has it?  
Q. Are you describing a fear of what might happen, or an actual  
 consequence that did happen?  
```

## E. Credit-Repair-Organization Discovery During Deposition  
  
Many FCRA dispute letters are drafted by credit-repair organizations (CROs). Discovery of CRO involvement is important because:  
  
1. **Reg V § 1022.43(b)(2)** permits a furnisher to disregard a *direct* dispute (i.e., one sent directly to the furnisher) that is "submitted by, or prepared on behalf of, the consumer by a credit repair organization, as defined in 15 U.S.C. § 1679a(3), or an entity that would be a credit repair organization, but for [the statutory exclusions]." A dispute prepared by a CRO and sent directly to the furnisher may fall outside § 1022.43 — although disputes that travel through a CRA under § 1681i and § 1681s-2(b) generally remain in the FCRA dispute regime.  
2. **Credibility.** A plaintiff who hires a CRO to dispute accurate information undermines emotional-distress claims.  
3. **Damages causation.** If CRO fees are claimed as damages, the defense will challenge them as voluntary and unrelated to the alleged FCRA violation.  
4. **Volume disputes.** A pattern of mass disputes orchestrated by a CRO is relevant to "frivolous" determinations.  
  
Build the CRO record carefully:  
- Contract and engagement documents.  
- Fee receipts and payment records.  
- Sample dispute letters with shared language.  
- Communications between plaintiff and CRO.  
- Authorizations granted to the CRO.

## F. Common Plaintiff Witness Traps  
  
### F.1 Inconsistencies Between Deposition and Interrogatory Responses  
  
Mark each verified interrogatory response. When deposition testimony diverges, pin down the inconsistency:  
  
```  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 5, your interrogatory response number 7,  
 verified by you on January 12, 2026. You testified — sorry,  
 you stated — that the dispute letter of June 10 was drafted  
 by you personally. Do you see that?  
Q. Just now you told me that [CRO] drafted that letter. Which  
 is correct?  
```  
  
### F.2 Overstating Damages  
  
```  
Q. You said earlier that your emotional distress was "constant  
 and unbearable." Was it constant — meaning 24 hours a day —  
 or did it come and go?  
Q. When you went to your daughter's wedding in July 2024, were  
 you experiencing constant unbearable distress?  
```  
  
Social media posts showing the plaintiff at celebratory or normal life events are classic impeachment material. (Plaintiff's counsel should prepare the client for this.)  
  
### F.3 Selective Memory ("I Don't Recall" Patterns)  
  
Track patterns: vivid recall of harm-causing details, no recall of corroborative or contradictory details.  
  
```  
Q. You don't recall the name of the lender who denied your  
 application?  
Q. But you do recall the precise emotional distress you felt  
 that day, in detail?  
Q. How is it that one recollection is so clear and the other  
 is missing?  
```  
  
### F.4 Volunteering Information  
  
Plaintiff witnesses prone to volunteering broaden the scope of follow-up. Defense exploits this; plaintiff's counsel must prepare the client to answer the question asked and stop. (Counter-rule for plaintiff prep: "If your one-word answer is responsive, give the one-word answer. If a number is asked for, give the number. Do not explain unless asked.")

## G. Common Plaintiff Lawyer Objections (Defense View)  
  
### G.1 Privilege  
  
- Attorney-client: communications with counsel are privileged in substance. Existence, dates, and participants are not.  
- Work product (Rule 26(b)(3)): materials prepared in anticipation of litigation. CRO-prepared materials are *not* attorney work product.  
  
### G.2 Form  
  
- Compound: ask one at a time.  
- Vague: rephrase with specificity.  
- Calls for legal conclusion: rephrase as a factual or operational question.  
- Assumes facts not in evidence: re-anchor in the record.  
  
### G.3 Asked-and-Answered  
  
Generally not a valid objection in deposition — Rule 30(c)(2) allows form objections only. Even when stated, the witness must still answer. Some plaintiff counsel use this as a coaching cue; defense should note for the record and move on.  
  
### G.4 Beyond Scope  
  
A deposition is broader in scope than topics in a Rule 30(b)(6) notice or trial — Rule 26(b)(1) defines the limits. For a fact-witness deposition of an individual party, the scope is the entire scope of discovery (proportional to needs of the case).

## H. Plaintiff's Counsel: Defending the Deposition  
  
### H.1 Pre-Deposition Preparation of the Client  
  
A typical client prep covers:  
  
- Mechanics of a deposition; oath; court reporter; video.  
- Listen to the question; answer the question; if you don't understand, say so; if you don't know, say so; if you don't remember, say so.  
- "I don't recall" is acceptable when truthful, but exhaust your memory before saying it.  
- Do not guess.  
- Do not volunteer.  
- Take a breath before answering — your lawyer needs time to object.  
- Ask for breaks when needed; but breaks during a pending question are limited.  
- Review key documents in advance with counsel.  
- Walk through likely topics.  
  
### H.2 Coaching Limits — Rule 30(c) and *Hall v. Clifton Precision*  
  
The leading case is *Hall v. Clifton Precision*, 150 F.R.D. 525 (E.D. Pa. 1993), which articulated:  
  
- No private conferences between witness and counsel during examination except to determine privilege.  
- No coaching through speaking objections.  
- Witnesses may not confer with counsel during the questioning except on privilege.  
  
Cases such as *In re Stratosphere Corp. Securities Litigation*, 182 F.R.D. 614 (D. Nev. 1998), and *Plaisted v. Geisinger Medical Center*, 210 F.R.D. 527 (M.D. Pa. 2002), apply similar principles. Sanctions under Rule 30(d)(2) are available against counsel who "impede, delay, or frustrate the fair examination."  
  
### H.3 Speaking Objections — Appropriate Use  
  
The narrow legitimate uses for any verbal contribution beyond "object to form":  
  
- Asserting a privilege (with sufficient specificity).  
- Instructing the witness not to answer to preserve privilege (Rule 30(c)(2)).  
- Identifying the basis for a Rule 30(d)(3) motion to terminate.  
  
Plaintiff's counsel should not (i) re-cast the question for the witness, (ii) explain to the witness what the question "really means," or (iii) suggest the answer.  
  
### H.4 When to Break  
  
- Routine breaks: every 60–90 minutes, by agreement.  
- For privilege: necessary and proper.  
- During a pending question: generally not permitted.  
  
When a witness is exhausted or visibly struggling, counsel may request a break — but the question must usually be answered first.  
  
### H.5 Errata Strategy  
  
Under Rule 30(e), the witness has 30 days to review the transcript and submit changes in form or substance with reasons. Substantive changes ("yes" to "no") create impeachment opportunities — courts split on the legal effect. Plaintiff's counsel should reserve reading and signing routinely and review the transcript with the client carefully, but limit substantive changes to genuine corrections.

## I. Sample Question Sequences  
  
### I.1 Getting Damages Admissions  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Damages Admission Sequence  
  
Q. You're claiming emotional-distress damages, correct?  
Q. Total amount?  
Q. How did you arrive at that number?  
Q. Did you talk to a doctor about that distress?  
Q. Did you take medication?  
Q. Did you miss work?  
Q. Did you suffer any physical symptoms?  
Q. Tell me, in order of severity, the three worst moments of  
 distress you experienced.  
Q. For each — date, location, who else was present, what  
 happened.  
Q. Have you told anyone — friend, family, therapist — about  
 each of those three moments?  
Q. Who?  
Q. When?  
Q. Are you willing to have your therapist or doctor testify  
 about your distress?  
```  
  
### I.2 Getting Timeline Admissions  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Timeline Admission Sequence  
  
Q. When did you first learn that the disputed item was on your  
 credit report?  
Q. How?  
Q. What date did you send your first dispute letter?  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 12, the dispute letter. Is this it?  
Q. What date did you receive the response?  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 13. Is this it?  
Q. What did you do after receiving this response?  
Q. Walk me through each subsequent dispute in chronological order.  
```  
  
### I.3 Getting Standing Concessions  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Standing Concession Sequence  
  
Q. Apart from credit reports you obtained yourself, can you  
 identify any third party that received the disputed report?  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 18, the inquiries list. The hard inquiries  
 here — those represent applications you made, correct?  
Q. Did any of those applications result in denial?  
Q. For each denial, do you know whether the disputed item was  
 a stated reason?  
Q. If you cannot identify a specific person at the lender who  
 saw the disputed item, what is your evidence that anyone  
 saw it?  
```  
  
### I.4 Drawing Out CRO Involvement  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — CRO Involvement Sequence  
  
Q. Have you ever paid anyone to help with your credit?  
Q. Who?  
Q. When?  
Q. How much?  
Q. Refer to Exhibit 25, your bank statement from June 2024 —  
 line item showing $99.00 to [CRO]. Is this the company you  
 used?  
Q. What did they do?  
Q. Did they draft dispute letters for you?  
Q. Did they mail them for you?  
Q. Did they tell you which accounts to dispute?  
Q. Did they ever tell you to dispute accurate information?  
```

## Summary Notes for Plaintiff's Counsel  
  
- Prepare the client to answer the question asked — period.  
- Build the standing record affirmatively: identify each act of dissemination, each concrete consequence, each third-party witness.  
- Document emotional-distress damages with corroborating witnesses and (where appropriate) professional treatment well before the deposition.  
- Avoid CRO involvement entirely if possible; if a CRO has been used, get the documents and the story straight before the deposition.  
- Defend the deposition cleanly — privilege only, no speaking objections, exhaustive prep instead.  
  
## Summary Notes for Defense Counsel  
  
- Pre-deposition document mastery is decisive.  
- Open with rapport, but close with the standing, CRO, and damages sequences once trust is established.  
- Use *TransUnion v. Ramirez* dissemination questions in every deposition.  
- Probe pre-event credit, confounding stressors, and prior litigation to defeat causation and quantum.  
- Make a clean record for summary judgment on standing and damages.
