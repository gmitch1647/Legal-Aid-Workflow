## Overview  
  
Pre-suit "informal" discovery is the most overlooked phase of an FCRA case. Long before a complaint is filed and a Rule 26(f) conference occurs, the consumer and her counsel can build the evidentiary foundation of the case through statutory dispute and disclosure rights, FOIA and state public-records requests, public CFPB and FTC databases, and the identity-theft remediation process. Done well, pre-suit work yields three benefits:  
  
1. **Element-by-element evidence.** A § 1681i dispute letter, an ACDV demand, and a follow-up disclosure request together establish the existence of a dispute, the inaccuracy, the CRA's response, and (often) the furnisher's "verification" — the entire reinvestigation claim under § 1681i.  
2. **Willfulness/recklessness.** Under *Safeco*, willfulness includes reckless disregard of an objectively reasonable reading of the statute. Pre-suit notice, CFPB complaints, and prior consent orders demonstrate that the defendant knew or should have known its procedures were deficient.  
3. **Settlement leverage.** A complete pre-suit file lets counsel make a documented demand and price the case before the burdens of formal discovery shift the cost calculus.  
  
This file walks through each pre-suit tool and provides template language. Source discipline: every authority cited is public-domain primary law, court opinion, agency rule, or agency guidance.

## A. The *Cushman* Dispute Letter as Evidence-Building  
  
### 1. Why *Cushman* matters  
  
In *Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.*, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997), the Third Circuit reversed summary judgment for a CRA on a § 1681i claim and articulated what is still the leading framework for "reasonable reinvestigation":  
  
> "[T]he scope of the agency's duty to reinvestigate depends upon (1) the cost of verifying the accuracy of the source versus the potential harm to the consumer; and (2) the extent of the information the credit reporting agency possesses…. Once the credit reporting agency receives notice from the consumer that the credit report is inaccurate, it may be required to conduct a more thorough investigation, one that requires it to make inquiries beyond the original source of the information."  
  
*Cushman* also held that punitive damages are available where the CRA "adopted its reinvestigation policy either knowing that policy to be in contravention of the rights possessed by consumers pursuant to the FCRA or in reckless disregard of whether the policy contravened those rights." That punitive-damages standard is now read together with *Safeco*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007).  
  
The practical takeaway: the more information and supporting evidence a consumer puts into the dispute letter, the more the CRA's duty expands — and the harder it is for the CRA to claim its rote "parrot the furnisher" reinvestigation was reasonable.  
  
### 2. Elements of a *Cushman*-style § 1681i dispute letter  
  
A dispute letter intended to support later litigation should contain:  
  
- **Clear identification of the consumer.** Full legal name, current address, prior addresses for the last two years, date of birth, last four of SSN. This forecloses any "matching" or identity-confusion defense.  
- **Specific identification of each disputed item.** Tradeline name, partial account number, balance, status, and the exact factual basis for the dispute ("not mine," "paid as agreed," "fraudulent — see police report"). Avoid generic "dispute all" language.  
- **Attached supporting evidence.** Cancelled checks, prior payoff letters, account-closure letters, identity-theft report, court orders of discharge, prior CRA disclosures showing the disputed item, etc.  
- **Express invocation of § 1681i.** Identify the statute. Demand reinvestigation, deletion if not verified, and the § 1681i(a)(6) notice of results.  
- **Demand for the ACDV record.** Cite § 1681i(a)(6)(B)(iii) and request the description of the procedure used to determine the accuracy. Demand the ACDV transmission record and any communication with the furnisher.  
- **Demand for disclosure under § 1681g.** Request the consumer's full file as it existed at the time of the dispute and after reinvestigation.  
- **Sent by trackable method.** Certified mail, return receipt requested, with a postal tracking number. Some practitioners pair with email/portal submission to defeat receipt arguments.  
- **Retention copy.** Keep an exact copy, the receipt, and the green card.  
  
### 3. Cross-reference: direct disputes under § 1681s-2(a)(8) and 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43  
  
Under § 1681s-2(a)(8) and Reg V at 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43, consumers may also dispute directly with the furnisher. A direct dispute that satisfies § 1022.43(c) (identifying information, items disputed, basis, and supporting documents) obligates the furnisher to conduct a reasonable investigation, review information provided, complete the investigation within the § 1681i(a)(1) period, and correct any inaccuracy with each CRA to which it furnished. See CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-07 (reasonable investigation of consumer reporting disputes).  
  
Sending both an indirect dispute (to each CRA) and a direct dispute (to the furnisher) on the same operative facts creates parallel records and exposes any inconsistency between the furnisher's ACDV response and its direct-dispute response — a frequent willfulness fact pattern.

```  
of that provision are met.  
  
Sincerely,  
[Consumer signature]  
  
Enclosures: Exhibits A–[ ]  
```

## B. Free Annual Disclosures and Consumer File Requests  
  
### 1. AnnualCreditReport.com — § 1681j(a)  
  
Under 15 U.S.C. § 1681j(a) and the FACT Act implementing regulations, consumers are entitled to a free disclosure from each nationwide CRA every twelve months, accessible through the centralized source described in 12 C.F.R. § 1022.136 — practically, AnnualCreditReport.com. Since 2020 the three nationwide CRAs have voluntarily offered weekly free reports; that voluntary expansion does not displace the statutory right.  
  
For evidence-building, pull all three reports the same day so the snapshot is contemporaneous, save the PDFs, and hash or print-to-PDF with timestamp.  
  
### 2. Direct disclosure under § 1681g  
  
Section 1681g gives the consumer a right to "all information in the consumer's file at the time of the request," "the sources of the information," and the identification of "each person … that procured a consumer report" within specified periods. Send a § 1681g request directly to the CRA (not through AnnualCreditReport.com) when:  
  
- The case may require the "full file" (including codes and history not shown in the consumer-facing report).  
- The dispute involves "soft" inquiries the consumer-facing report does not display.  
- There is reason to suspect mixed-file or identity-confusion issues — the consumer file's residual identifiers (former names, addresses, SSN variations) become evidence.  
  
### 3. Specialty CRA disclosures  
  
Section 1681a(p) (nationwide specialty CRAs) and § 1681j(a)(1)(C) entitle consumers to an annual free disclosure from each. Practitioners should always pull, where relevant to the case:  
  
- **LexisNexis C.L.U.E.** (insurance loss history) and LexisNexis Accurint/Risk View.  
- **ChexSystems / Early Warning Services** (checking-account history).  
- **The Work Number / Equifax Workforce Solutions** (employment/income).  
- **Innovis** (fourth-bureau credit data).  
- **CoreLogic Credco**, **SageStream / LexisNexis Risk Solutions** (alternative credit).  
- **MIB Group** (life and health insurance).  
- **Certegy / TeleCheck** (check authorization).  
  
For tenant-screening and background-check disputes, request from each consumer reporting agency identified in the adverse-action notice (§ 1681m).  
  
### 4. Identity-theft fraud alerts and security freezes — § 1681c-1  
  
The Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act of 2018 (EGRRCPA) amended § 1681c-1 to make security freezes free of charge at all nationwide CRAs and extended the initial fraud-alert duration from 90 days to one year. Both create paper trails — a fraud-alert request requires the CRA to refer the consumer to the other nationwide CRAs (§ 1681c-1(a)(1)(B)) and to use procedures designed to verify identity before extending credit. Failures of these procedures are themselves FCRA violations.  
  
### 5. Identity-theft reports — § 1681a(q) and § 1681c-2  
  
Section 1681a(q) defines an "identity theft report" as a report alleging identity theft filed with an "appropriate Federal, State, or local law enforcement agency," subject to additional CRA-imposed reasonable requirements. The FTC's IdentityTheft.gov platform produces an FTC Identity Theft Report that satisfies § 1681a(q). Where additional support is needed, the FTC's Form ID Theft Affidavit and a local police report should be obtained.  
  
Once an identity-theft report is in place, § 1681c-2 obligates a CRA to block the reporting of any information identified by the consumer as resulting from identity theft within four business days of receipt, and to notify the furnisher. The § 1681c-2 record (block request, CRA acknowledgment, furnisher notification) becomes core evidence in any subsequent suit.

## C. FOIA and Federal-Agency Public Records  
  
### 1. CFPB FOIA — 5 U.S.C. § 552; 12 C.F.R. Part 1070  
  
Use cases:  
- CFPB supervisory and enforcement information about a specific furnisher or CRA, to the extent disclosable.  
- Consumer complaint patterns and underlying complaint narratives (much of which is already public via the database — see Section E).  
- Records of communications between the CFPB and a target entity.  
- Closing letters and unredacted versions of public consent orders.  
  
12 C.F.R. § 1070.13 requires written requests "reasonably describing" the records. CFPB Exemption 8 (bank examination) and Exemption 4 (commercial/trade secret) are common withholding grounds. Build the request to seek (a) records *received* by CFPB rather than examination work product, and (b) records about the consumer herself.  
  
### 2. FTC FOIA — 16 C.F.R. § 4.11  
  
The FTC's procedures are at 16 C.F.R. § 4.11. The FTC holds Consumer Sentinel data, FCRA enforcement records, identity-theft reports filed via IdentityTheft.gov, and historical guidance. A FOIA request can yield:  
  
- Complaint counts against a specific defendant.  
- The FTC's records of its enforcement file (for closed matters).  
- Identity-theft report copies submitted by the consumer or a relative.  
  
### 3. CFPB Consumer Complaint Database (public)  
  
The CFPB's Consumer Complaint Database at consumerfinance.gov/data-research/consumer-complaints is searchable without FOIA. Each record reflects an individual complaint and the company's public response. The "consumer narrative" field (when the consumer consented to publication) is often gold for showing pattern conduct. See Section E for query strategy.  
  
### 4. Sentinel database  
  
The FTC's Consumer Sentinel Network is a law-enforcement database; full access is restricted. Aggregated reports — including the FTC's annual "Consumer Sentinel Network Data Book" — are public and provide ranked complaint counts by category (e.g., "Credit Bureaus, Info. Furnishers and Report Users") that establish industry baselines.  
  
### 5. Prudential-regulator data  
  
The Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC publish enforcement actions, consent orders, and CMP (civil money penalty) records in searchable databases (e.g., FFIEC, OCC's enforcement-actions search, FDIC's Enforcement Decisions and Orders search). Many depository-institution furnishers are subject to one of these regulators; their public enforcement file is a rich source of FCRA-related compliance findings.  
  
### 6. CFPB consent orders and enforcement actions  
  
The CFPB's enforcement page lists all public consent orders. For a named defendant — particularly a nationwide CRA or large furnisher — search the consent-order page by entity, then pull the order itself for stipulated findings of fact (which can support willfulness/recklessness).

## D. State Public-Records Requests  
  
State-law mechanisms (often called "public records laws," "open records acts," or "freedom of information acts") supply records federal FOIA cannot reach.  
  
- **State attorney general consumer-complaint files.** Most state AGs maintain a consumer-complaint intake system. A public-records request will produce complaint counts and (often) complaint narratives for a named defendant.  
- **State banking and financial-services regulators.** Money-transmitter licensees, state-chartered banks, and consumer-credit licensees report supervisory data; many states make examination findings or summaries public.  
- **State DMV.** Where identity confusion is in issue, DMV records establish what addresses, license numbers, and dates of birth are associated with the consumer. Driver's Privacy Protection Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2721, controls permissible uses.  
- **Court records.**  
 - **PACER** for federal court — search by defendant name in federal civil cases (party-name search, nationwide). Identifying prior suits against the same CRA or furnisher is essential for willfulness, pattern, and class-certification analyses.  
 - **State court** dockets through state-specific portals (e.g., New York E-Courts, California Superior Court portals). Many states aggregate via vendors. Search both defendant-as-plaintiff (debt-collection suits the furnisher filed against consumers) and defendant-as-defendant.  
- **Bankruptcy court records.** PACER bankruptcy database — useful where the disputed account intersects a bankruptcy (e.g., reporting discharged debt as currently owed, a common FCRA violation pattern under *Carrera* and *Pittman* line of cases).  
  
### TEMPLATE — State public-records request (generic)  
  
```  
[Date]

Re: Request under [STATE PUBLIC RECORDS STATUTE]

```

## E. CFPB Complaint Database Queries  
  
The CFPB Consumer Complaint Database is the most useful public dataset in pre-suit FCRA practice. Located at consumerfinance.gov/data-research/consumer-complaints, the database supports query by:  
  
- **Company.** Use the exact registered name; check parent and subsidiary variants (e.g., "Equifax Information Services LLC" vs. "Equifax Inc.").  
- **Product / sub-product.** For FCRA cases: "Credit reporting, credit repair services, or other personal consumer reports" → "Credit reporting."  
- **Issue / sub-issue.** Examples: "Incorrect information on your report" → "Account status incorrect"; "Problem with a credit reporting company's investigation into an existing problem" → "Their investigation did not fix an error on your report."  
- **Date range, state, ZIP**, **submitted via**, and **consumer narrative** (full-text).  
  
The dataset is downloadable in CSV/JSON. Counsel can run frequency counts to demonstrate pattern conduct and import narrative excerpts (anonymized) into the complaint.  
  
### Using complaint patterns for *Safeco* willfulness  
  
Pre-suit notice that a defendant is receiving complaints about the same conduct, paired with a consent order or supervisory finding, supports the *Safeco*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007), "objectively unreasonable" prong. Counsel should:  
  
1. Pull all complaints against the defendant during the relevant period.  
2. Filter to the same issue/sub-issue as the consumer's claim.  
3. Quote the defendant's public response patterns (frequently boilerplate denials).  
4. Pair with any prior consent order acknowledging the conduct (CFPB enforcement page, FTC press releases, prudential-regulator records).  
5. Plead these facts to support § 1681n willfulness allegations.

## F. Pre-Suit Investigation of Furnishers  
  
### 1. Identify the right entity  
  
Furnisher identification errors waste service costs and provoke 12(b)(6) dismissals. Before drafting the complaint:  
  
- Pull the most recent consumer file from each nationwide CRA. The "creditor name" displayed is often a brand or servicer, not the real party in interest.  
- For revolving credit: identify the issuer of record, the servicer, and any affiliated billing entity.  
- For installment loans: identify the originator, the current note holder, and the servicer. Many subprime auto and student-loan accounts have been sold multiple times.  
- For debt buyers: identify the buyer of the receivable and any successive transferees; demand chain-of-title evidence (note transfers, bills of sale, account-level data files).  
- Check SEC EDGAR (10-K filings list subsidiaries) and state corporation-records databases.  
- Check NMLS Consumer Access for mortgage- and consumer-finance-licensee details.  
  
### 2. Furnisher's notice-of-furnishing address — § 1681s-2(a)(1)(C)  
  
Section 1681s-2(a)(1)(C) requires a furnisher that "clearly and conspicuously specifies to the consumer an address" for direct disputes to report only at that address. CRAs maintain a furnisher-address directory (and some publish furnisher-specific dispute addresses in the consumer's own report). Use this address for any pre-suit direct dispute and preserve confirmation of delivery; failure to use that address can preclude § 1681s-2(b) suit on a direct-dispute theory.  
  
### 3. CDIA and Metro 2 information  
  
The Consumer Data Industry Association (CDIA) publishes the *Credit Reporting Resource Guide* documenting Metro 2 format. While the *Guide* itself is copyrighted, public-domain materials include:  
  
- e-OSCAR's public materials at e-oscar.org (getting-started pages and high-level data dictionaries).  
- CFPB Supervisory Highlights and Bulletins discussing Metro 2 fields and ACDV codes.  
- Published court opinions reproducing ACDV screenshots and Metro 2 records (e.g., the many decisions discussing dispute codes 102 and 105).  
  
Pre-suit, counsel should understand at least: account status codes, payment-rating codes, the Compliance Condition Code (CCC) field, the Special Comment Code (SCC) field, and the Account Type Code. These will drive RFP language post-filing.

## G. Pre-Suit Identity-Theft Remediation as Discovery  
  
The § 1681c-2 block process and the § 1681c-1 fraud-alert process are evidence-producing pipelines:  
  
1. **Initial fraud alert** under § 1681c-1(a) — the CRA must (i) place the alert, (ii) refer the consumer to the other nationwide CRAs, and (iii) include the alert in any consumer report furnished. Save the CRA's confirmation; it documents the date the CRA had actual notice of the alleged identity theft.  
2. **§ 1681c-2 block request** — submit (i) appropriate proof of identity, (ii) an identity-theft report under § 1681a(q), (iii) identification of the information to be blocked, and (iv) a statement that the information is not the result of any transaction by the consumer. The CRA has four business days. The CRA's response (block, decline-to-block, or "decline" with cited basis under § 1681c-2(c)) is direct evidence of compliance or noncompliance.  
3. **Furnisher notification** — the CRA must promptly notify the furnisher that the information may result from identity theft (§ 1681c-2(b)). Counsel should later subpoena both sides of that transmission.  
  
Where the CRA declines a block under § 1681c-2(c), the decline letter is itself a litigation roadmap — it cites the specific subsection, and any error in the cited basis is willful-violation evidence.

## H. Pre-Suit Settlement and Demand Correspondence  
  
### 1. Why a pre-suit demand letter is useful  
  
The FCRA does not require a pre-suit demand. But a well-drafted demand letter (a) creates a documented offer to resolve, (b) communicates the consumer's evidence and theory, (c) pins the defendant to a response (which often includes admissions), and (d) supports later fee-shifting under § 1681n(a)(3) or § 1681o(a)(2) by showing reasonable pre-suit conduct.  
  
### 2. Rule 408 / FRE 408 considerations  
  
Federal Rule of Evidence 408 makes most "compromise offers and negotiations" inadmissible to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim. But Rule 408 has two exceptions to keep in mind:  
  
- It does **not** exclude evidence offered for "another purpose" — e.g., proving the defendant's knowledge of the dispute, the defendant's procedures, or the defendant's response (relevant to *Cushman*/§ 1681i reasonableness and *Safeco* willfulness).  
- Statements made **before** any "disputed claim" arises — i.e., before the defendant has rejected the consumer's position — may not be covered at all.  
  
Counsel should accordingly draft demand letters that (i) clearly mark settlement-discussion portions, (ii) keep factual recitations and statutory citations outside any "compromise" frame, and (iii) preserve the right to use the letter for purposes other than proving liability or damages.  
  
### 3. Best practices  
  
- Send by certified mail and email.  
- Attach the underlying evidence package (dispute letter, CRA response, ACDV records if obtained, credit denials).  
- Identify counsel and put the recipient on notice of representation (especially relevant under FDCPA's third-party-contact rule for related debt-collection claims).  
- Set a reasonable response deadline (typically 21–30 days).  
- Include a litigation-hold demand (see File 19) — even a pre-suit letter can trigger the duty to preserve.  
  
### 4. TEMPLATE — Pre-suit demand letter (with Rule 408 reservations)  
  
```  
[Law firm letterhead]  
[Date]  
  
VIA CERTIFIED MAIL AND EMAIL  
[Defendant — registered agent / general counsel]  
  
Re: [Consumer name] — FCRA Pre-Suit Demand and Litigation-Hold  
 Notice  
  
Sections marked "[FRE 408]" below are made for purposes of  
settlement discussions only and are not admissible to prove or  
disprove the validity or amount of any disputed claim. All other  
content is intended for, and may be used for, any non-Rule-408  
purpose, including but not limited to proving notice, knowledge,  
willfulness, procedures, and the existence and content of the  
parties' communications.  
  
I. PARTIES AND BACKGROUND  
 [Recite facts, identify FCRA provisions implicated, attach  
 evidence — non-Rule-408 section.]  
  
II. LITIGATION HOLD  
 You and your subsidiaries and affiliates are on notice of a  
 reasonably anticipated FCRA action. You are required to  
 preserve all ESI and tangible records relevant to this  
 dispute, including but not limited to: ACDV records,  
 e-OSCAR transmissions, dispute investigation files, training  
 materials, written policies and procedures, communications,  
 audit records, and database records. [See File 19 for full  
 scope.] [Non-Rule-408.]  
  
III. STATUTORY VIOLATIONS  
 [Identify §§ 1681e(b), 1681i, 1681s-2 violations with the  
 supporting facts. Non-Rule-408 — being communicated to put  
 you on notice.]  
  
IV. [FRE 408] SETTLEMENT PROPOSAL  
 Solely for purposes of compromise discussions under Federal  
 Rule of Evidence 408, my client offers to resolve all claims  
 relating to [scope] for [terms]. This offer expires [date].  
  
Please confirm receipt and counsel of record.  
  
Sincerely,  
[Counsel]  
```
