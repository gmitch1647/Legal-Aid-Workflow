# File 20 — Individual Witness Depositions in FCRA Cases  
  
**Topic:** Deposing individual fact witnesses at CRAs and furnishers (employees, supervisors, compliance personnel, custodians) — distinguished from Rule 30(b)(6) corporate-representative depositions covered in Files 11 and 12.  
  
**Primary FRCP rules:** 30 (depositions by oral examination), 30(c) (conduct, objections, instructions not to answer), 30(d) (duration, sanctions), 32 (use at trial), 45 (subpoenas for non-parties).  
  
**Primary FCRA / Reg V cross-references:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures for maximum possible accuracy), § 1681i (reinvestigation), § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duties on dispute), § 1681n (willful), § 1681o (negligent); 12 C.F.R. § 1022.42 (written policies); § 1022.43 (direct disputes).  
  
**Federal Rules of Evidence:** 602 (personal knowledge), 611 (mode of interrogation), 612 (writing used to refresh recollection), 801–807 (hearsay), 803(6) (business records), 901 (authentication).  
  
**Key cases cited:** *Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.*, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997); *Henson v. CSC Credit Services*, 29 F.3d 280 (7th Cir. 1994); *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016); *Safeco Ins. Co. v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007); *Sarver v. Experian*, 390 F.3d 969 (7th Cir. 2004); *Dalton v. Capital Associated Indus.*, 257 F.3d 409 (4th Cir. 2001); *Johnson v. MBNA America Bank*, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004); *Boggio v. USAA Federal Savings Bank*, 696 F.3d 611 (6th Cir. 2012).  
  
**Last updated:** 2026-05-16  
  
**See also:** File 11 (CRA 30(b)(6) topics), File 12 (Furnisher 30(b)(6) topics), File 14 (ESI from CRAs), File 17 (Privilege logs and objections), File 21 (Consumer-plaintiff deposition).

## Overview  
  
Individual fact-witness depositions are where abstract corporate procedures meet on-the-ground reality. A well-prepared 30(b)(6) designee will recite "our procedures" in the corporate voice. The frontline ACDV handler — the actual human who spent 90 seconds clicking "Verified" — will tell you what actually happened. Use individual depositions to (a) confirm or contradict the corporate testimony, (b) lock in admissions on willfulness factors under *Safeco*, and (c) build the trial narrative of human decisions made under productivity pressure.  
  
Plan the sequence carefully: usually take 30(b)(6) first to establish the policy baseline, then individuals to test compliance with that baseline. Sometimes the inverse is better — depose the line employee first to capture candor, then box in the corporate designee.

## A. The CRA Dispute Investigator Deposition  
  
### A.1 Who They Are  
  
CRAs staff dispute resolution with several tiers:  
  
- **Front-line ACDV handlers / dispute analysts** — often outsourced (Philippines, Costa Rica, Chile, India). Process consumer disputes through the e-OSCAR ACDV system. Productivity metrics typically demand 70–120 disputes per shift, equating to roughly 2–4 minutes per dispute.  
- **Dispute team leads / senior analysts** — handle escalations, file "frivolous or irrelevant" determinations under § 1681i(a)(3), interpret unclear consumer letters.  
- **U.S.-based supervisors / quality assurance** — review samples of offshore work, manage exception queues, interact with regulatory complaints.  
- **Reinvestigation specialists** — handle disputes flagged for manual review (typically mixed files, identity theft, repeat disputes).  
  
Identify them through (i) ACDV records produced in discovery (operator IDs), (ii) the corporate org chart obtained via 30(b)(6) Topic on organizational structure, (iii) interrogatory answers identifying persons with knowledge.

### A.3 Sample Question Sequences  
  
**Sequence 1: Pinning down "verification" as a matching exercise (the Cushman attack)**  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Sample Questions, CRA Investigator  
  
Q. When you process a dispute and the furnisher responds "verified,"  
 what do you do with that response?  
Q. Do you compare the furnisher's verified information to anything?  
Q. What do you compare it to?  
Q. Do you compare it to what's already in the CRA's system?  
Q. If the furnisher response matches what's already in the CRA system,  
 is that the end of your investigation?  
Q. Do you compare the furnisher's response to the consumer's evidence?  
Q. Did you in this case?  
Q. Why not?  
Q. Is it your understanding that "verification" means the furnisher  
 confirmed the data, or that the data is actually correct?  
Q. Are those the same thing?  
Q. Have you ever overruled a furnisher's "verified" response?  
Q. How often?  
Q. Under what circumstances?  
```  
  
The goal is to confirm — on the record — the *Cushman*-criticized practice of treating the furnisher's say-so as conclusive without independent evaluation, especially when the consumer has provided contradictory documentation.  
  
**Sequence 2: Time spent**  
  
```  
Q. On a typical shift, how many disputes do you handle?  
Q. How long is your shift?  
Q. What's the math — minutes per dispute?  
Q. Of those minutes, how much is "active work" vs. waiting for the  
 ACDV to return from the furnisher?  
Q. How many minutes of active work are you spending on the dispute  
 you actually do investigation on?  
Q. Looking at Exhibit 12 — this is the ACDV record for Ms. Smith's  
 dispute — does this system track how long you spent on this dispute?  
Q. Can you tell me how long you spent?  
Q. In those minutes, what did you read?  
```  
  
**Sequence 3: Documents reviewed**  
  
```  
Q. When you opened this dispute, did the consumer's letter appear  
 on your screen?  
Q. Could you see her attachments?  
Q. Did you read the attachments?  
Q. Did you forward the attachments to the furnisher?  
Q. Did the ACDV system send the attachments automatically?  
Q. Did the ACDV system summarize the consumer's claim in a two-digit  
 code?  
Q. What code was used here?  
Q. Does that code capture all the detail in the consumer's letter?  
```  
  
### A.4 Document-Handling Strategy  
  
Pre-mark exhibits in chronological order. Standard exhibit set for a CRA investigator dep:  
  
- ACDV record (input and output) for the disputes at issue.  
- Consumer's dispute letters and attachments.  
- CRA's written dispute-handling policies and procedures (in effect on relevant dates).  
- The investigator's training materials.  
- Productivity reports / metrics applicable to the investigator.  
- Any internal notes, e-mails, or system entries on the disputes.  
- Consent orders and supervisory findings (CFPB Supervisory Highlights, FTC settlements) that the corporate compliance team has acknowledged.  
  
Show the witness one document at a time. Lay foundation (FRE 901/902, 803(6)): "Do you recognize this? What is it? Was it generated in the ordinary course of business?"  
  
### A.5 Common Evasive Responses and How to Handle Them  
  
- **"I don't remember this specific dispute."** Acknowledge, then refresh recollection under FRE 612 with the ACDV record and any contemporaneous notes. If recollection cannot be refreshed, switch to habit/practice testimony under FRE 406: "Tell me how you would have handled it under your usual practice."  
- **"I just followed the procedure."** Pin down which procedure, what version, how the witness knew its contents, last time they read it.  
- **"That's a legal question."** Reframe as factual: "I'm not asking what the law requires. I'm asking what you actually did."  
- **"The system did it automatically."** Drill down: which system, what inputs trigger which outputs, who configured the rules, who can override.  
- **"You'd have to ask my supervisor."** Note the answer, ask for supervisor identity, return to witness's personal knowledge.

## B. The CRA Data Quality / Accuracy Specialist Deposition  
  
### B.1 Who They Are  
  
Behind the dispute team is an engineering and analytics function that designs and tunes the matching algorithms determining how data lands on a consumer file in the first place. Titles vary: data quality engineer, matching algorithm analyst, file-resolution specialist, mixed-file remediation team lead, data scientist on the consumer-identification team.  
  
### B.2 Outline (abbreviated)  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — CRA Data Quality / Accuracy Specialist  
  
1. Background and qualifications  
2. Department structure and mission  
3. The matching algorithm  
 a. Inputs from furnishers (name, SSN, DOB, address, etc.)  
 b. Matching tolerance — partial matches, fuzzy matching  
 c. SSN match requirements (full, last-4, none?)  
 d. Name-only matching scenarios  
 e. Algorithm version history and changes  
4. Mixed-file rate  
 a. Internal estimates of error rate  
 b. How rate is measured  
 c. Trends over time  
5. Known root causes of mixed files  
 a. Common surnames  
 b. Family members with similar names (Jr./Sr.)  
 c. Address overlap  
 d. Furnisher data quality  
6. Remediation  
 a. How identified mixed files are corrected  
 b. Re-occurrence rates  
 c. Manual review escalation  
7. Knowledge of FTC criticism  
 a. FTC "40 Years of Experience with the Fair Credit Reporting Act"  
 (2011) report on name-only matching  
 b. FTC mixed-file workshop materials  
 c. CFPB Supervisory Highlights addressing matching  
8. Knowledge of relevant court decisions  
 a. Sarver v. Experian (7th Cir. 2004)  
 b. Dalton v. Capital Associated Industries (4th Cir. 2001)  
 c. Any internal communications about court rulings  
9. Maximum possible accuracy under § 1681e(b)  
 a. Internal interpretation of the standard  
 b. How algorithm decisions weigh accuracy vs. coverage  
10. Trade-offs the team manages  
 a. False matches (data lands on wrong file)  
 b. False non-matches (data not reported, splits)  
 c. Internal acceptable error rate  
11. Audits and testing  
 a. A/B testing of algorithm changes  
 b. Sampling for accuracy review  
12. The specific consumer's file  
 a. Why did this data land on this file?  
 b. What matching criteria fired?  
```  
  
### B.3 Sample Questions  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Sample Questions, Data Quality Specialist  
  
Q. Does your team measure the rate at which data lands on the wrong  
 consumer's file?  
Q. What is that rate today?  
Q. What was it five years ago?  
Q. Is your team familiar with the FTC's 2011 report "40 Years of  
 Experience with the Fair Credit Reporting Act"?  
Q. That report criticized name-only matching. Are you aware of that?  
Q. Does [CRA] still permit data to be added to a file based on a  
 less-than-exact match of identifying information?  
Q. When the algorithm permits a partial match — for example, name  
 plus partial SSN — what is the documented purpose of allowing

Q. Is "maximum possible accuracy" a standard your team uses?  
Q. How is that standard operationalized in algorithm design?  
```

## D. The Furnisher Dispute-Handling Employee Deposition  
  
### D.1 Who They Are  
  
Dispute-resolution specialists, account-documentation team members, debt-buyer chain-of-title staff. The *Hinkle v. Midland* (11th Cir. 2016) line of cases makes this witness essential: the question is whether the furnisher conducted a *reasonable* investigation under § 1681s-2(b), which often turns on whether the witness actually looked at any account-level documentation or merely confirmed that the data in the furnisher's own system matched the data in the CRA's system.  
  
### D.2 Outline  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Furnisher Dispute Employee  
  
1. Identification, role, supervisor  
2. Training (FCRA-specific and Reg V)  
3. Productivity expectations (disputes per shift)  
4. ACDV intake from CRAs  
 a. Receipt and routing  
 b. Two-digit dispute code interpretation  
 c. Consumer attachments — received? reviewed?  
5. System(s) of record reviewed  
 a. Originating-creditor system (for debt buyers: seller's data)  
 b. Internal collections system  
 c. Imaged documents (applications, statements, contracts)  
 d. Notes and call logs  
6. Hinkle question: what is actually reviewed?  
 a. Original account documents  
 b. Bills of sale and account-level data (debt buyers)  
 c. Affidavits or attestations from seller  
7. Authority to delete or modify  
 a. Threshold for deletion  
 b. Threshold for "verify"  
 c. Escalation path  
8. Time per dispute  
9. Communication with consumer  
 a. Phone calls during dispute  
 b. Written requests for additional information  
10. Communication with seller / originator (debt buyers)  
 a. Requests for back-up documentation  
 b. SLAs for seller response  
 c. What seller actually provides  
11. Specific dispute(s) at issue  
 a. Walk through each step  
 b. Documents reviewed  
 c. Time spent  
 d. Decision rationale  
12. Policy materials  
13. Knowledge of contrary information  
 a. Prior disputes from same consumer  
 b. Internal notes flagging issue  
14. Identity-theft handling  
15. Bankruptcy / discharge accounts  
16. The Hinkle question, directly  
 a. "Did you confirm the account belongs to the consumer or did  
 you confirm the data in your system matched the CRA's data?"  
```  
  
### D.3 Sample Questions  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Furnisher Dispute Employee, Hinkle Sequence  
  
Q. When you receive an ACDV from a CRA, what's the first thing you  
 do?  
Q. Do you pull the original account file?  
Q. By "original account file," I mean the application, the contract,  
 the statements — those original documents. Do you pull those?  
Q. In this case, did you pull any of those documents?  
Q. If you didn't, what did you look at?  
Q. When you confirmed the account, you confirmed that the data in  
 your collection system matched the data in the CRA's system,  
 correct?  
Q. You did not independently verify that the underlying account  
 actually belongs to Ms. Smith — true?  
```  
  
For debt buyers:  
  
```  
Q. Your employer purchased this account from [Seller], correct?  
Q. In purchasing this account, what account-level documentation did  
 your employer obtain?  
Q. Did you obtain the original signed application?  
Q. Did you obtain monthly statements?  
Q. Did you obtain a record of payment history?  
Q. Or did you receive only a data file with account information?  
Q. When Ms. Smith disputed, did you request additional documentation  
 from [Seller]?  
Q. Did [Seller] provide any?  
Q. What did you do with what they provided?  
```

## F. Document Custodian Depositions  
  
### F.1 Purpose  
  
Often-overlooked but critical for trial. The custodian authenticates documents under FRE 901(b)(1) and lays foundation for the business-records exception to hearsay under FRE 803(6).  
  
### F.2 Outline  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Document Custodian  
  
1. Identity and role; custody and control  
2. Record-keeping systems  
 a. Systems used in regular course  
 b. Data sources and integrations  
 c. Retention schedules  
3. Authentication (per FRE 901, 803(6))  
 a. Are these records made at or near the time?  
 b. By a person with knowledge?  
 c. Kept in the regular course of business?  
 d. Was it the regular practice of the business to make them?  
4. Specific documents  
 a. Identify each exhibit  
 b. Confirm where it lives, how it's generated  
5. Production scope  
 a. What was searched  
 b. What was produced  
 c. What was withheld and why  
6. Gaps  
 a. Categories not produced  
 b. Categories not retained  
 c. Categories destroyed  
7. Litigation hold  
 a. When issued  
 b. To whom  
 c. Confirmation of compliance  
8. Chain of custody for ESI  
 a. Collection vendor  
 b. Hash verification  
 c. Processing  
```  
  
### F.3 Identifying Gaps  
  
The custodian dep is the moment to make a record on documents that *should* exist but don't appear in production: phone logs, instant-message threads, ticketing-system tickets, draft policies. Ask categorically — what types of records existed during the relevant period? — then compare against what was produced.

## G. Practical Deposition Mechanics  
  
### G.1 Pre-Deposition Document Indexing  
  
Build an exhibit binder organized chronologically with cross-references. For each exhibit prepare a one-line index entry: exhibit number, Bates range, date, source, purpose.  
  
### G.2 Exhibit Handling and Bates Numbering  
  
Pre-mark exhibits before the deposition. Use sequential numbering across the case (Exhibit 1 in dep 1 is not Exhibit 1 in dep 2). Keep an exhibit log. Always have spare clean copies for the witness, witness's counsel, your second chair, the videographer, and the court reporter.  
  
### G.3 Reading-and-Signing Procedures  
  
Under FRCP 30(e)(1), the deponent may request review of the transcript and make changes in form or substance within 30 days. The witness signs a statement listing the reasons for changes. Reserve reading and signing routinely; waive only when there is a strategic reason.  
  
### G.4 Errata and Use at Trial  
  
A material substantive change ("yes" to "no") on errata creates impeachment opportunity. Courts split on whether substantive errata change the original testimony or merely supplement it. Trial use under Rule 32(a) permits use of an adverse party's deposition for any purpose; preserve all impeachment uses.  
  
### G.5 Video Deposition Strategy  
  
Notice video under Rule 30(b)(3)(A). Plan camera framing for trial playback. Avoid stage directions on the record. Watch the witness's body language. For trial-preservation depositions (witness unavailable under Rule 32(a)(4)), brief the witness on appearance.

## H. Defending Against Deposition Objections  
  
### H.1 Form vs. Speaking Objections  
  
Rule 30(c)(2) permits only objections "stated concisely in a nonargumentative and nonsuggestive manner." Acceptable: "Object to form." Unacceptable: "Object to form — the question is compound, vague, and assumes facts not in evidence; if you understood the question to mean X, then you can answer." The latter coaches the witness.  
  
### H.2 Privilege Instructions Not to Answer  
  
Rule 30(c)(2) permits an instruction not to answer only (i) to preserve a privilege, (ii) to enforce a court-ordered limitation, or (iii) to present a Rule 30(d)(3) motion. Anything else is improper. When opposing counsel instructs the witness not to answer on any other ground, make a clean record: identify the question, the instruction, the ground asserted, your position, and (if appropriate) suspend and seek a ruling under Rule 30(d)(3).  
  
### H.3 Coaching and Speaking Objections — Sanctions  
  
Courts have sanctioned counsel for speaking objections and witness coaching. See *Hall v. Clifton Precision*, 150 F.R.D. 525 (E.D. Pa. 1993) (no private conferences during questioning); *In re Stratosphere Corp. Securities Litigation*, 182 F.R.D. 614 (D. Nev. 1998). When confronted with coaching, ask the witness on the record: "Did you understand the objection to suggest an answer? Did your lawyer's statement help you understand the question? Please answer the question I asked you, in your own words." Then move on.  
  
### H.4 Breaks During Deposition  
  
Rule 30(c)(2) and the *Hall v. Clifton Precision* line: no consultation between counsel and witness during a question. Between questions, breaks are generally permissible, but cannot be used to coach. If opposing counsel requests a break in the middle of a question, complete the question and answer first.

## I. Common Evasion Tactics and Counters  
  
### I.1 "I Don't Recall" — Exhausting Recollection  
  
```  
TEMPLATE — Exhausting "I Don't Recall"  
  
Q. You don't recall — is that right?  
Q. Is there any document that would refresh your recollection?  
Q. Is there any person you could speak with who would refresh your  
 recollection?  
Q. Let me show you Exhibit 12. Does this refresh your recollection?  
Q. If not, can you tell me how you would have handled it under your  
 normal practice?  
```  
  
If the witness genuinely cannot recall and no document refreshes, switch to FRE 406 habit/practice testimony to capture how the witness *would have* handled it. Memorialize the failure of recollection cleanly so the witness cannot later supply details at trial without being impeached.  
  
### I.2 "It Depends" — Pinning Down Hypotheticals  
  
```  
Q. "It depends" — depends on what?  
Q. Let's take each variable in turn. Variable 1 is X. If X is true,  
 what's the answer?  
Q. If X is false?  
```  
  
Convert the conditional into a series of definite answers. Or push for an answer about the specific case: "Setting aside hypotheticals, in this case, what happened?"  
  
### I.3 "Refer to the Document" — Getting Witness's Own Knowledge  
  
```  
Q. I'm not asking what the document says. I'm asking what you  
 personally know.  
Q. Do you have any personal knowledge separate from the document?  
Q. Did you create the document? Were you involved in its creation?  
Q. Did you act on the document?  
```  
  
### I.4 Privilege Overreach — Making the Record  
  
When counsel asserts privilege over factual questions (e.g., "Did you talk to your supervisor about this?"), be precise: ask first for the existence of the communication, then the date, then the participants. Only the content is privileged. Make a clean record: identify each question, the objection, your response, and reserve to compel.
