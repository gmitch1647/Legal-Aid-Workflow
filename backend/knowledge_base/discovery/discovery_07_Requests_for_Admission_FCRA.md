# Requests for Admission in FCRA Litigation — Both Sides  
**Topic:** Requests for admission (RFAs) under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 36 — strategic use, sample templates for CRAs, furnishers, and consumer plaintiffs, and common pitfalls  
**Primary rule:** FRCP 36; FRE 36(b) (admissions conclusively established)  
**Cross-references:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1681e(b), 1681i, 1681s-2; 12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.40–.43; FRCP 56 (summary judgment); FRE 401, 403, 901 (authentication); CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-07  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
## Introduction and strategic role  
  
Requests for admission under FRCP 36 occupy a particular and underused niche in FCRA practice. Unlike interrogatories and document requests, RFAs are not discovery in the strict sense; they are devices to narrow the issues for trial and to remove undisputed facts from contention. An admitted RFA is "conclusively established" under FRCP 36(b) (subject to withdrawal or amendment on a showing tied to the merits and absence of prejudice).  
  
Used well, RFAs in FCRA cases accomplish four things: (1) authenticate documents and ESI to streamline summary-judgment exhibits and trial; (2) pin down concessions on dispute receipts, timelines, and contents that would otherwise be debated at trial; (3) narrow the live issues to the contested legal questions (reasonableness; willfulness; damages); and (4) shift the costs of unreasonable denial under FRCP 37(c)(2).  
  
A common drafting error is to use RFAs as alternative-pleading "smoking-gun" questions. Courts and rules disfavor argumentative, compound, or legal-conclusion RFAs. The most effective RFAs are short, factually precise, and tied to a document or specific event. Numerical limits vary by district and case management order, but RFAs that authenticate documents are typically treated as unlimited or generously limited, as in most local rules. Drafters should check the local rules and the scheduling order.  
  
All sample text is delineated as "TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE."

## A. RFAs to CRAs (consumer reporting agencies)  
  
The CRA's records are mostly structured electronic data: the consumer's "file" within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(g); the dispute logs; the timing of forwarded ACDVs to furnishers; the procedures for handling specific dispute categories. RFAs to CRAs should authenticate that data and lock down the timeline of reinvestigation under § 1681i.  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
1. Admit that Exhibit __ is a true and correct copy of Plaintiff's consumer file maintained by Defendant on [date], within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(g).  
  
2. Admit that Defendant received from Plaintiff a written dispute concerning the [account/item] identified in Paragraph __ of the Complaint, on [date].  
  
3. Admit that Exhibit __ is a true and correct copy of the dispute correspondence Defendant received from Plaintiff on [date].  
  
4. Admit that Defendant transmitted an ACDV concerning Plaintiff's dispute to Defendant [furnisher] on [date].  
  
5. Admit that Defendant did not transmit any attachment from Plaintiff's dispute correspondence with the ACDV to the furnisher.  
 *Annotation: targets CFPB Circular 2022-07 — CRA must forward "all relevant information," including consumer-supplied documents.*  
  
6. Admit that Defendant did not request any additional information from Plaintiff before transmitting the ACDV to the furnisher.  
  
7. Admit that Defendant received an ACDV response from the furnisher on [date].  
  
8. Admit that the ACDV response received from the furnisher was "verified as reported" (or insert specific response code).  
  
9. Admit that Defendant did not seek any further information from the furnisher beyond the ACDV response before completing the reinvestigation.  
  
10. Admit that Defendant did not contact Plaintiff for any clarifying information after receiving the furnisher's ACDV response.  
  
11. Admit that Defendant's reinvestigation of Plaintiff's [date] dispute was completed within [N] days of receipt of the dispute.  
  
12. Admit that the [account/item] identified in Paragraph __ of the Complaint was furnished by Defendant to [identify third-party user] on [date].  
  
13. Admit that, during the 12 months preceding Plaintiff's [date] dispute, Defendant had no written procedure specifically directed to dispute type [identify category — mixed file / identity theft / post-discharge bankruptcy reporting / etc.] beyond Defendant's general dispute-handling procedure.  
 *Annotation: targets the procedural-gap holding in *Williams v. First Advantage LNS Screening Solutions, Inc.*, 947 F.3d 735, 760–62 (11th Cir. 2020), where the absence of a procedure for flagging disputed criminal-record information supported willfulness.*  
  
14. Admit that Exhibit __ is a true and correct copy of Defendant's written policy or procedure governing the handling of disputes of the type at issue in this case, as in effect on [date].  
  
15. Admit that Defendant did not request from the furnisher any document beyond the ACDV response in connection with Plaintiff's [date] dispute.

## B. RFAs to furnishers  
  
RFAs to furnishers should authenticate ACDV records, lock down the timing and contents of the dispute investigation, and identify procedural gaps. They are particularly valuable for nailing down the ACDV control number, the identity of the investigator, and the absence of account-level documentation in debt-buyer cases.  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
16. Admit that Exhibit __ is a true and correct copy of the inbound ACDV that Defendant received from [CRA] concerning Plaintiff on [date].  
  
17. Admit that Exhibit __ is a true and correct copy of Defendant's outbound response to the ACDV identified in Request 16.  
  
18. Admit that Defendant received the ACDV identified in Request 16 on [date].  
  
19. Admit that Defendant transmitted the response identified in Request 17 on [date].  
  
20. Admit that the only document Defendant reviewed in investigating the ACDV identified in Request 16 was a screen print or summary screen from Defendant's account-management system.  
  
21. Admit that Defendant did not retrieve or review the original application for Plaintiff's account in investigating the ACDV identified in Request 16.  
  
22. Admit that Defendant did not retrieve or review any payment records, statements, or transaction history (beyond a summary screen) for Plaintiff's account in investigating the ACDV identified in Request 16.  
  
23. Admit that Defendant did not contact Plaintiff in connection with investigating the ACDV identified in Request 16.  
  
24. Admit that Defendant has no written policy or procedure that specifically requires retrieval of account-level documents in connection with dispute type [identify].  
 *Annotation: under *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295, 1305–08 (11th Cir. 2016), the absence of such a procedure for debt-buyer "verified as reported" responses supports a willfulness finding.*  
  
25. Admit that Defendant did not request from the seller of Plaintiff's account, or from any prior owner, any account-level documentation in connection with the ACDV identified in Request 16. [Debt-buyer-specific.]  
  
26. Admit that Defendant's purchase agreement for Plaintiff's account entitled Defendant to request account-level documentation from the seller. [Debt-buyer-specific.]  
  
27. Admit that Defendant has received [N] or more ACDVs during the relevant period bearing the same inbound dispute code at issue in this case.  
  
28. Admit that Defendant has been a defendant in [N] or more civil actions during the relevant period alleging violations of 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b).  
  
29. Admit that Defendant did not flag Plaintiff's account as disputed under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(a)(3) before the date Plaintiff filed this action.  
 *Annotation: targets the *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co. of Va.*, 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008), holding that failure to flag a known dispute renders furnished information materially misleading.*  
  
30. Admit that the response code Defendant transmitted in response to the ACDV identified in Request 16 was "verified as reported" (or insert exact code).  
  
31. Admit that Defendant has no written policy or procedure that specifically addresses the prohibition in 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(a)(6)(B) on refurnishing of information blocked under § 1681c-2. [Identity-theft-specific.]  
  
32. Admit that no employee of Defendant who handled the ACDV identified in Request 16 had completed training on dispute type [identify] before handling the dispute.  
  
33. Admit that Defendant maintains no written record of any periodic review under 12 C.F.R. § 1022.42(c) of its policies and procedures concerning [specific subject matter] during the relevant period.

## C. RFAs to consumer plaintiffs (defense-side)  
  
Defendants use RFAs to consumer plaintiffs to: lock down dollar-damages limitations; remove certain affirmative-case elements (e.g., emotional distress) when not pled or developed; develop background-credit-history facts that may bear on actual damages; and identify any communications with credit repair organizations (which can affect both damages and causation). These RFAs must be carefully tied to admissible inquiries — they cannot intrude on permissible-purposes confidential matters and should not be argumentative.  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
34. Admit that, in the 24 months preceding the filing of the Complaint, Plaintiff had at least one consumer credit account in delinquent status with a creditor other than Defendant.  
  
35. Admit that Plaintiff did not apply for any credit product between [date of dispute] and [date], other than as identified in Plaintiff's responses to Defendant's Interrogatory No. __.  
  
36. Admit that Plaintiff did not consult a medical or mental health professional for treatment of any condition Plaintiff attributes to Defendant's conduct.  
  
37. Admit that Plaintiff did not lose any specific job, promotion, or business opportunity as a direct result of Defendant's conduct alleged in the Complaint, other than as identified in Plaintiff's responses to Defendant's Interrogatory No. __.  
  
38. Admit that Plaintiff did not pay any out-of-pocket expense to obtain credit at a less favorable rate than Plaintiff would otherwise have received as a result of Defendant's conduct alleged in the Complaint, other than as identified in Plaintiff's responses to Defendant's Interrogatory No. __.  
  
39. Admit that Plaintiff retained a credit-repair organization (within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1679a(3)) at any time between [date] and [date].  
  
40. Admit that Plaintiff sent the dispute correspondence dated [date] (Exhibit __) at the direction or through the assistance of a credit-repair organization.  
  
41. Admit that Plaintiff seeks no damages other than statutory damages and attorneys' fees in this action. [Use only where the complaint or written-discovery responses arguably support this.]  
  
42. Admit that, before filing this action, Plaintiff did not request from Defendant a copy of Plaintiff's account file.

## D. Strategic deployment  
  
### RFAs and motion for summary judgment  
  
A well-deployed series of RFAs can convert a contested fact pattern into one on which summary judgment is appropriate. The pattern:  
  
1. Authenticate the ACDV record by RFA → admitted document is FRE 901-authenticated and admissible at summary judgment.  
2. Lock down the elements of receipt and timing → these become uncontested in opposition.  
3. Use the furnisher's denial-or-admission of "reasonable investigation" not as a substantive admission (it is a legal conclusion) but as a vehicle to flush out what *specific facts* the furnisher relies on.  
  
The plaintiff's reply on summary judgment can then cite admitted RFAs and put the remaining issues (reasonableness, willfulness, damages) into clear contestable shape.  
  
### FRE 36(b) — conclusive effect and withdrawal  
  
Under FRCP 36(b), an admitted RFA "is conclusively established unless the court, on motion, permits the admission to be withdrawn or amended." Withdrawal requires that the merits be served and that the requesting party not be prejudiced. Drafters can use this to plant authenticating RFAs early; if a later motion to withdraw is filed, prejudice is often clear once trial preparation has been keyed to the admission.  
  
### FRCP 37(c)(2) — cost-shifting for unreasonable denial  
  
FRCP 37(c)(2) provides for an award of reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, when a party "fails to admit what is requested under Rule 36" and the requesting party later proves the matter true. The exceptions are narrow: the RFA was objectionable; the admission was of no substantial importance; the denying party had a reasonable ground to believe it might prevail; or there was other good reason for the failure to admit. Plaintiffs should track non-admissions and use FRCP 37(c)(2) at the close of trial.  
  
### Trial preparation  
  
Admitted RFAs become read-in stipulations at trial under FRCP 36(b). For complex FCRA fact patterns — especially Metro 2 field histories and ACDV chronologies — read-in stipulations are far more efficient than direct examination. The drafter should build the RFAs with the eventual trial read-in in mind: short, declarative, free of "claims," "alleges," or other lawyerly hedges that would read awkwardly.

## E. Common pitfalls  
  
- **Argumentative RFAs.** "Admit that Defendant violated 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b)." This is a legal conclusion and almost always met with an objection sustained under FRCP 36(a)(4). Restructure as factual: "Admit that the investigation of the ACDV identified in Request __ consisted solely of [specific action]."  
  
- **Compound RFAs.** RFAs that combine multiple factual assertions invite a one-word denial. Each fact gets its own RFA.  
  
- **Calling for legal conclusions.** "Admit that the report was inaccurate." Restructure: "Admit that on [date] Defendant furnished a balance of $X for Plaintiff's account; admit that on [date] Plaintiff's balance was $0; admit that the difference between $X and $0 is material to a credit decision."  
  
- **Burying the document.** "Admit the truth of the contents of Exhibit __." This is rarely effective. Authenticate the document with one RFA, then use targeted RFAs about specific facts contained in the document.  
  
- **Misuse for discovery.** RFAs are not the right vehicle to learn unknown facts. They are the vehicle to remove known facts from contention.  
  
- **Failure to police evasive denials.** A denial must "fairly respond to the substance of the matter," FRCP 36(a)(4). Lawyers' "denied as written" or "denied on information and belief" responses are typically improper; move to determine sufficiency under FRCP 36(a)(6).  
  
- **Confusing identification with authentication.** "Admit that Exhibit __ is what it purports to be" is rarely useful. Specify: "Admit that Exhibit __ is a true and correct copy of [the document Defendant produced as Bates 0001]."

## F. Sample RFAs for the most common FCRA fact patterns  
  
### Mixed-file case (consumer A's file contains consumer B's data)  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
A1. Admit that Defendant's file on Plaintiff during the relevant period contained at least one tradeline that did not belong to Plaintiff.  
  
A2. Admit that Defendant did not match all of the following identifiers in determining whether to include the tradeline identified in Request A1 in Plaintiff's file: full name; complete Social Security Number; date of birth; address.  
  
A3. Admit that Defendant had no written procedure specifically directed to dispute claims of mixed-file error during the relevant period.  
  
A4. Admit that Defendant's matching algorithm during the relevant period included a tolerance for partial Social Security Number matches.  
  
### Reinvestigation failure (CRA defendant)  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
B1. Admit that Defendant received Plaintiff's dispute of [item] on [date].  
  
B2. Admit that Defendant's complete reinvestigation under 15 U.S.C. § 1681i consisted of transmitting an ACDV to the furnisher and receiving the furnisher's response.  
  
B3. Admit that Defendant did not retrieve, review, or consider any document supplied by Plaintiff with the dispute beyond transmitting it (or failing to transmit it) to the furnisher.  
  
B4. Admit that the response Defendant received from the furnisher was identical, in all substantive respects, to the information previously furnished by the furnisher and disputed by Plaintiff.  
  
B5. Admit that Defendant has no written procedure that requires escalation to a human reviewer when the furnisher's ACDV response is identical to the disputed information.  
  
### Furnisher failure to flag dispute (post-*Saunders*)  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
C1. Admit that Plaintiff sent Defendant a written communication on [date] (Exhibit __) disputing the account identified in Paragraph __ of the Complaint.  
  
C2. Admit that, in furnishings of information about Plaintiff's account to any CRA on or after [date], Defendant did not include any notice that the account was disputed.  
  
C3. Admit that Defendant has no written procedure that requires automated insertion of an "XB" Consumer Information Indicator or other dispute-notice code on furnishings of disputed accounts.  
  
C4. Admit that Defendant first marked Plaintiff's account as disputed in furnishings to any CRA on [date], which was after Plaintiff filed this action.  
  
### Identity-theft case (furnisher refurnishing of blocked information)  
  
**TEMPLATE — ADAPT TO CASE**  
  
D1. Admit that Plaintiff submitted to Defendant an identity-theft report (within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(q)(4)) on [date].  
  
D2. Admit that Defendant received notice from [CRA] on [date] that the account identified in Paragraph __ of the Complaint had been blocked under 15 U.S.C. § 1681c-2.  
  
D3. Admit that Defendant furnished information about the account identified in Paragraph __ to [CRA] on [date], which is after the date identified in Request D2.  
  
D4. Admit that the information identified in Request D3 was the information blocked pursuant to 15 U.S.C. § 1681c-2.
