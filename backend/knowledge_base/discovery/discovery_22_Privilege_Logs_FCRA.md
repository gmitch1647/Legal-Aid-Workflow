# Discovery Module 22: Privilege Logs and Privilege Challenges in FCRA Litigation  
  
---  
  
**Topic:** Privilege logs and privilege challenges specific to FCRA consumer cases  
**Primary FRCP Rules:** Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(3), 26(b)(5), 37(a)  
**Primary FRE Rules:** Fed. R. Evid. 501, 502(b), 502(d)  
**FCRA Cross-References:** 15 U.S.C. Sec. 1681i (reinvestigation duty); 15 U.S.C. Sec. 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duty); CFPB Confidential Supervisory Information rules at 12 C.F.R. Sec. 1070.40-.49  
**Last Updated:** 2026-05-16

## A. Privilege Log Requirements Under Rule 26(b)(5)  
  
### The Rule  
  
Rule 26(b)(5)(A) provides that when a party withholds information otherwise discoverable by claiming privilege or work-product protection, the party must:  
  
1. Expressly make the claim; and  
2. Describe the nature of the documents, communications, or tangible things not produced or disclosed, and do so "in a manner that, without revealing information itself privileged or protected, will enable other parties to assess the claim."  
  
A bare invocation of "privilege" is insufficient. See, e.g., *In re Grand Jury Subpoena*, 274 F.3d 563, 575-76 (1st Cir. 2001) (collecting authority that conclusory privilege claims waive privilege). Failure to provide an adequate log can constitute waiver. *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 408 F.3d 1142, 1149 (9th Cir. 2005) (boilerplate objections without log entries risk waiver).  
  
### Document-by-Document vs. Categorical Logs  
  
The traditional approach is a document-by-document log. However, courts increasingly accept categorical logs where document-by-document logging would be unduly burdensome, provided the categories are sufficiently specific. See *SEC v. Thrasher*, 1996 WL 125661, at *1 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 20, 1996); *In re Imperial Corp. of Am.*, 174 F.R.D. 475, 479 (S.D. Cal. 1997). In FCRA cases involving repeated form communications (e.g., a CRA's reinvestigation file containing hundreds of templated dispute-tracking screens), parties sometimes negotiate categorical logging in the Rule 26(f) report.  
  
### Required Fields  
  
A defensible privilege log entry should include:  
  
- **Bates range or unique document identifier**  
- **Date of document**  
- **Author / sender** (name and title/role)  
- **All recipients** including cc and bcc (name and title/role)  
- **Document type** (email, memo, draft, etc.)  
- **Subject matter** described with enough specificity to assess the claim but not enough to reveal privileged content  
- **Privilege asserted** (attorney-client, work product, etc.)  
- **Basis for assertion** (e.g., "communication seeking legal advice from in-house counsel regarding ACDV response procedures")  
  
### Timing  
  
The log is generally due at the time of the response to the discovery request unless the court's scheduling order says otherwise. Many districts have local rules or standing orders extending this period. Late logs can result in waiver. See *Universal City Dev. Partners, Ltd. v. Ride & Show Eng'g, Inc.*, 230 F.R.D. 688, 695 (M.D. Fla. 2005).  
  
### Updates and Supplementation  
  
Rule 26(e) imposes a duty to supplement. New documents that become responsive (e.g., a post-suit dispute investigation file) must be added. Stale or incorrect log entries must be corrected.

## B. Common Privileges Asserted in FCRA Cases  
  
### Attorney-Client Privilege  
  
The classic test: (1) a communication; (2) made between privileged persons; (3) in confidence; (4) for the purpose of seeking, obtaining, or providing legal assistance to the client. In corporate FCRA defendants, the privilege extends to communications between counsel and corporate employees within the scope of *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383 (1981) - communications by employees concerning matters within the scope of their duties, made at the direction of superiors, for the purpose of securing legal advice.  
  
### Work Product Doctrine  
  
Codified at Rule 26(b)(3) and rooted in *Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495 (1947). Protects documents and tangible things prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial by or for a party or its representative. Two tiers: ordinary work product (overcome by substantial need plus undue hardship) and opinion work product (mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal theories - near-absolute protection).  
  
### Self-Critical Analysis Privilege - Generally Rejected  
  
The "self-critical analysis" or "self-evaluation" privilege has been raised - and rejected - by most federal courts in the FCRA context. See Section C below.  
  
### Bank Examination Privilege  
  
A qualified common-law privilege protecting communications between a federal bank examiner and the regulated bank. *In re Subpoena Served Upon Comptroller of Currency*, 967 F.2d 630, 633-34 (D.C. Cir. 1992); *In re Bankers Tr. Co.*, 61 F.3d 465, 471 (6th Cir. 1995). Crucially, the privilege does *not* protect underlying bank records; it shields the examiner's deliberative communications and the bank's responses. Belongs to the regulator, not the bank.  
  
### Trade Secret Protection  
  
Not a true privilege. Rule 26(c)(1)(G) authorizes protective orders to prevent disclosure of "a trade secret or other confidential research, development, or commercial information." Common in FCRA cases for matching algorithms, dispute-handling software workflows, fraud-detection scoring models. The information is discoverable; access is restricted.  
  
### Confidential Supervisory Information (CSI)  
  
CSI is a regulatory protection, not a common-law privilege.  
  
- **CFPB:** 12 C.F.R. Sec. 1070.40-.49. Examination reports, supervisory letters, and related communications are CFPB property; the supervised entity must obtain CFPB's written permission before disclosure even in litigation. Compelled disclosure requires following the CFPB's *Touhy* regulations at 12 C.F.R. Sec. 1070.50-.55.  
- **Federal Reserve Board:** Regulation PS-equivalent regime at 12 C.F.R. Sec. 261.20-.23 protecting confidential supervisory information.  
- **OCC:** 12 C.F.R. Part 4 (Subpart C), particularly 12 C.F.R. Sec. 4.32-.39.  
  
Courts generally require parties to subpoena the agency under its *Touhy* regulations rather than treat CSI as automatically off-limits. *In re Bankers Tr. Co.*, 61 F.3d at 470-71.

## C. The Self-Critical Analysis Privilege  
  
### The Doctrine  
  
The doctrine originated in *Bredice v. Doctors Hospital, Inc.*, 50 F.R.D. 249 (D.D.C. 1970), aff'd, 479 F.2d 920 (D.C. Cir. 1973), shielding hospital peer-review materials. It has since been asserted, with varying success, in employment discrimination, products liability, and (occasionally) FCRA matters.  
  
### Why Most Courts Reject It in FCRA Cases  
  
The Supreme Court has signaled hostility. *Univ. of Pennsylvania v. EEOC*, 493 U.S. 182, 189 (1990), rejected an academic self-critical analysis privilege, emphasizing that new privileges are disfavored and that the legislative branch is the proper body to create them.  
  
Federal courts addressing the privilege in FCRA and adjacent consumer-protection contexts have generally rejected it. See *Reichhold Chems., Inc. v. Textron, Inc.*, 157 F.R.D. 522, 524-27 (N.D. Fla. 1994) (collecting cases); *In re Air Crash Near Cali, Colom.*, 959 F. Supp. 1529, 1532-35 (S.D. Fla. 1997). The reasoning:  
  
1. No FRE or FRCP basis;  
2. *University of Pennsylvania* counsels against new federal privileges;  
3. The "chilling effect" rationale - that disclosure will deter useful self-evaluation - is empirically weak when other forces (regulators, internal risk management, customer complaints) drive the same conduct;  
4. CRAs and furnishers conduct audits because of regulatory and business pressures, not because of confidentiality assurances.  
  
### Practical Implications  
  
The following CRA and furnisher documents are *generally discoverable* notwithstanding self-critical analysis claims:  
  
- Internal audit reports on dispute handling  
- Root-cause analyses of accuracy failures  
- Quality assurance reviews of ACDV / e-OSCAR processes  
- Six Sigma / Lean Six Sigma project documents touching on dispute accuracy  
- Internal "lessons learned" memos following CFPB consent orders  
- Compliance management system effectiveness reviews  
- Internal scorecards on reinvestigation accuracy  
  
### Sample Challenge Language  
  
> TEMPLATE - Reply on Self-Critical Analysis:  
>  
> Defendant's invocation of a "self-critical analysis privilege" fails as a matter of law. The Supreme Court in *Univ. of Pennsylvania v. EEOC*, 493 U.S. 182 (1990), counseled strongly against judicial creation of new evidentiary privileges. No federal court of appeals has recognized a self-critical analysis privilege in the FCRA context, and the great weight of district court authority rejects it. *See, e.g., Reichhold Chems., Inc. v. Textron, Inc.*, 157 F.R.D. 522 (N.D. Fla. 1994). Defendant's internal audits of its reinvestigation processes are exactly the kind of material Sec. 1681i puts at issue: they speak directly to the reasonableness of the procedures Congress required Defendant to follow.

## D. Work Product in FCRA Cases  
  
### The Anticipation-of-Litigation Test  
  
Rule 26(b)(3) protects materials "prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial." Courts apply variants of the "because of" test articulated in *United States v. Adlman*, 134 F.3d 1194, 1202 (2d Cir. 1998): a document is work product if, "in light of the nature of the document and the factual situation in the particular case, the document can fairly be said to have been prepared or obtained because of the prospect of litigation."  
  
### Ordinary vs. Opinion Work Product  
  
Ordinary work product (factual material gathered) can be overcome by a showing of substantial need plus inability to obtain the substantial equivalent without undue hardship. Opinion work product (mental impressions, legal theories) enjoys near-absolute protection. Rule 26(b)(3)(B).  
  
### Common FCRA Disputes  
  
The hardest fights:  
  
- **Dispute-handling notes** during reinvestigation. Generally NOT work product because they are created in the ordinary course of statutory compliance under Sec. 1681i, not in anticipation of litigation. See *Henson v. CSC Credit Servs.*, 29 F.3d 280, 287 (7th Cir. 1994) (reinvestigation duty is ordinary course).  
- **Post-dispute investigation files.** Mixed. The reinvestigation itself is ordinary course; a parallel litigation-focused investigation by counsel may be protected.  
- **Complaint-response documents.** Once a Cushman/MIDA-style dispute letter ripens into actual litigation threat (e.g., a demand letter from counsel), subsequent investigation files may qualify. Documents created BEFORE that threshold typically do not.  
  
### The Dual-Purpose Document Problem  
  
Many CRA documents serve both compliance and litigation-preparedness purposes. Courts apply the "primary motivating purpose" or "because of" test. See *Adlman*, 134 F.3d at 1202; *United States v. Roxworthy*, 457 F.3d 590, 593-95 (6th Cir. 2006). FCRA-specific tip: the existence of a parallel statutory compliance obligation (Sec. 1681i; Sec. 1681s-2(b)) strongly suggests the document would have been created anyway, defeating work product. *See In re Grand Jury Subpoena (Mark Torf/Torf Envtl. Mgmt.)*, 357 F.3d 900, 908 (9th Cir. 2004) (analyzing dual-purpose work product).  
  
### Practical Tip - The Cushman Letter as the Trigger  
  
A common, useful demarcation is the date of the consumer's first dispute letter (in furnisher cases under *Johnson v. MBNA Am. Bank, N.A.*, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004)) or the first attorney correspondence threatening litigation. Documents BEFORE that point are typically ordinary-course compliance materials and not work product. Counsel should push for production of all materials before the trigger date.

## E. Attorney-Client Privilege Issues  
  
### In-House Compliance Counsel  
  
In-house counsel communications are privileged only when counsel acts in a legal, not business, capacity. *In re Vioxx Prods. Liab. Litig.*, 501 F. Supp. 2d 789, 796 (E.D. La. 2007); *United States v. Chevron Corp.*, 1996 WL 264769, at *4 (N.D. Cal. May 30, 1996). Common FCRA fights:  
  
- Compliance officer communications copying counsel: courts ask whether the primary purpose was legal advice. *In re Kellogg Brown & Root, Inc.*, 756 F.3d 754, 759-60 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (the "a significant purpose" test).  
- Routine ACDV / e-OSCAR escalation procedures generally are not privileged simply because counsel was copied.  
  
### Mixed Legal/Business Advice (Upjohn Factors)  
  
The privilege applies to corporate-employee communications where:  
1. Made to corporate counsel acting as such;  
2. At the direction of corporate superiors;  
3. To enable the corporation to obtain legal advice;  
4. Concerning matters within the scope of the employees' corporate duties;  
5. The employees were aware they were being questioned for that purpose; and  
6. Communications were considered confidential.  
*Upjohn*, 449 U.S. at 394-95.  
  
### Waiver - Subject-Matter and Selective Disclosure  
  
Voluntary disclosure of a privileged communication generally waives the privilege as to the communication and, in many courts, as to the entire subject matter. *In re Keeper of Records (Grand Jury Subpoena Addressed to XYZ Corp.)*, 348 F.3d 16, 22-25 (1st Cir. 2003); *In re EchoStar Commc'ns Corp.*, 448 F.3d 1294, 1301-02 (Fed. Cir. 2006). FRE 502(a) limits subject-matter waiver to circumstances where (1) the waiver is intentional, (2) the disclosed and undisclosed communications concern the same subject matter, and (3) fairness requires their consideration together.  
  
### Privileged Communications with Consultants vs. Outside Counsel  
  
Privilege does not extend to communications with non-attorney consultants unless brought within the attorney's umbrella under the *Kovel* doctrine. *United States v. Kovel*, 296 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1961). For FCRA cases this matters when CRAs use outside accuracy consultants or vendor audit firms.

## F. Drafting Privilege Log Entries  
  
### Sample Entry - Table Form  
  
| Bates | Date | Author | Recipients | Type | Subject | Privilege | Basis |  
|-------|------|--------|------------|------|---------|-----------|-------|  
| PRIV-0001 | 2024-03-15 | J. Smith, GC | A. Lee, VP Compliance; cc: M. Patel, Outside Counsel | Email | Legal advice re draft response to CFPB consent order obligations on ACDV staffing | A/C; WP | Email seeking and providing legal advice from in-house GC regarding regulatory and litigation exposure on ACDV staffing levels |  
| PRIV-0014 | 2024-04-02 | M. Patel, Outside Counsel | J. Smith, GC | Memo | Litigation risk assessment re anticipated *Smith v. CRA Co.* matter | A/C; Opinion WP | Memo prepared by outside counsel, after demand letter from plaintiff's counsel, analyzing litigation strategy |  
  
### Sample Entry - Narrative Form  
  
> **PRIV-0001 - Email dated March 15, 2024.** From J. Smith (General Counsel) to A. Lee (VP, Compliance), copying M. Patel (outside counsel, Wilson & Daley LLP). Subject line: "Re: ACDV staffing - legal review." Email seeks and provides legal advice regarding the company's regulatory obligations and litigation exposure following the CFPB's 2023 consent order. Privilege: attorney-client; work product. Basis: communication between corporate client, in-house counsel, and outside counsel for the primary purpose of obtaining legal advice in anticipation of litigation.  
  
### Common Defects  
  
Defective entries include those that:  
  
- List only the privilege ("A/C") without describing the document or the basis;  
- Describe subject matter as "communications regarding company business" or "internal review";  
- Omit recipients (especially cc'd attorneys, the linchpin of the privilege claim);  
- Fail to identify all email-chain participants;  
- Group hundreds of documents under one entry without categorical justification;  
- Apply work product without identifying the litigation anticipated and the date the anticipation arose.  
  
### Templates for Common FCRA Document Types  
  
**Dispute Investigation Memo (typically NOT privileged):**  
  
> Entry should describe the document with awareness that the Sec. 1681i reinvestigation is statutorily mandated, ordinary-course conduct. A bare work product claim is likely to fail unless the memo was authored by or at the direction of counsel preparing for an identified litigation matter.  
  
**Internal Audit Report:**  
  
> "Internal audit report dated [date] prepared by [internal audit function]." Audit reports are generally discoverable; self-critical analysis privilege is not recognized. Attorney-client privilege applies only where the audit was conducted at the direction of counsel for the primary purpose of legal advice.  
  
**Communications with Regulators (CSI):**  
  
> Identify the regulator, the date, the regulatory framework (12 C.F.R. Sec. 1070.40 et seq. for CFPB), and indicate whether *Touhy* permission has been sought.  
  
**Communications with Outside Counsel:**  
  
> Always list outside counsel's firm and role. Identify each non-attorney recipient and explain why they are within the privileged circle (e.g., "Senior compliance officer functioning as control group member under *Upjohn*").

## G. Privilege Challenges  
  
### Meet-and-Confer  
  
Most courts require a Rule 37(a)(1) meet-and-confer certification before any motion. In FCRA cases, the meet-and-confer letter should:  
  
1. Identify each contested log entry by Bates;  
2. State the specific deficiency;  
3. Cite controlling authority on the privilege element challenged;  
4. Request supplementation, revised entries, or production;  
5. Propose in camera review as a fallback;  
6. Set a reasonable deadline (commonly 14 days).  
  
### Motion to Compel - Rule 37  
  
The motion should track each contested entry. Courts disfavor undifferentiated challenges. A useful structure:  
  
- Introduction and standard of review;  
- The privilege claimed and its elements;  
- Why the log is facially deficient (Rule 26(b)(5));  
- Why the privilege does not apply on the merits (Sec. C, D, E above);  
- Request for in camera review;  
- Request for sanctions or waiver finding under *Burlington Northern*.  
  
### In Camera Review  
  
Courts may inspect contested documents in camera. *Kerr v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 426 U.S. 394, 405-06 (1976). To trigger in camera review, the challenger must make some showing of a factual basis for doubt. *United States v. Zolin*, 491 U.S. 554, 572 (1989) (crime-fraud context).  
  
### Briefing Strategy  
  
For FCRA plaintiffs, the most successful briefing strategies:  
  
1. Frame the dispute as ordinary course statutory compliance, not litigation prep;  
2. Emphasize the Sec. 1681i and Sec. 1681s-2(b) statutory obligations as the source of the documents;  
3. Note that no circuit has adopted self-critical analysis privilege in FCRA cases;  
4. Quote the log entries verbatim and show their deficiency;  
5. Request fee-shifting under Rule 37(a)(5)(A).  
  
### Sample Motion-to-Compel Language  
  
> TEMPLATE - Motion to Compel:  
>  
> Defendant's privilege log entries Nos. 14-72, 88-104, and 119 fail Rule 26(b)(5)'s requirement to describe withheld documents "in a manner that ... will enable other parties to assess the claim." Each entry recites only "internal compliance review" and "attorney-client privilege," without identifying recipients beyond a single name, the legal advice purportedly sought, or the corporate role of the participants. The Ninth Circuit has held that a privilege log so inadequate constitutes waiver. *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 408 F.3d 1142, 1149 (9th Cir. 2005).  
>  
> Even setting aside the log's facial defects, the documents are not privileged. Defendant's reinvestigation files were generated to satisfy its statutory duty under 15 U.S.C. Sec. 1681i, not in anticipation of litigation. *See United States v. Adlman*, 134 F.3d 1194 (2d Cir. 1998). The "primary purpose" test the controlling authority requires is not met. Defendant's separate claim of self-critical analysis privilege fares no better: no court of appeals has recognized that privilege, and *Univ. of Pennsylvania v. EEOC*, 493 U.S. 182 (1990), counsels strongly against creating one.

## H. Subject-Matter Waiver  
  
### When Affirmative Use Waives  
  
Subject-matter waiver doctrine prevents parties from using privilege as both shield and sword. *In re Kidder Peabody Sec. Litig.*, 168 F.R.D. 459, 469-70 (S.D.N.Y. 1996). Affirmatively putting privileged advice at issue (advice-of-counsel defense; reliance on counsel's investigation in defending willfulness; testimony that "we consulted our lawyers and they said X") generally waives the privilege as to the entire subject matter.  
  
### Common FCRA Scenario - The Compliance Officer's "We Consulted Counsel"  
  
Suppose the defendant's compliance officer testifies, "I consulted our attorneys before we adopted these reinvestigation procedures." If that testimony is offered to defend against willfulness under Sec. 1681n, the answer is almost certainly subject-matter waiver. *See Cox v. Adm'r U.S. Steel & Carnegie*, 17 F.3d 1386, 1418-19 (11th Cir. 1994); *Bittaker v. Woodford*, 331 F.3d 715, 720 (9th Cir. 2003) (en banc) (the "fairness doctrine").  
  
To avoid waiver, defendants typically must either disclaim reliance on counsel as a defense or produce the underlying advice.  
  
### Strategy  
  
**Plaintiff:** Pin down the witness in deposition. Ask whether the company relied on legal advice in adopting the procedures. If yes, propound follow-up requests for all communications, opinions, and memoranda on the subject. Move to compel and request waiver findings.  
  
**Defendant:** Train witnesses NOT to volunteer references to counsel unless reliance on counsel is to be affirmatively asserted. Decide early whether to assert advice-of-counsel; if not, instruct witnesses accordingly.

## I. Spoliation and Privilege  
  
Privilege cannot be invoked to shield destruction of relevant records. A party may not first destroy and then claim privilege as a basis for non-production. See *Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC*, 229 F.R.D. 422 (S.D.N.Y. 2004). When documents responsive to a discovery request are destroyed and the party simultaneously claims they "would have been privileged," courts may impose adverse inferences or other sanctions under Rule 37(e) for ESI, or under the court's inherent power for non-ESI.  
  
The plaintiff should propound an early Rule 34 request specifically targeting:  
  
- Litigation hold notices issued (and their dates);  
- Document retention/destruction schedules;  
- Confirmation that holds were enforced for known custodians.  
  
If destroyed-and-privilege-claimed documents exist, an adverse inference is appropriate. *Residential Funding Corp. v. DeGeorge Fin. Corp.*, 306 F.3d 99, 107 (2d Cir. 2002).

## J. Federal Rule of Evidence 502  
  
### FRE 502(b) - Inadvertent Disclosure  
  
Inadvertent disclosure in a federal proceeding does not waive privilege if:  
  
1. The disclosure was inadvertent;  
2. The holder took reasonable steps to prevent disclosure; AND  
3. The holder promptly took reasonable steps to rectify the error.  
  
Courts apply a multi-factor analysis. *Mt. Hawley Ins. Co. v. Felman Prod., Inc.*, 271 F.R.D. 125, 132-37 (S.D. W. Va. 2010).  
  
### FRE 502(d) - Clawback Orders  
  
The single most powerful tool: a court order under FRE 502(d) makes a disclosure non-waiving in any federal or state proceeding, regardless of the steps taken. Best practice in any FCRA case involving substantial ESI is to negotiate and enter a 502(d) order at the outset.  
  
### Sample 502(d) Order Language  
  
> TEMPLATE - Stipulated FRE 502(d) Order:  
>  
> 1. **Non-Waiver of Privilege.** Pursuant to Federal Rule of Evidence 502(d), the production of any document subject to attorney-client privilege, work-product protection, or any other applicable privilege or protection in this litigation, whether inadvertent or otherwise, shall not constitute a waiver of any privilege or protection, in this litigation or in any other federal or state proceeding.  
>  
> 2. **Clawback.** A producing party may demand return or destruction of any inadvertently or otherwise produced privileged or protected material by written notice identifying the material with reasonable particularity. Within seven (7) days of receipt of such notice, the receiving party shall (a) return or certify destruction of all copies of the identified material; (b) not use, disclose, or rely on the material; and (c) make reasonable efforts to retrieve copies from any third parties.  
>  
> 3. **Challenge.** A party receiving a clawback demand may challenge the privilege claim by motion within fourteen (14) days. Pending resolution, the receiving party shall not use the material for any purpose.  
>  
> 4. **Sequestration.** During any challenge, the material shall be sequestered and submitted for in camera review.  
>  
> 5. **No Subject-Matter Waiver.** Production under this Order shall not effect a subject-matter waiver under FRE 502(a) absent the producing party's intentional use of the material at issue.
