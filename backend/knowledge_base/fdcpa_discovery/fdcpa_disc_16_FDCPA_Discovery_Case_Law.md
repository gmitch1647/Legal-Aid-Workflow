\#\# A. Call recording production — discoverability, retention, spoliation  
  
Call recordings are the single most probative category of FDCPA evidence (harassment under § 1692d, false statements under § 1692e, time-of-call violations under § 1692c(a)(1)). They are presumptively discoverable as non-privileged business records under Rule 26(b)(1), and Regulation F now imposes a record-retention floor.  
  
\#\#\# 1. Regulation F record-retention floor — 12 C.F.R. § 1006.100  
  
Although not a "case," the CFPB's Regulation F (effective Nov. 30, 2021) is the dispositive starting point for any call-recording dispute. \*\*12 C.F.R. § 1006.100\*\* requires a debt collector that records telephone calls in connection with the collection of a debt to retain the recording for \*\*three years\*\* after the date of the call. Pre-Regulation F retention disputes typically applied state UCC, common-law spoliation, or the catch-all that records "reasonably anticipated to be relevant to litigation" must be preserved (see \*Zubulake\*, below). Why it matters: any destruction of a call recording made within three years of the call now violates a federal regulatory floor and supports a spoliation inference independent of when litigation was reasonably anticipated.  
  
\#\#\# 2. \*Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC\*, 220 F.R.D. 212 (S.D.N.Y. 2003) (Zubulake IV); 229 F.R.D. 422 (S.D.N.Y. 2004) (Zubulake V)  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Securities trader sued former employer for gender discrimination; UBS failed to preserve emails after litigation became reasonably foreseeable. \*\*Holding.\*\* Judge Scheindlin laid out the modern ESI-preservation framework: the duty to preserve arises when litigation is reasonably anticipated; the party must issue a written litigation hold, communicate it to relevant custodians, and police compliance at the system level. \*\*Why it matters.\*\* Zubulake remains the most-cited authority on the duty to preserve electronically stored information, including call recordings. It is the doctrinal anchor for FDCPA spoliation motions where a collector "happened" to lose a recording after dispute or pre-suit demand.  
  
\#\#\# 3. \*Reichert v. National Credit Systems, Inc.\*, 531 F.3d 1002 (9th Cir. 2008)  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Apartment tenant sued debt collector for trying to collect unauthorized fees; collector asserted bona-fide-error defense based on its "procedures." \*\*Holding.\*\* The Ninth Circuit held that the bona-fide-error defense requires "more than a mere assertion" that procedures exist; the collector must show procedures reasonably adapted to avoid the specific type of error at issue. \*\*Why it matters.\*\* \*Reichert\* is the principal driver of plaintiff-side discovery into the collector's policies, training scripts, and call-monitoring records — including call recordings used in QA review. Discovery cannot be cut off short of the actual procedures the collector says it follows.  
  
\#\#\# 4. \*In re Plastics Additives Antitrust Litig.\*, 2004 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 23989 (E.D. Pa. Aug. 6, 2004) \`\[VERIFY CITATION\]\`  
  
Frequently cited for the proposition that selectively-recorded conversations create asymmetric production obligations. Cited in FDCPA secondary literature for the principle that if a collector records \*some\* calls (typically inbound or escalated calls), every recorded call within the limitations period is discoverable. Verify the docket/cite before relying.

\#\# B. Form letter / template library production — class-certification fuel  
  
Most FDCPA class actions are built on uniform written communications. The template library — every dunning letter, validation notice, settlement letter, and email template used during the class period — is the single most valuable production.
