\# FDCPA Discovery Strategy Overview  
  
\*\*Topic:\*\* Discovery strategy in Fair Debt Collection Practices Act litigation  
\*\*Primary statutes:\*\* 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692-1692p (FDCPA); 12 C.F.R. Part 1006 (Regulation F)  
\*\*FDCPA sections covered:\*\* §§ 1692a, 1692b, 1692c, 1692d, 1692e, 1692f, 1692g, 1692i, 1692k  
\*\*Reg F cross-references:\*\* 12 C.F.R. §§ 1006.6, 1006.14, 1006.18, 1006.22, 1006.26, 1006.30, 1006.34, 1006.38, 1006.42  
\*\*Procedural authority:\*\* Fed. R. Civ. P. 26, 30, 33, 34, 36, 45  
\*\*Last updated:\*\* May 16, 2026  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 1. Purpose and Orientation  
  
FDCPA discovery is highly targeted, because the statute itself is highly granular. Unlike open-ended consumer-fraud actions, FDCPA cases turn on discrete acts of communication, the contents of standardized form letters, recorded telephone calls, and the collector's procedural posture under Regulation F. Discovery should be designed around (a) the specific subsection alleged, (b) the affirmative defenses the collector is most likely to assert (especially bona fide error, § 1692k(c)), and (c) the evidentiary needs at trial and on summary judgment under the "least sophisticated consumer" or "unsophisticated consumer" standard depending on circuit. See \*Jacobson v. Healthcare Fin. Servs., Inc.\*, 516 F.3d 85 (2d Cir. 2008); \*Gammon v. GC Servs. Ltd. P'ship\*, 27 F.3d 1254 (7th Cir. 1994); \*Bartlett v. Heibl\*, 128 F.3d 497 (7th Cir. 1997).  
  
This file frames the strategy. Specific written-discovery vehicles, deposition outlines, and motion practice are addressed in the companion files in this folder (see "See Also" at end).

\#\# 2. Discovery Objectives by Claim Type  
  
\#\#\# 2.1 § 1692e — False, Deceptive, or Misleading Representations  
The most-litigated section. Discovery objectives:  
\- Obtain the \*\*exact form letter or template\*\* sent, in native format, plus the universe of letters in the template library during the relevant period.  
\- Identify the \*\*drafter, reviewer, and approver\*\* of the letter (compliance, legal, operations).  
\- Capture every variable and conditional field merged into the consumer's letter — the printed letter is the artifact, but the merge logic is the violation.  
\- Investigate any \*\*interest or fee accrual disclosure\*\* under \*Avila v. Riexinger & Assocs., LLC\*, 817 F.3d 72 (2d Cir. 2016) (collector must disclose that balance may increase due to interest and fees, or use safe-harbor language).  
\- For "amount of the debt" disputes, pull the ledger, principal/interest/fee breakdown, and any post-charge-off changes.  
\- For attorney-collector letters, develop the level of attorney involvement under \*Clomon v. Jackson\*, 988 F.2d 1314 (2d Cir. 1993), and \*Miller v. Wolpoff & Abramson, L.L.P.\*, 321 F.3d 292 (2d Cir. 2003).  
  
\#\#\# 2.2 § 1692g — Validation Notice / Validation Rights  
Reg F supplies the modern template (12 C.F.R. § 1006.34, the "Model Validation Notice"). Discovery should:  
\- Confirm whether the collector used the Reg F model form or a non-model form (loss of safe harbor under § 1006.34(d)(2)).  
\- Identify itemization date (current balance date / charge-off / last statement / transaction date / judgment, § 1006.34(b)(3)).  
\- Pull the e-delivery procedures under § 1006.42(c) if the notice was sent electronically.  
\- Track every dispute received and the collector's response (cessation of collection until verification, § 1692g(b); § 1006.38).  
\- Determine if "overshadowing" occurred — collection demands within the 30-day window that contradict validation rights. \*Bartlett v. Heibl\*, 128 F.3d 497 (7th Cir. 1997).  
  
\#\#\# 2.3 § 1692c — Communications in Connection with Debt Collection  
\- Times, places, and circumstances of communications (8 a.m. – 9 p.m. local rule, § 1692c(a)(1)).  
\- Communication at work after notice it was prohibited (§ 1692c(a)(3)).  
\- Cease-and-desist letters and post-cease communications (§ 1692c(c)).  
\- Communications with third parties beyond § 1692b acquisition (§ 1692c(b)).  
\- Communications with represented consumers (§ 1692c(a)(2)) — discover when the collector learned of representation and how the system was supposed to flag it.  
  
\#\#\# 2.4 § 1692d — Harassment or Abuse  
\- Call frequency data: dialer records, manual call logs, "right-party-contact" disposition codes.  
\- Reg F's 7-in-7 presumption (§ 1006.14(b)) — calls per particular debt within 7 consecutive days; within 7 days after a telephone conversation.  
\- Recordings of allegedly abusive calls.  
\- Profanity/threat language and supervisor escalation records.  
  
\#\#\# 2.5 § 1692f — Unfair or Unconscionable Practices  
\- Unauthorized fees (§ 1692f(1)) — discover the contract or statutory authority for every charge.  
\- Post-dated check or check threats (§ 1692f(3)-(4)).  
\- Postcards and identifying envelope language (§ 1692f(7)-(8)). \*Douglass v. Convergent Outsourcing\*, 765 F.3d 299 (3d Cir. 2014) (account number visible through envelope window).  
  
\#\#\# 2.6 § 1692b — Acquisition of Location Information  
\- Scripts and call recordings for third-party calls.  
\- Disclosure that collector is confirming or correcting location information only.  
\- "Once" rule under § 1692b(3) — discovery on repeat third-party contacts.  
  
\#\#\# 2.7 § 1692i — Venue / Suit in Distant Forum  
\- Where suit was filed vs. where consumer signed contract or resides.  
\- The collector's filing-venue selection policy and the data fields used to route filings.

\#\# 3. Targets of Discovery  
  
\#\#\# 3.1 Debt Collectors (§ 1692a(6))  
The principal target. Demand corporate structure, licensing, the collection-management system (CMS) data export for the consumer, and the policy library.  
  
\#\#\# 3.2 Debt Buyers  
Discovery should always include the \*\*chain of title\*\*: bill of sale, account-level data file, purchase and sale agreement (PSA), and seller representations and warranties. Many PSAs contain disclaimers that contradict the buyer's later representations about the debt. Chain-of-title problems can drive both FDCPA § 1692e(2) (false amount/character/legal status) and § 1692e(10) claims. See \*Midland Funding, LLC v. Johnson\*, 137 S. Ct. 1407 (2017) (filing time-barred proof of claim in bankruptcy was not "false, deceptive, or misleading" under the FDCPA — context-dependent; chain-of-title disputes remain very much alive under § 1692e in non-bankruptcy contexts).  
  
\#\#\# 3.3 Collection Law Firms  
Lawyers who "regularly" engage in debt collection are subject to the FDCPA. \*Heintz v. Jenkins\*, 514 U.S. 291 (1995). Discovery into the firm should probe (a) volume and percentage of collection litigation, (b) attorney-review procedures for demand letters, (c) the document-assembly systems used, and (d) the reliance on creditor-supplied data. \*Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA\*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010) (mistake of law is not a bona fide error).  
  
\#\#\# 3.4 Original Creditors  
Generally outside the FDCPA's reach — \*Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.\*, 137 S. Ct. 1718 (2017) (a person who buys defaulted debt for its own account is not necessarily a "debt collector"). But discovery from original creditors may be available where:  
\- The creditor uses a "name other than" its own (§ 1692a(6) flush language).  
\- The creditor is a "principal purpose" debt collector (open issue post-\*Henson\*).  
\- Third-party non-party discovery is needed under Rule 45 to source the underlying account records and any seller representations.  
  
\#\#\# 3.5 Vendors and Service Providers  
Letter vendors (e.g., print-and-mail vendors), dialer vendors, e-OSCAR/ACDV subscribers, skip-tracing vendors. Use Rule 45 subpoenas when the collector claims it does not possess the data.

\#\# 4. Taking Apart the Bona Fide Error Defense  
  
The bona fide error defense, 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(c), requires the collector to show by a preponderance that the violation was (1) \*\*not intentional\*\*, (2) resulted from a \*\*bona fide error\*\*, and (3) occurred \*\*notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid the error\*\*. \*Jerman\* held that mistake of law is unavailable; \*Reichert v. Nat'l Credit Sys., Inc.\*, 531 F.3d 1002 (9th Cir. 2008), and \*Edwards v. Niagara Credit Solutions\*, 584 F.3d 1350 (11th Cir. 2009), describe the rigor of the "procedures" prong.  
  
Discovery roadmap to defeat BFE:  
\- \*\*Procedures\*\*: written policies, the date adopted, the date last revised, who owns them. The defense fails without contemporaneous written procedures \*of the specific kind that would have caught this error\*. \*Johnson v. Riddle\*, 443 F.3d 723 (10th Cir. 2006).  
\- \*\*Training\*\*: training materials, attendance records, quizzes, and refresh cadence. Untrained or undertrained staff defeats "reasonably adapted."  
\- \*\*Audits\*\*: QA scorecards, call monitoring sample sizes, internal audit reports, and remediation.  
\- \*\*Recurrence\*\*: prior consumer complaints, CFPB complaint downloads, state AG correspondence, NMLS reportable events. Repeated occurrence of the same error undermines reasonableness. \*Kort v. Diversified Collection Servs.\*, 394 F.3d 530 (7th Cir. 2005).  
\- \*\*Root cause for this specific violation\*\*: incident reports, post-mortems, ticket tracking, change-management logs.  
\- \*\*Vendor reliance\*\*: if the collector blames a vendor (e.g., letter vendor printed wrong language), depose the vendor and probe the SLAs/QA.  
  
A targeted RFP set should request "All documents reflecting the procedures the defendant contends satisfy 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(c)" — locking the defense to its produced documents.

\#\# 5. Sequencing  
  
A typical FDCPA discovery sequence:  
  
1\. \*\*Rule 26(f) conference\*\*: scope of ESI, call recordings, retention, format of production, protective order.  
2\. \*\*Rule 26(a)(1) initial disclosures\*\* (see fdcpa\_disc\_02): demand bona fide error procedures, training materials, the account file, and insurance policies.  
3\. \*\*First-wave written discovery\*\*: interrogatories (fdcpa\_disc\_03), RFPs (fdcpa\_disc\_04), and selective RFAs targeting the form letter, recordings, and policies.  
4\. \*\*Document review\*\* and meet-and-confer over deficiencies.  
5\. \*\*Fact depositions\*\*: front-line collector who handled the account; compliance officer; QA manager.  
6\. \*\*Rule 30(b)(6) deposition\*\* of the collector on bona fide error procedures, Reg F implementation, call recordings architecture, and letter approval chain.  
7\. \*\*Third-party discovery\*\* under Rule 45 to vendors, the original creditor, the seller, the CRA (for furnishing-related claims).  
8\. \*\*Expert discovery\*\* under Rule 26(a)(2) where damages (especially emotional distress, statutory cap analysis for class) or industry-standard procedure are contested.  
9\. \*\*Class discovery\*\* (if applicable) on the size of the recipient population, net worth (§ 1692k(a)(2)(B) class cap), and commonality.  
  
Sequencing tip: pull recordings and letters \*\*first\*\* so depositions are document-driven.

\#\# 6. Common Defense Objections  
  
\- \*\*Relevance\*\* — overbroad with respect to other consumers. Counter: form-letter claims and BFE both turn on systemic conduct; \*Foti v. NCO Fin. Sys., Inc.\*, 424 F. Supp. 2d 643 (S.D.N.Y. 2006).  
\- \*\*Proportionality\*\* (Rule 26(b)(1)) — especially in statutory-only $1,000 cases. Counter: amount in controversy is not the sole factor; FDCPA serves a remedial purpose, and the issues at stake are not measured solely by statutory damages. See \*Oppenheimer Fund, Inc. v. Sanders\*, 437 U.S. 340 (1978) (relevance liberally construed under the prior rule); \*Henry v. Morgan's Hotel Grp., Inc.\*, No. 15-cv-1789, 2016 WL 303114 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 25, 2016) (proportionality factors).  
\- \*\*Work product\*\* — letter approval chains involving in-house counsel. Counter: factual underpinnings are not protected; ordinary-course business documents created in compliance review are not made in anticipation of litigation.  
\- \*\*Trade secret\*\* — for scripts, dialer logic, vendor agreements. Counter: a stipulated protective order resolves this; the FDCPA cannot be enforced without the very materials at issue.  
\- \*\*Undue burden\*\* ��� particularly with call recordings and ESI. Counter: cost-shifting is the exception; the collector built the system and bears the burden.

\#\# 7. Standing-Related Discovery Post-\*TransUnion v. Ramirez\*  
  
\*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez\*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), tightened Article III standing for statutory violations. While \*TransUnion\* arose under the FCRA, defendants routinely import it into FDCPA cases, especially after \*Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Mgmt. Servs., Inc.\*, 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc) (no standing where letter vendor disclosure did not cause concrete harm). Discovery into standing:  
\- Consumer's actual concrete harm: confusion, treatment with provider, missed deadlines, medical/therapeutic records, contemporaneous communications with family.  
\- Whether consumer paid, was sued, or suffered downstream credit consequences.  
\- Subjective vs. objective intrusion-upon-seclusion analogues for § 1692d harassment claims.  
\- Defendant cannot use standing as both a sword (dismiss the case) and a shield (refuse discovery into the underlying facts).  
  
In class cases, discovery must support a \*typicality\* and \*commonality\* finding consistent with \*TransUnion\*'s holding that every class member must have standing for damages.

\#\# 8. Class vs. Individual Discovery  
  
Class discovery in FDCPA cases tends to focus on:  
\- \*\*Letter populations\*\*: the universe of consumers who received the form letter at issue, by template ID and date range. This is usually a CMS query.  
\- \*\*Defendant net worth\*\* for the 1% statutory class cap, § 1692k(a)(2)(B). Pull audited financials and tax returns under protective order.  
\- \*\*Numerosity\*\* and \*\*ascertainability\*\* data — typically downloaded from the CMS.  
\- \*\*Bifurcation\*\*: defendants often press to defer merits discovery until class cert. Resist when the merits and class theory overlap (which is the norm in form-letter cases).  
  
Individual cases focus on the consumer's specific account, communication record, damages, and the procedures applicable to that specific letter or call.

\#\# 9. Statutory-Damages-Only Cases and Proportionality  
  
When the plaintiff disclaims actual damages and seeks only the $1,000 statutory cap, defendants press a sharp proportionality objection. Plaintiffs should:  
\- Frame issues at stake broadly: deterrence, public-policy enforcement, attorneys' fees.  
\- Limit interrogatories to a defensible number; use RFAs to streamline.  
\- Pre-narrow ESI custodians and time periods.  
\- Use targeted Rule 30(b)(6) topics rather than long fact depositions.  
\- Be ready with a \*Bell v. Wong\*-style proportionality argument: "the FDCPA is a private attorney general statute; refusing reasonable discovery on proportionality grounds would render the statute unenforceable."

\#\# 10. FDCPA-Specific Issues to Probe in Every Case  
  
\- \*\*Call recordings retention\*\*: how long, format, indexed by what fields, accessible to whom, deleted under what schedule. Spoliation is a real risk; serve a litigation-hold demand at the outset.  
\- \*\*Reg F compliance procedures\*\*: written procedures for §§ 1006.6, 1006.14, 1006.18, 1006.22, 1006.26, 1006.34, 1006.38, 1006.42. Date of adoption, training, audits.  
\- \*\*Letter approval chains\*\*: drafter, legal/compliance reviewer, signoff authority, version-control system, the live template at the time of the consumer's letter.  
\- \*\*Time-barred debt detection\*\*: under Reg F § 1006.26, collectors may not sue or threaten to sue on time-barred debt. Discover the SOL-tagging logic, the data fields feeding it, and what happens when the SOL date is missing.  
\- \*\*Debt buyer chain of title\*\*: PSA, bill of sale, account-level data file, prior-owner reps and warranties, "as-is" disclaimers, indemnification provisions. Pull the full chain back to the original creditor.  
\- \*\*Hunstein-style vendor disclosures\*\*: identify every third-party recipient of consumer information (letter vendors, skip tracers, dialers, analytics vendors, BPO).  
\- \*\*Limited-content messages\*\* under § 1006.2(j): scripts, voicemail templates, training on the "limited content" boundaries.  
\- \*\*Electronic communications\*\*: e-mail and text disclosures (Reg F § 1006.6(d)); the "reasonable procedures" to avoid disclosure to third parties; opt-out mechanics.

\#\# 11. Practical Pointers  
  
\- Begin every FDCPA case with a litigation-hold letter naming \*\*call recordings\*\*, \*\*the letter template library\*\*, \*\*CMS account notes\*\*, \*\*e-mail of named collectors and supervisors\*\*, \*\*policies and training materials\*\*, and \*\*vendor agreements\*\*.  
\- Lock in the \*\*exact form letter\*\* as Exhibit 1 to your first set of RFAs, with an admission as to authenticity and dispatch.  
\- Use \*\*CMS field dictionaries\*\* — the data dictionary explains every disposition code; without it, the disposition column is uninterpretable.  
\- Use \*\*CFPB consent orders\*\* (publicly available) against the same defendant to anticipate procedural deficiencies. Pull the CFPB complaint database for the defendant for the relevant period.  
\- For class cases, plan for an early \*\*summary-judgment-on-liability\*\* motion to drive settlement before extensive class-cert work.
