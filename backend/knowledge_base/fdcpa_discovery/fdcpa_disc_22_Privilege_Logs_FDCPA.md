\# FDCPA Discovery — Privilege Logs and Challenges  
  
\*\*Topic:\*\* Privilege logs and privilege challenges in FDCPA cases.  
\*\*FRCP cross-references:\*\* Rules 26(b)(1), 26(b)(3), 26(b)(5), 30(c)(2), 37(a)(3)(B)(iv); FRE 501, 502.  
\*\*FDCPA cross-references:\*\* 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692k(c) (bona fide error); applied through \*Jerman v. Carlisle\*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010).  
\*\*Last updated:\*\* May 16, 2026.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 1. Rule 26(b)(5) — The Privilege-Log Requirement  
  
FRCP 26(b)(5)(A) provides that when a party withholds information under a privilege or work-product protection, the party must:  
  
\> (i) expressly make the claim; and  
\> (ii) describe the nature of the documents, communications, or tangible things not produced or disclosed — and do so in a manner that, without revealing information itself privileged or protected, will enable other parties to assess the claim.  
  
The standard log entry must — at minimum — identify:  
  
\- Bates number or other unique identifier.  
\- Date.  
\- Author / sender (with title and role).  
\- Recipients (with titles; distinguish "To," "Cc," "Bcc").  
\- Document type (e-mail, memo, letter, draft pleading, draft policy, voicemail, text, etc.).  
\- Subject matter described at sufficient detail to assess privilege without revealing privileged content.  
\- Privilege(s) asserted (attorney-client; work product; other).  
\- For work product, the case / matter prepared in anticipation of and the litigation theory.  
  
A bare "communication with counsel regarding legal advice" is insufficient. \*See In re Grand Jury Investigation\*, 974 F.2d 1068, 1071 (9th Cir. 1992); \*United States v. Constr. Prods. Research, Inc.\*, 73 F.3d 464, 473 (2d Cir. 1996).  
  
Many districts have local rules or standing orders adding fields (cf. S.D.N.Y. Local Civil Rule 26.2; N.D. Cal. ESI Guidelines; E.D. Tex. Patent Rules).

\#\# 2. Common Privileges Asserted in FDCPA Cases  
  
\#\#\# 2.1 Attorney-client privilege  
  
The classic \*Upjohn\* corporate-client privilege (\*Upjohn Co. v. United States\*, 449 U.S. 383 (1981)) covers:  
  
\- Communications between the debt collector's in-house counsel and employees within the scope of their corporate duties.  
\- Communications with outside counsel.  
\- Communications by clerical staff transmitting privileged content.  
  
It does \*not\* cover:  
  
\- Business or operational advice from in-house counsel acting in a business role. \*See United States v. Chevron Corp.\*, 1996 WL 264769 (N.D. Cal. 1996).  
\- Communications where counsel is not acting as counsel (e.g., HR or compliance functions performed by non-lawyer compliance officers).  
\- Communications shared with third parties (waiver).  
  
In FDCPA cases involving collection law firms, the "client" is the debt-collector creditor, not the firm itself. Internal firm communications among lawyers about the \*firm's\* compliance (separate from a specific client matter) may not be client communications at all and may be work product only if litigation-anticipated.  
  
\#\#\# 2.2 Work product  
  
FRCP 26(b)(3). Covers documents prepared "in anticipation of litigation or for trial" by or for a party or its representative. Two categories:  
  
\- \*\*Fact work product\*\* — discoverable on showing of substantial need and undue hardship.  
\- \*\*Opinion work product\*\* — mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal theories; near-absolute protection. \*Upjohn\*, 449 U.S. at 401–02.  
  
For debt collectors, \*every\* account is potentially in anticipation of litigation, but the workaday production of validation letters, dispute responses, and collection-notes is not. The test in most circuits is whether the document "can fairly be said to have been prepared or obtained because of the prospect of litigation." \*United States v. Adlman\*, 134 F.3d 1194, 1202 (2d Cir. 1998).  
  
\#\#\# 2.3 Self-critical analysis — \*\*rejected\*\*  
  
The self-critical-analysis privilege would protect internal audits and root-cause analyses from disclosure. It has been \*\*rejected\*\* by most federal courts in the FDCPA / consumer-finance context. \*See Dowling v. Am. Hawaii Cruises, Inc.\*, 971 F.2d 423, 426 (9th Cir. 1992); \*In re Diet Drugs\*, 1999 WL 124414 (E.D. Pa. 1999); \*Wade v. Wash. Metro. Area Transit Auth.\*, 2006 WL 89829 (D.D.C. 2006); \*Spencer Sav. Bank, SLA v. Excell Mortg. Corp.\*, 960 F. Supp. 835 (D.N.J. 1997). The Supreme Court has never recognized it; courts that have considered it for compliance audits routinely refuse. \*Compare\* \*Bredice v. Doctors Hosp., Inc.\*, 50 F.R.D. 249 (D.D.C. 1970) (medical peer review; very narrow).  
  
In an FDCPA case, expect to see compliance audits, internal investigations of collector misconduct, root-cause analyses of complaint trends, and QA-failure reviews. These are routinely discoverable when relevance is established under FRCP 26(b)(1).
