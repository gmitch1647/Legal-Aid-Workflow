\# FDCPA Discovery: Protective Orders  
  
\*\*Topic:\*\* Protective orders in FDCPA litigation — confidentiality of call recordings, customer information, commercial documents, and complaint records  
\*\*Cross-references:\*\* Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(c); 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692c, 1681 (FCRA cross-reference for furnisher records); state wiretap statutes; 18 U.S.C. § 2510 (federal wiretap reference)  
\*\*Last updated:\*\* May 16, 2026  
\*\*Source discipline:\*\* Public-domain primary sources only (FRCP, published federal opinions, CFPB guidance)  
  
\---  
  
\#\# Overview  
  
Protective orders in FDCPA cases differ in emphasis from those in FCRA litigation. The FCRA context features extensive contests over algorithm and matching-logic trade secrets; the FDCPA context tilts toward (a) consumer privacy in call recordings, account notes, and complaint files; (b) ordinary commercial sensitivity in purchase agreements, vendor contracts, and pricing terms; and (c) limited and largely meritless trade-secret assertions over call scripts and form letter content.  
  
Rule 26(c)(1) authorizes a protective order on a showing of "good cause." The party seeking protection bears the burden. \*Foltz v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.\*, 331 F.3d 1122, 1130 (9th Cir. 2003); \*Pansy v. Borough of Stroudsburg\*, 23 F.3d 772, 786 (3d Cir. 1994). Stipulated protective orders are routine, but courts must still find good cause to enter them. \*Public Citizen v. Liggett Grp., Inc.\*, 858 F.2d 775, 789 (1st Cir. 1988).  
  
This memorandum addresses the categories of FDCPA discovery materials commonly subject to protective-order designation and supplies model tiered-PO language for individual and class actions.

\#\# Categories of FDCPA Materials Warranting Confidentiality  
  
\#\#\# 1. Call Recordings and Telephone Logs  
  
Call recordings often include the consumer's voice, social-security number, account numbers, employer information, and discussion of financial hardship — sometimes also medical or family-status information. Many recordings include the voices of consumer-identified third parties (spouses, family members, employers) who never consented to litigation use.  
  
\*\*Good-cause showing.\*\* Sensitive personal information of plaintiff and of non-parties; risk of public dissemination of voice recordings. State wiretap law in two-party-consent states (e.g., California, Pennsylvania, Florida) supplies independent privacy interest.  
  
\*\*Typical protections.\*\* Designate recordings "Confidential — Subject to Protective Order." Limit listening to outside counsel and retained experts. Require redaction of non-party voices, SSNs, and account numbers if recordings are used at deposition or filed publicly. \*See, e.g., In re Cooper Tire & Rubber Co.\*, 568 F.3d 1180 (10th Cir. 2009) (privacy as good cause).  
  
\#\#\# 2. Customer Information, SSNs, and Account Numbers  
  
The collector's account file typically includes the consumer's SSN, account number, date of birth, mailing addresses, telephone numbers, employer, payment history, and other PII.  
  
\*\*Good-cause showing.\*\* Federal Rule 5.2 protections; Gramm-Leach-Bliley financial-privacy framework (15 U.S.C. § 6801 et seq.) as background norm.  
  
\*\*Typical protections.\*\* Mandatory Rule 5.2 redaction for any filing. Production limited to outside counsel, experts, and a defined plaintiff acknowledgment. The consumer's own PII is not, of course, confidential against the consumer — but production should include only the named plaintiff's account, not unrelated consumers' accounts absent class-certification posture.  
  
\#\#\# 3. Commercial-Relationship Documents  
  
In debt-buyer cases, purchase and sale agreements and account-transfer documents typically include pricing terms (price per dollar of face value), portfolio composition, and seller-side reps and warranties. In third-party-collector cases, vendor contracts include fee structures and service-level commitments.  
  
\*\*Good-cause showing.\*\* Genuine business-confidentiality interest. Courts recognize that pricing and economic terms can be competitively sensitive. \*See In re Adobe Sys. Inc. Sec. Litig.\*, 141 F.R.D. 155 (N.D. Cal. 1992).  
  
\*\*Typical protections.\*\* "Confidential" or "Highly Confidential — Attorney's Eyes Only" depending on sensitivity. AEO is appropriate for pricing detail; ordinary confidential for non-economic provisions.  
  
\#\#\# 4. Call Scripts and Form Letters (Limited Trade-Secret Claims)  
  
Defendants sometimes assert trade-secret protection over call scripts, talk-offs, and form letters. These claims are weak: form letters are mass-distributed to consumers, and the very content of the script is the subject of the FDCPA analysis. \*See Foti v. NCO Fin. Sys., Inc.\*, 424 F. Supp. 2d 643 (S.D.N.Y. 2006) (scripts central to liability).
