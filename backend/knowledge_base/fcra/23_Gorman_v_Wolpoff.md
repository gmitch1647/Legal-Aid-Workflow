# Gorman v. Wolpoff & Abramson, LLP, 584 F.3d 1147 (9th Cir. 2009)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** David Gorman, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. Wolpoff & Abramson, LLP, Defendant-Appellee, 584 F.3d 1147 (9th Cir. 2009)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
**Date:** October 22, 2009
**Docket:** No. 07-56515
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the Southern District of California
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duties upon notice of dispute), § 1681s-2(a) (furnisher duty to report accurate information), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance), § 1681n (willful noncompliance)

**PERSUASIVE AUTHORITY:** Ninth Circuit precedent. Not binding in the Northern District of Georgia but highly influential and frequently cited in all circuits for defining the scope of a furnisher's investigation duty under § 1681s-2(b). The Ninth Circuit's analysis in *Gorman* was part of the multi-circuit consensus that influenced the Eleventh Circuit's holding in *Hinkle*.

---

## Facts

David Gorman disputed a debt being reported on his credit file by Wolpoff & Abramson, LLP ("W&A"), a debt collection law firm that was furnishing information to consumer reporting agencies about Gorman's alleged account.

**The Underlying Debt:** Gorman disputed a consumer debt that W&A was collecting and reporting to the CRAs. Gorman contended that the debt was not owed, that the amount was incorrect, and/or that the reporting was inaccurate in material respects.

**The Dispute and Investigation:** Gorman disputed the debt through the CRAs, which triggered notice to W&A under § 1681s-2(b). Upon receiving the ACDV notice, W&A was required to conduct an investigation of the disputed information.

**W&A's Investigation:** W&A's investigation consisted of checking its own internal collection records — the same records it used to initiate collection and to furnish information to the CRAs. W&A did not:

- Obtain or review the underlying documentation from the original creditor that would substantiate the debt
- Examine original contracts, account agreements, or signed applications
- Review the original creditor's complete account records
- Investigate the specific factual basis for Gorman's dispute
- Go beyond its own records (which were derivative of what the original creditor provided at the time of account placement)

**The Systemic Problem:** As a debt collection law firm, W&A received accounts for collection from creditors with only the information necessary to pursue collection — typically an electronic data file with account numbers, balances, and consumer information. W&A's "investigation" of a dispute consisted of checking this same data file and confirming it matched what had been reported. This was functionally identical to asking "did we report what we were told?" rather than "is what we were told accurate?"

**Key Evidence:** The record showed that W&A's investigation process was standardized and cursory — disputes were handled as routine administrative matters rather than genuine factual inquiries. There was no individualized assessment of the merits of Gorman's specific dispute.

---

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (S.D. Cal.):** Granted summary judgment to W&A, finding its investigation reasonable as a matter of law
2. **Ninth Circuit:** REVERSED and REMANDED — holding that W&A's investigation was not reasonable and that the adequacy of a furnisher's investigation is a question for the jury

## Holdings

### Holding 1: Furnisher Cannot Rubber-Stamp Its Own Records

> "A furnisher does not reasonably investigate a dispute about the validity or accuracy of reported information by merely reviewing the information previously provided by the creditor that placed the account for collection."

The Ninth Circuit held that a furnisher's investigation under § 1681s-2(b) requires more than confirming the information in its own files. When those files are themselves derivative of another source (the original creditor), the furnisher must look beyond those derivative records to conduct a meaningful investigation.

### Holding 2: Must Review Underlying Documentation

> "A reasonable investigation requires some degree of careful inquiry — for example, reviewing the documentation underlying the reported information to determine whether it substantiates the consumer's dispute."

The court established that a "reasonable" investigation under § 1681s-2(b) requires, at minimum, review of the underlying documentation that substantiates the reported information. For a debt collector, this means obtaining and reviewing the original creditor's account records — not merely re-reading the collection file.

### Holding 3: Scope of Furnisher Investigation Parallels CRA Reinvestigation Duty

> "The furnisher's duty to investigate under § 1681s-2(b) is analogous to the CRA's reinvestigation duty under § 1681i(a) — both require more than a perfunctory review, and both must be evaluated under a reasonableness standard."

The Ninth Circuit explicitly drew the parallel between furnisher investigation duties and CRA reinvestigation duties, holding that the same principles apply: just as a CRA cannot satisfy § 1681i(a) by merely re-asking the furnisher (per *Cushman*), a furnisher cannot satisfy § 1681s-2(b) by merely re-checking its own records.

### Holding 4: Standard for "Reasonable" Furnisher Investigation

The court articulated factors relevant to determining whether a furnisher's investigation was reasonable:

> "In evaluating the reasonableness of a furnisher's investigation, relevant considerations include: (1) whether the furnisher received notice that the consumer disputed the information; (2) whether there was sufficient information in the notice to investigate; (3) the extent and results of the investigation actually undertaken; (4) the cost and availability of additional steps; and (5) the risk of harm to the consumer from continued inaccurate reporting."

This multi-factor test provides a framework for evaluating furnisher investigations in all circuits.

### Holding 5: Private Right of Action Under § 1681s-2(b) Encompasses Investigation Adequacy

The court confirmed that consumers have a private right of action under § 1681s-2(b) to challenge the adequacy of a furnisher's investigation — not just whether an investigation occurred, but whether it was reasonable in scope and execution.

> "The private right of action under § 1681s-2(b) is not limited to the question whether the furnisher conducted any investigation. The consumer may challenge the reasonableness of the investigation conducted."

### Holding 6: Debt Collector/Law Firm Bears Same Duty as Other Furnishers

The court rejected any argument that a debt collection law firm's investigation duty is lesser than that of an original creditor. Any entity that furnishes information to CRAs bears the full duty of § 1681s-2(b), regardless of its relationship to the original debt:

> "The statute makes no distinction between categories of furnishers. All furnishers bear the same duty to conduct a reasonable investigation upon receiving notice of a dispute."

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### The Statutory Framework

Section 1681s-2(b) establishes a three-step process:
1. Consumer disputes information with the CRA
2. CRA notifies the furnisher of the dispute
3. Furnisher must: (A) conduct an investigation, (B) review all relevant information forwarded by the CRA, (C) report results, and (D) modify/delete/block information found inaccurate

The Ninth Circuit focused on what step (A) — "conduct an investigation" — actually requires.

### The Textual Analysis

The court engaged in careful textual analysis of § 1681s-2(b)(1)(A):

- The statute uses "investigation" — a term that implies active inquiry, factual development, and substantive analysis
- Congress did not use "review," "check," or "verify" — terms that would suggest a more limited obligation
- The use of "investigation" in context (alongside requirements to "review all relevant information" and to "report results") suggests a sequential process: receive information, investigate, report findings
- An "investigation" that consists solely of re-reading one's own files is not an investigation at all — it is a confirmation

### The Functional Analysis

The court examined the practical reality of what furnishers do when they merely check internal records:

1. **Original creditor places account with collector** → collector receives electronic data file
2. **Collector reports to CRA** based on the data file received
3. **Consumer disputes** → CRA sends ACDV to collector
4. **Collector "investigates"** by checking the same data file
5. **Collector verifies** the information back to the CRA

This process is entirely circular. The information being "investigated" is derived from the same source being checked. No new information enters the system. No error can be detected. The "investigation" is functionally meaningless.

The court recognized that this circular process defeats the purpose of § 1681s-2(b), which exists to provide a mechanism for error correction. If the investigation cannot identify errors, the statutory scheme fails.

### The CRA Parallel

The court drew explicitly on CRA reinvestigation case law — particularly *Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.*, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997) — to reason about the furnisher's duty:

- Just as a CRA cannot satisfy § 1681i(a) by merely re-asking the source that provided the disputed information, a furnisher cannot satisfy § 1681s-2(b) by merely re-reading its own files
- The same "rubber-stamp" concern applies at both levels
- The reasonableness standard requires the same type of cost-benefit analysis at both levels
- Both duties are evaluated under the "totality of the circumstances"

### The Reasonableness Framework

The court's five-factor test for reasonableness provides a structured framework:

1. **Notice adequacy** — Did the furnisher receive meaningful notice of what was disputed?
2. **Information sufficiency** — Was there enough detail in the dispute to investigate?
3. **Investigation scope** — What did the furnisher actually do?
4. **Cost of additional steps** — How expensive/difficult would more investigation have been?
5. **Risk of harm** — What was the potential harm to the consumer from continued inaccurate reporting?

This framework allows courts and juries to evaluate furnisher investigations contextually rather than through rigid rules.

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Gorman

Cite *Gorman* as the leading Ninth Circuit authority on furnisher investigation duty. While not binding in the Eleventh Circuit, it is frequently cited alongside *Hinkle* and provides the most detailed framework for evaluating furnisher investigations.

1. **When the furnisher is a debt collector, servicer, or other entity with derivative records** — *Gorman* directly addresses entities that collect or service accounts originally held by another creditor. If the furnisher is a debt collector (Midland, Portfolio Recovery, LVNV, Encore), a servicer (Navient, MOHELA, Mr. Cooper), or a collection law firm, *Gorman* is directly on point.

2. **When arguing the "rubber stamp" theory** — *Gorman* provides the most explicit judicial statement that a furnisher "cannot rubber-stamp its own records." This phrase has become standard shorthand in FCRA briefing. Cite *Gorman* whenever you use the "rubber stamp" characterization.

3. **When you need the multi-factor reasonableness framework** — *Gorman*'s five-factor test for evaluating reasonableness is useful in briefing at all stages: complaint (to allege why the investigation was unreasonable), MSJ (to identify disputed material facts), and trial (to frame jury instructions).

4. **When arguing the CRA/furnisher parallel** — If your case involves both CRA reinvestigation claims and furnisher investigation claims, *Gorman* explicitly draws the parallel. Use it to argue that the same principles condemning the CRA's perfunctory reinvestigation also condemn the furnisher's rubber-stamp investigation.

5. **When establishing multi-circuit consensus for willfulness** — *Gorman* (9th Cir. 2009), *Saunders* (4th Cir. 2008), *Johnson* (4th Cir. 2004), and *Hinkle* (11th Cir. 2016) all reach the same conclusion. This consensus establishes that the law has been clear for well over a decade — supporting willfulness claims against furnishers who continue to rubber-stamp.

Here, each factor weighs against Defendant: [apply each factor to your facts]."

**For Cases Against Debt Collectors Specifically:**

> "As a debt collector, Defendant received Plaintiff's account from [original creditor] with only limited electronic data — the same data from which Defendant generated its CRA reporting. When Plaintiff disputed the reported information, Defendant's 'investigation' consisted of re-reading this derivative data file. Under *Gorman v. Wolpoff & Abramson, LLP*, 584 F.3d 1147 (9th Cir. 2009), this is insufficient: 'A furnisher does not reasonably investigate a dispute about the validity or accuracy of reported information by merely reviewing the information previously provided by the creditor that placed the account for collection.' *Id.* at 1157. A reasonable investigation required Defendant to obtain and review the original creditor's underlying account documentation."

### Discovery Requests Supported by Gorman

**Interrogatories:**

1. "Describe the source(s) of information Defendant relies upon when furnishing information about Plaintiff's account to consumer reporting agencies. For each source, state: (a) who provided the information; (b) when it was provided; (c) in what format; and (d) whether Defendant independently verified its accuracy before furnishing it to the CRAs."

2. "State whether Defendant obtained or reviewed any documents from the original creditor during its investigation of Plaintiff's dispute. If so, identify each document obtained and reviewed. If not, explain why Defendant did not seek documentation from the original creditor."

3. "Describe Defendant's policies and procedures for investigating consumer disputes where the underlying account was placed with Defendant by a third party (i.e., original creditor, debt seller, or prior servicer). Specifically describe what steps, if any, are taken to obtain or review records from that third party."

4. "Apply the following factors to Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute: (a) What notice did Defendant receive of Plaintiff's dispute? (b) What information was provided with the notice? (c) What steps did Defendant take in its investigation? (d) What additional steps were available and at what cost? (e) What was the risk to Plaintiff from continued inaccurate reporting?"

5. "Identify the total time spent investigating Plaintiff's dispute, the identity and qualifications of each person who participated, and whether any supervisor or quality assurance review occurred."

**Requests for Production:**

1. "All documents received from the original creditor or account seller at the time Plaintiff's account was placed with or transferred to Defendant."

2. "All documents obtained from any source other than Defendant's own internal systems during the investigation of Plaintiff's dispute."

3. "All communications between Defendant and the original creditor, account seller, or any third party concerning Plaintiff's account during the investigation of Plaintiff's dispute."

4. "Defendant's policies and procedures for investigating consumer disputes, including any distinctions in procedure based on the type of dispute, the age of the account, or the source of the account."

5. "Documents sufficient to show Defendant's dispute investigation statistics, including: (a) total disputes received; (b) average investigation time; (c) percentage verified vs. modified/deleted; and (d) the frequency with which original creditor documentation is obtained during investigations."

**Requests for Admission:**

1. "Admit that Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute did not include obtaining any documentation from the original creditor."

2. "Admit that the records Defendant reviewed during its investigation of Plaintiff's dispute were derived from information provided by the original creditor at the time of account placement."

3. "Admit that Defendant did not independently verify the accuracy of the information provided by the original creditor before reporting it to the CRAs."

4. "Admit that Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute consisted solely of reviewing records already in Defendant's possession."

5. "Admit that obtaining documentation from the original creditor was possible during Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Gorman as the "Why" Behind Hinkle:** In NDGA, *Hinkle* is binding and you need not rely on *Gorman* for the holding. But *Gorman* provides the most detailed explanation of WHY rubber-stamping fails — the circular reasoning, the functional futility, the statutory purpose analysis. When you need to EXPLAIN the principle (rather than merely cite it), *Gorman* provides the analytical depth.

2. **The Five-Factor Framework for Trial:** *Gorman*'s five-factor reasonableness test is excellent for structuring closing arguments and proposed jury instructions. Frame the evidence around each factor: notice (clear), information (specific), investigation (cursory), cost of more (minimal), harm (severe). This structure makes the jury's decision clear.

3. **Debt Collector Cases:** If your furnisher defendant is a debt collector (Midland, Portfolio Recovery, LVNV, Encore Capital, or a collection firm), *Gorman* is your strongest factual analogue. The defendant in *Gorman* was a debt collection law firm — entities that, like debt buyers, hold only derivative data and cannot meaningfully "investigate" by reviewing their own files.

4. **Building the Willfulness Case:** The multi-circuit consensus (*Johnson* 2004, *Saunders* 2008, *Gorman* 2009, *Hinkle* 2016) creates an irresistible willfulness argument. After 2016 at the latest (when *Hinkle* became binding in the Eleventh Circuit), no furnisher in Georgia can claim ignorance of the rubber-stamp prohibition. But *Gorman* and the earlier cases push the date back further — the law was "clearly established" across circuits by 2009.

5. **Expert Testimony Framework:** The *Gorman* factors provide a framework for expert testimony on industry standards. An expert witness can testify about what a reasonable investigation looks like using *Gorman*'s framework — evaluating cost, availability of information, risk of harm, and comparing the defendant's process to best practices.

6. **Proportionality in Discovery Disputes:** If the defendant objects to discovery about its investigation procedures as burdensome, cite *Gorman*'s factor (4) — cost of additional steps. If the cost of investigating the dispute itself was minimal (and the defendant still didn't do it), the defendant can hardly claim that the cost of producing documents about that non-investigation is burdensome.

## Related Cases

- *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016) — Binding 11th Circuit authority; rubber-stamping furnisher records insufficient
- *Johnson v. MBNA America Bank*, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004) — Earliest foundational case; furnisher must review underlying documents
- *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008) — Mere verification vs. investigation distinction
- *Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.*, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997) — CRA parallel; perfunctory reinvestigation unreasonable
- *Dennis v. BEH-1, LLC*, 520 F.3d 1066 (9th Cir. 2008) — Ninth Circuit; scope of reasonable reinvestigation is jury question

## Key Takeaway for the AI Drafter

*Gorman* provides the analytical framework and detailed reasoning for why a furnisher's "investigation" that consists solely of reviewing its own internal records — particularly when those records are derivative of another source — is not a reasonable investigation under § 1681s-2(b). In NDGA practice, cite *Hinkle* for the binding holding and *Gorman* for the analytical depth. Together with *Saunders* and *Johnson*, these cases establish an unassailable multi-circuit consensus that rubber-stamping fails — a consensus that supports willfulness claims against any furnisher that continues this practice after 2016. The five-factor reasonableness test provides structure for complaints, MSJ briefing, jury instructions, and closing arguments.
