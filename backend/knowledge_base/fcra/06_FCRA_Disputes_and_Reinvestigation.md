# FCRA Disputes and the Reinvestigation Process  
**Topic:** Consumer disputes, CRA reinvestigation, furnisher reinvestigation, and procedural mechanics  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681i (FCRA § 611); 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(a)(8) and (b) (FCRA § 623)  
**Cross-references:** 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43 (Regulation V direct disputes); 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) (accuracy procedures); 15 U.S.C. § 1681n / § 1681o (civil liability)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
## Introduction  
  
The FCRA dispute-and-reinvestigation framework is the engine of consumer self-help and of most modern consumer-reporting litigation. Section 611 of the Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1681i, imposes a reinvestigation duty on consumer reporting agencies (CRAs) when a consumer disputes information in his or her file. Section 623, 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2, imposes parallel duties on furnishers — but with a critical asymmetry: only the post-CRA-notification duties under § 1681s-2(b) are privately enforceable. This file walks through the statutory mechanics of the dispute process, the leading case law interpreting "reasonable reinvestigation," the practical anatomy of a dispute letter, and the litigation traps that arise at the intersection of these two regimes.  
  
## § 1681i — CRA Reinvestigation Duties  
  
### § 1681i(a)(1) — The 30-day reinvestigation window  
  
If a consumer disputes the completeness or accuracy of any item of information in his or her file, the CRA must "conduct a reasonable reinvestigation to determine whether the disputed information is inaccurate" and either confirm or correct the information "before the end of the 30-day period beginning on the date on which the agency receives the notice of the dispute from the consumer." 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(1)(A). The window can be extended by up to 15 additional days (for a 45-day total) if the consumer provides additional relevant information during the initial 30-day period. § 1681i(a)(1)(B). The 15-day extension is unavailable, however, if the CRA has already determined within the original 30 days that the information is inaccurate or cannot be verified. § 1681i(a)(1)(C). Reinvestigation must be conducted "free of charge." § 1681i(a)(1)(A).  
  
### Reasonable reinvestigation — the *Cushman* standard  
  
The Third Circuit's decision in *Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.*, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997), is the foundational appellate authority on what "reasonable reinvestigation" means. The court held that, in some circumstances, a CRA must do more than simply re-contact the original source: it may be required "to verify the accuracy of its initial source of information." *Cushman*, 115 F.3d at 225. Trans Union's policy of simply re-asking the furnisher to verify the disputed item — sometimes called "parroting" — was found inadequate as a matter of law where the consumer had provided documentary evidence undermining the furnisher's report. The Third Circuit also held that punitive damages may lie where the CRA's reinvestigation policy was adopted in reckless disregard of consumer rights.  
  
Every circuit has now adopted some version of the *Cushman* reasonableness standard. The CFPB's Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-07 (Reasonable Investigation of Consumer Reporting Disputes) reinforces that both CRAs and furnishers must conduct an investigation calibrated to the dispute — including, where appropriate, examining the consumer's documentation rather than rotely confirming the furnisher's records.  
  
### § 1681i(a)(2) — Forwarding the dispute to the furnisher  
  
Within five business days of receiving the consumer's dispute, the CRA must "provide notification of the dispute to any person who provided any item of information in dispute," along with "all relevant information regarding the dispute that the agency has received from the consumer." 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(2). This is the trigger that activates the furnisher's parallel duties under § 1681s-2(b). The five-day forwarding requirement is independent of the 30-day reinvestigation deadline; failure to forward consumer-supplied documentation has been a recurring CFPB enforcement target. CFPB Supervisory Highlights, Issue 32 (Spring 2024), reported examination findings that CRAs were failing to forward consumer-submitted documents (e.g., billing statements, identity-theft affidavits) to furnishers along with the dispute notice.  
  
### § 1681i(a)(4) — Consideration of all relevant information  
  
The CRA must "review and consider all relevant information submitted by the consumer." 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(4). A CRA cannot satisfy this duty by treating the dispute as an automated data-comparison exercise. Where the consumer submits documentary evidence — court records, paid-in-full letters, identity-theft police reports — the CRA must actually consider it. The CFPB's 2022 Circular emphasizes this point and warns that mechanically funneling disputes through automated systems without human review of documentary evidence can violate § 1681i(a)(4).  
  
### § 1681i(a)(5) — Result requirements; deletion or modification; notice to consumer  
  
If the reinvestigation finds the disputed information inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable, the CRA must "promptly delete that item of information from the file of the consumer, or modify that item of information, as appropriate, based on the results of the reinvestigation." 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(5)(A). The "cannot be verified" prong is critical: the consumer prevails even if the underlying truth remains unknown, so long as the CRA cannot affirmatively verify the item. The CRA must also "promptly notify the furnisher of that information that the information has been modified or deleted."  
  
The CRA must send the consumer written notice of the results within five business days of completion of the reinvestigation, including (i) a statement that the reinvestigation is complete, (ii) a consumer report based on the consumer's file as revised, (iii) a notice including the name, address, and telephone number of any furnisher contacted, (iv) a notice of the consumer's right to add a statement of dispute to the file, and (v) a notice of the right to request that notice of the deletion be furnished to past recipients of the report. 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(6) and (a)(7). ### § 1681i(a)(5)(B) — Reinsertion procedures (the "five-day notice")  
  
Once deleted, the disputed information "may not be reinserted in the file by the consumer reporting agency unless the person who furnishes the information certifies that the information is complete and accurate." § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(i). If the CRA does reinsert the information, it must notify the consumer in writing of the reinsertion "not later than 5 business days after the reinsertion." § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii). The reinsertion notice must include a statement that the information has been reinserted, the business name and address of the furnisher who certified the information, and a notice of the consumer's right to add a statement of dispute. [Note: the question's framing referred to "§ 1681i(f)" for reinsertion; the correct citation under the current codification is § 1681i(a)(5)(B).]  
  
### § 1681i(b) and (c) — Consumer statement of dispute  
  
If the reinvestigation does not resolve the dispute in the consumer's favor, the consumer may file "a brief statement setting forth the nature of the dispute." § 1681i(b). The CRA "may limit such statements to not more than one hundred words if it provides the consumer with assistance in writing a clear summary of the dispute." § 1681i(b). Once such a statement is on file, the CRA must, in any subsequent consumer report containing the disputed information, "clearly note that it is disputed by the consumer and provide either the consumer's statement or a clear and accurate codification or summary thereof," unless the agency has reasonable grounds to believe the dispute is frivolous or irrelevant. § 1681i(c).  
  
### § 1681i(d) — Notice of deletion / modification to other CRAs and prior users  
  
Upon the consumer's request, the CRA must furnish notice of the deletion or modification to "any person specifically designated by the consumer" who received the consumer report (i) for employment purposes within the two-year period preceding the request, or (ii) for any other purpose within the six-month period preceding the request. 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(d). [VERIFY: the look-back period for non-employment purposes is sometimes cited as one year; confirm against current codification.] This is the "back-notification" mechanism that allows correction of disseminated reports.  
  
### § 1681i(a)(3) — The "frivolous or irrelevant" exception  
  
A CRA may terminate a reinvestigation if it "reasonably determines" that the dispute is "frivolous or irrelevant" — including because the consumer has failed to provide sufficient information to investigate the dispute. § 1681i(a)(3)(A). The CRA must, within five business days of making such a determination, notify the consumer in writing of the termination, the specific reasons for the determination, and the information needed to reopen the dispute. § 1681i(a)(3)(B)–(C). Courts have policed this exception carefully: a CRA cannot label a dispute "frivolous" simply because the furnisher disagrees, and "credit repair organization" disputes are not automatically frivolous merely because they originate from a third-party preparer. See, e.g., FTC guidance disfavoring blanket rejection of disputes based on third-party preparation.  
  
## Direct disputes to furnishers under § 1681s-2(a)(8) and 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43  
  
A consumer may also dispute information directly with the furnisher under § 1681s-2(a)(8) and its implementing regulation, 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43. The direct-dispute regime is procedurally separate from the CRA-mediated dispute under § 1681i.  
  
**Categories of disputes that trigger the direct-dispute duty.** Under § 1022.43(a), a furnisher must conduct a reasonable investigation of a direct dispute only if it relates to (1) the consumer's liability for a credit account or other debt with the furnisher, (2) the terms of a credit account or other debt with the furnisher, (3) the consumer's performance or other conduct concerning an account or other relationship with the furnisher, or (4) any other information contained in a consumer report regarding an account or relationship with the furnisher that bears on the consumer's creditworthiness, credit standing, credit capacity, character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living.  
  
**Excluded categories.** § 1022.43(b) excludes a range of disputes from the direct-dispute regime — for example, disputes about the consumer's identifying information (other than a complete identity-theft block), disputes initiated by credit repair organizations, and disputes about information reported by furnishers to specialty CRAs not used in connection with the underlying credit decision. [VERIFY: precise scope of the credit-repair exclusion under current Reg V.]  
  
**Submission requirements.** Under § 1022.43(c), the consumer must submit the direct dispute to (1) the address provided by the furnisher on the consumer report, (2) any address clearly and conspicuously specified by the furnisher for direct disputes, or (3) any business address of the furnisher if no specific dispute address is provided.  
  
**Critical doctrinal point.** Most courts — including the First Circuit in *Chiang v. Verizon New England Inc.*, 595 F.3d 26 (1st Cir. 2010) — have held that a direct dispute to the furnisher under § 1681s-2(a)(8) does NOT itself trigger the furnisher's private-action duties under § 1681s-2(b). The § 1681s-2(b) duties are triggered only by notice from a CRA. As a practical matter, this means that a consumer who wants to preserve a private right of action against a furnisher must dispute through a CRA, not just write to the furnisher directly.  
  
## Indirect dispute through CRA: the private-action track  
  
The indirect dispute — consumer to CRA, CRA to furnisher — is the workhorse of FCRA litigation against furnishers. The mechanics are:  
  
1. Consumer files a dispute with one or more CRAs under § 1681i(a)(1).  
2. The CRA forwards the dispute and "all relevant information" to the furnisher within five business days under § 1681i(a)(2).  
3. The furnisher's § 1681s-2(b) duties are then triggered: investigate, review the CRA-provided information, report results, and modify/delete/permanently block if the information is inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable.  
4. The CRA completes its own reinvestigation within 30 (or 45) days.  
5. Each side independently faces a private right of action — the CRA under § 1681i, the furnisher under § 1681s-2(b).  
  
## Practical mechanics: writing a dispute letter  
  
A well-drafted dispute letter should:  
  
- Identify the consumer (full name, current and previous addresses, date of birth, last four digits of SSN).  
- Identify the specific tradeline or item disputed (creditor name, account number, the precise information disputed).  
- State plainly what the consumer contends is wrong and what the correct information is.  
- Attach documentary evidence (bills, court records, identity-theft affidavit under § 1681c-2, paid-in-full letters).  
- Be sent in writing (preferably certified mail, return receipt requested) to preserve a record of receipt and timing.  
- Be sent to each CRA reporting the disputed item (for indirect disputes preserving § 1681s-2(b) claims) and, if also pursuing direct-dispute remedies, to the furnisher's designated dispute address.  
  
The CFPB's online portal and the CRAs' online dispute systems satisfy § 1681i's "directly or indirectly" language, but written certified mail remains the gold standard for litigation preservation.  
  
## e-OSCAR and "automated dispute verifications"  
  
The major CRAs and most large furnishers exchange dispute data through e-OSCAR, an electronic platform that converts consumer disputes into short "dispute codes" (so-called ACDV — Automated Consumer Dispute Verification — forms). Plaintiffs' counsel and courts have repeatedly criticized e-OSCAR's role in producing rote, mechanical "verifications" that fail the *Cushman* reasonableness standard. The Eleventh Circuit in *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Management, Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016), held that a furnisher cannot satisfy § 1681s-2(b)'s "reasonable investigation" duty merely by re-confirming what is already in its own electronic records; where the consumer has alleged that a debt was never theirs, account-level documentation may be required. The CFPB's 2022 Circular and its Spring 2024 Supervisory Highlights (Issue 32) reach similar conclusions. The CFPB also found that some CRAs were stripping consumer-submitted documents before forwarding ACDV codes to furnishers — a practice the agency identified as a likely § 1681i(a)(2) violation.  
  
## Litigation traps  
  
- **Pre-suit dispute is NOT a statutory prerequisite for a § 1681e(b) claim.** A consumer may sue under § 1681e(b) without first having disputed the information through § 1681i. The accuracy-procedures duty is independent of the reinvestigation duty.  
- **Pre-suit dispute through a CRA IS a statutory prerequisite for a § 1681s-2(b) claim.** Because the furnisher's § 1681s-2(b) duty is triggered by a CRA notice, the consumer must have first disputed through a CRA. A direct dispute to the furnisher under § 1681s-2(a)(8) does not satisfy this prerequisite for purposes of the private right of action.  
- **No private right of action under § 1681s-2(a).** See § 1681s-2(c) and (d), enforced exclusively by federal and state agencies. This is the single most common pleading defect in pro se furnisher complaints.  
- **Statute of limitations.** Under 15 U.S.C. § 1681p, claims must be brought within the earlier of (a) two years after the consumer discovers the violation, or (b) five years after the date on which the violation occurred. The "discovery rule" was confirmed by the Supreme Court's framing in *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19 (2001), although the statute was amended in 2003 (FACTA) to codify discovery accrual.  
  
## CFPB Supervisory Highlights findings on dispute handling  
  
CFPB Supervisory Highlights, Issue 32 (Spring 2024), specifically focused on consumer reporting and identified the following recurring violations:  
  
- Furnishers continuing to report disputed information without notifying the CRA that the information was disputed.  
- Furnishers refusing to investigate direct disputes that did not satisfy "extraneous identity verification requirements" the furnisher had unilaterally imposed.  
- Furnishers continuing to furnish information they had themselves determined was incomplete or inaccurate, sometimes for years.  
- CRAs failing to forward consumer-submitted documents to furnishers along with dispute notices.  
- CRAs treating disputes as automated comparison exercises without human review of relevant documentary evidence.  
  
These findings are routinely cited in plaintiffs' briefs and provide important context for jury arguments about industry-wide patterns of FCRA non-compliance.
