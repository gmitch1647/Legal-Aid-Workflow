# FCRA Furnisher Duties Under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2  
**Topic:** Statutory and regulatory duties of furnishers of information to consumer reporting agencies, with emphasis on the private right of action under § 1681s-2(b)  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2 (FCRA § 623)  
**Cross-references:** 12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.40–.43 (Regulation V Subpart E); 15 U.S.C. § 1681i (CRA reinvestigation); 15 U.S.C. § 1681n / § 1681o (civil liability)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
## Introduction  
  
Furnishers — banks, credit card issuers, mortgage servicers, auto lenders, debt collectors, debt buyers, telecom providers, utilities, landlords reporting to specialty CRAs, and any other entity that transmits consumer information to a CRA — are the dominant defendants in modern FCRA litigation. Their duties are codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2 and implemented at 12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.40–.43 (Regulation V Subpart E). The statute has a distinctive split-enforcement architecture: most of the furnisher duties are agency-enforced only, while a single subsection — § 1681s-2(b) — supports a private right of action. This file maps the statutory architecture, surveys the leading case law, and identifies recurring fact patterns in furnisher litigation and CFPB enforcement.  
  
## § 1681s-2(a) — Duty to provide accurate information (no private right of action)  
  
### § 1681s-2(a)(1) — Prohibition on knowingly reporting inaccurate information  
  
A furnisher "shall not furnish any information relating to a consumer to any consumer reporting agency if the person knows or has reasonable cause to believe that the information is inaccurate." 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(a)(1)(A). The statute exempts furnishers who provide a notice address for direct disputes; in that case, the higher "knows or consciously avoids knowing" standard applies. § 1681s-2(a)(1)(B)–(C). [VERIFY: precise interaction between the "reasonable cause to believe" and "consciously avoids knowing" standards under the current codification — the statute's two tiers can be confusing.]  
  
There is also a duty to correct: if the furnisher determines that previously reported information is inaccurate, it must promptly notify the CRA and "provide to the consumer reporting agency any corrections to that information." § 1681s-2(a)(2).  
  
### § 1681s-2(a)(2) — Duty to correct and update  
  
A furnisher that has provided information to a CRA and "thereafter determines that the information is not complete or accurate" must "promptly notify the consumer reporting agency of that determination" and provide the CRA with corrected or completed information. 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(a)(2). This is a continuing duty — the furnisher cannot remain silent once it discovers the prior reporting was wrong. CFPB Supervisory Highlights, Issue 32 (Spring 2024), found auto-loan furnishers in violation where they continued furnishing inaccurate information for months or years after discovering the error.  
  
### § 1681s-2(a)(3) — Duty to provide notice of dispute  
  
If a consumer notifies the furnisher (directly) that information being furnished is disputed, the furnisher must, in all subsequent reports to CRAs, "include notice of such dispute." 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(a)(3). The Fourth Circuit in *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co. of Va.*, 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008), held that a furnisher's failure to mark an account as disputed can render the reporting "incomplete or inaccurate" within the meaning of § 1681s-2(b)(1) — bridging the (a) duty (no private action) and the (b) duty (private action) through the materiality of dispute-flagging. The jury in *Saunders* awarded the maximum $1,000 statutory damages plus $80,000 in punitive damages for the furnisher's willful failure to report the dispute, and the Fourth Circuit affirmed.  
  
### § 1681s-2(a)(6)–(7) — Identity-theft protections  
  
Section 1681s-2(a)(6) addresses block requests under § 1681c-2 (identity-theft blocks). Section 1681s-2(a)(7) requires that a furnisher who receives an identity-theft report from a consumer not furnish information related to the alleged identity-theft transactions to any CRA. The provisions work in tandem with § 1681c-2 to give victims a statutory mechanism for blocking fraudulent items. [VERIFY: precise lettering of the identity-theft duties under § 1681s-2(a) — the FACTA amendments inserted several paragraphs out of strict numerical order.]  
  
### § 1681s-2(a)(8) — Direct disputes (with Regulation V § 1022.43 limitations)  
  
Subsection (a)(8) authorizes consumers to submit disputes directly to furnishers and requires furnishers to investigate them. The CFPB has implemented the provision through 12 C.F.R. § 1022.43, which establishes the categories of disputes that trigger furnisher investigation duties, the addresses to which consumers must submit them, and the categories of disputes that are excluded (notably, certain credit-repair-organization-submitted disputes and disputes about identifying information unrelated to a § 1681c-2 block).  
  
Crucially, the First Circuit in *Chiang v. Verizon New England Inc.*, 595 F.3d 26 (1st Cir. 2010), and other courts have held that violations of § 1681s-2(a)(8) — like violations of the rest of subsection (a) — are not privately enforceable; the only private cause of action against furnishers runs through § 1681s-2(b), which is triggered by CRA notification, not by a direct dispute.  
  
## § 1681s-2(b) — Duties upon notice of dispute from a CRA (PRIVATE RIGHT OF ACTION)  
  
Section 1681s-2(b) is the heart of furnisher litigation. When a CRA notifies a furnisher of a consumer dispute pursuant to § 1681i(a)(2), the furnisher must:  
  
1. **Conduct an investigation** with respect to the disputed information. § 1681s-2(b)(1)(A).  
2. **Review all relevant information** provided by the CRA. § 1681s-2(b)(1)(B).  
3. **Report the results** of the investigation to the CRA. § 1681s-2(b)(1)(C).  
4. **Report results to all other CRAs** to which the furnisher transmitted the inaccurate or incomplete information, if the investigation determines the information is in fact incomplete or inaccurate. § 1681s-2(b)(1)(D).  
5. **Modify, delete, or permanently block** the information if it is found to be inaccurate, incomplete, or cannot be verified. § 1681s-2(b)(1)(E).  
  
The duties must be completed within the period for the CRA's reinvestigation under § 1681i(a)(1) — i.e., 30 days from the date the CRA received the consumer's dispute (with a possible 15-day extension). § 1681s-2(b)(2).  
  
### "Reasonable investigation" standard for furnishers  
  
The "investigation" required by § 1681s-2(b)(1)(A) must be reasonable. The Ninth Circuit in *Gorman v. Wolpoff & Abramson, LLP*, 584 F.3d 1147 (9th Cir. 2009), held: "any investigation under § 1681s-2(b)(1)(A) must be reasonable" — and the question of reasonableness is normally for the jury. *Gorman* also held that a furnisher's duty to report inaccurate or incomplete information following a § 1681s-2(b) investigation extends to flagging an account as disputed where the consumer has lodged a bona fide dispute, picking up the *Saunders* analysis from the Fourth Circuit.  
  
The Eleventh Circuit in *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Management, Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016), supplied the leading appellate articulation of what reasonableness requires of a debt-buyer furnisher. The plaintiff alleged that two charged-off accounts on her credit report did not belong to her. Midland — a debt buyer — "verified" the accounts to the CRAs based solely on its own electronic records, without obtaining account-level documentation from the original creditors. The Eleventh Circuit reversed summary judgment for Midland, holding:  
  
- The reasonableness of a § 1681s-2(b) investigation depends on the nature of the dispute. A dispute that "the debt is not mine" calls for a different investigation than a dispute about a payment amount.  
- Where the furnisher is a debt buyer that has contractual rights to demand account-level documentation from the seller, a reasonable jury could find that failing to exercise those rights — and instead "verifying" based only on summary electronic records — is unreasonable.  
- A furnisher cannot "shift its burden of reasonable investigation to the consumer."  
- Section 1681s-2(b) "contemplates three potential ending points" to an investigation: verification, a determination of inaccuracy or incompleteness, or a determination that the information "cannot be verified." The third outcome carries the same legal consequence as the second — modification, deletion, or permanent block.  
  
Together, *Gorman*, *Hinkle*, *Saunders*, and *Chiang* establish that the furnisher's § 1681s-2(b) duty is substantive — not a mere "rubber stamp" of internal records — and that the duty extends to ensuring that the information reported to CRAs is both accurate and not misleading (including by failing to note disputes).  
  
### What furnishers must do in practice  
  
Courts and the CFPB have articulated a non-exhaustive list of investigative steps that may be required in a § 1681s-2(b) investigation:  
  
- Reviewing the CRA's ACDV (Automated Consumer Dispute Verification) record and any attached consumer documentation forwarded by the CRA.  
- Reviewing the furnisher's own internal account records, including payment histories, original loan documentation, and communications with the consumer.  
- For debt buyers and assignees, obtaining account-level documentation from the seller (per *Hinkle*).  
- For identity-theft and fraud disputes, comparing the consumer's identifying information with the information used to open the account.  
- Considering legal disputes, not just factual ones — the CFPB's amicus position (consistent with *Chiang*-line cases) is that a furnisher may not refuse to investigate simply because the dispute raises a legal rather than purely factual question.  
  
## § 1681s-2(c) — Limitation on liability: no private right of action for (a) violations  
  
Section 1681s-2(c) provides that sections 1681n and 1681o (the civil-liability provisions for willful and negligent FCRA violations) "do not apply to any violation of (1) subsection (a) of this section, including any regulations issued thereunder; [or] (2) subsection (e) of this section, except that nothing in this paragraph shall limit, expand, or otherwise affect liability under section 1681n or 1681o of this title, as applicable, for violations of subsection (b) of this section." § 1681s-2(c).  
  
The practical consequence is that consumers cannot sue furnishers for damages based on:  
- Reporting inaccurate information in the first instance (§ 1681s-2(a)(1)).  
- Failing to correct inaccurate information after determining it was wrong (§ 1681s-2(a)(2)).  
- Failing to flag accounts as disputed after a direct consumer dispute (§ 1681s-2(a)(3)) — though *Saunders* and *Gorman* allow the underlying conduct to be re-packaged as a § 1681s-2(b) violation if a CRA-mediated dispute also occurred.  
- Failing to investigate a direct dispute under § 1681s-2(a)(8).  
- Failing to comply with the accuracy guidelines under § 1681s-2(e).  
  
The lone private-action gateway is § 1681s-2(b), triggered by CRA notice. Courts have rigorously enforced this limitation. See *Nelson v. Chase Manhattan Mortgage Corp.*, 282 F.3d 1057 (9th Cir. 2002) (first appellate decision recognizing the limitation); *Gorman*, 584 F.3d at 1156.  
  
## § 1681s-2(d) — Limitation on enforcement to federal/state agencies  
  
Section 1681s-2(d) makes the limitation in subsection (c) effective by reserving enforcement of § 1681s-2(a) and (e) to "the Federal agencies and officials and the State officials identified in section 1681s of this title." 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(d). The FTC, the CFPB (for entities within its supervisory jurisdiction), the federal banking regulators, and state attorneys general are the exclusive enforcers. Consumer suits attempting to do an "end run" around § 1681s-2(c) — by, e.g., asserting state-law claims premised on § 1681s-2(a) violations — generally fail under FCRA preemption (§ 1681t(b)(1)(F)) or under the express-private-action limitation.  
  
## § 1681s-2(e) — Accuracy guidelines and regulations  
  
Section 1681s-2(e) directs the CFPB (originally the FTC and federal banking agencies) to "establish and maintain guidelines for use by each person that furnishes information to a consumer reporting agency regarding the accuracy and integrity of the information relating to consumers that such entities furnish to consumer reporting agencies" and to "prescribe regulations requiring each person that furnishes information to a consumer reporting agency to establish reasonable policies and procedures for implementing the guidelines." § 1681s-2(e). The implementing rules are Regulation V Subpart E, discussed below. Like (a) violations, (e) violations are not privately enforceable. § 1681s-2(c)(2).  
  
## Regulation V Subpart E: 12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.40–.43  
  
Subpart E of Regulation V is the implementing regulation for § 1681s-2's furnisher duties. Its key provisions:  
  
- **§ 1022.40** — Scope. Subpart E applies to "furnishers" defined in § 1022.41.  
- **§ 1022.41** — Definitions. "Furnisher" is broadly defined as an entity that furnishes information about a consumer to one or more CRAs for inclusion in a consumer report. "Direct dispute" is defined as a dispute submitted directly to a furnisher by a consumer concerning the accuracy of information contained in a consumer report and pertaining to an account or other relationship the furnisher has or had with the consumer. "Integrity" requires that furnished information be substantiated by the furnisher's records at the time of furnishing and provided "in a form and manner that is designed to minimize the likelihood that the information may be incorrectly reflected in a consumer report."  
- **§ 1022.42** — Reasonable policies and procedures. Each furnisher must establish and implement reasonable written policies and procedures regarding accuracy and integrity, "appropriate to the nature, size, complexity, and scope of each furnisher's activities." Appendix E to Part 1022 (the Interagency Guidelines) provides the substantive content of what those policies must address — including data accuracy at source, dispute-handling procedures, training, audits, and updating of furnishing systems.  
- **§ 1022.43** — Direct disputes. Implements § 1681s-2(a)(8). Defines the categories of disputes furnishers must investigate, the proper submission addresses, the categories excluded (notably credit-repair-organization disputes that satisfy the regulatory criteria), and the deadlines for furnisher response.

## "Reasonable investigation" — the cross-cutting standard  
  
Across these decisions, several principles emerge:  
  
1. **Objective standard.** Reasonableness is judged objectively, by reference to what a reasonable furnisher would do given the dispute. *Chiang*, 595 F.3d at 38; *Gorman*, 584 F.3d at 1157.  
2. **Calibrated to the dispute.** The depth of investigation required scales with the seriousness and specificity of the dispute. A bare "this is wrong" boilerplate may permit a less searching investigation than a detailed dispute with supporting documents. *Hinkle*, 827 F.3d at 1305–06.  
3. **Not a self-confirmation exercise.** A furnisher cannot satisfy its duty by merely re-running its own electronic records. *Hinkle*, 827 F.3d at 1304–05.  
4. **Account-level documentation may be required.** Especially for debt buyers, the absence of any documentation beyond an electronic data file is a recurring vulnerability. *Hinkle*, 827 F.3d at 1306–07.  
5. **Dispute notation is part of accuracy.** A failure to note that an account is disputed can itself make the report incomplete or inaccurate. *Saunders*, 526 F.3d at 148–50.  
6. **Legal disputes count.** The CFPB has taken the position (in its 2022 Circular and amicus briefs) that furnishers must investigate disputes raising legal questions — for example, whether a debt is barred by discharge in bankruptcy — and may not categorically reject such disputes as "non-factual."  
  
## Common furnisher violations from CFPB enforcement actions  
  
CFPB Supervisory Highlights, Issue 32 (Spring 2024), focused on consumer reporting and identified these recurring furnisher violations:  
  
- **Continuing to furnish disputed information without dispute flagging.** Furnishers that received direct disputes continued reporting the underlying information without notifying CRAs that the information was disputed — a § 1681s-2(a)(3) issue, which becomes a § 1681s-2(b) issue when combined with a CRA-mediated dispute.  
- **Refusing to investigate disputes lacking "extraneous" identity verification.** Auto-loan furnishers were found to have imposed identity-verification requirements not authorized by the statute or regulation and to have refused to investigate disputes that did not satisfy those self-imposed requirements.  
- **Continuing to furnish information after discovering it was inaccurate.** Some furnishers continued furnishing for months or years after determining (through dispute handling or systemic-issue identification) that information was incomplete or inaccurate — a clear § 1681s-2(a)(2) violation.  
- **Inadequate written policies and procedures.** Failures to maintain or update written policies satisfying § 1022.42 — including failure to address data sources, dispute-handling, and audit/training requirements.  
  
Earlier CFPB consent orders against furnishers — including major banks, credit card issuers, and student-loan servicers — have imposed multi-million-dollar civil money penalties for systemic furnishing failures, and the CFPB has obtained injunctive relief requiring written remediation plans, third-party audits, and customer redress.  
  
## The "credit repair" issue and frivolous-dispute defenses  
  
Furnishers sometimes argue that disputes are "frivolous" — particularly disputes generated by credit repair organizations or by automated dispute services. Regulation V § 1022.43(b)(2) excludes certain CRO-submitted disputes from the direct-dispute regime, but the exclusion is narrow and does not authorize blanket rejection of any third-party-prepared dispute. The CFPB has cautioned furnishers and CRAs against treating disputes as frivolous merely because they originate from a credit repair organization or display common formatting; the dispute must be evaluated on the merits.  
  
In CRA-mediated disputes under § 1681i, the "frivolous or irrelevant" exception in § 1681i(a)(3) belongs to the CRA, not the furnisher. A furnisher that receives an ACDV from a CRA may not unilaterally decline to investigate on frivolousness grounds.  
  
## Practical litigation/compliance notes  
  
- **Pleading a § 1681s-2(b) claim.** Allege (1) the inaccuracy or incompleteness of the reported information, (2) the consumer's dispute to a CRA, (3) the CRA's transmittal of the dispute to the furnisher, (4) the furnisher's unreasonable investigation, and (5) damages. Attaching dispute letters, certified mail receipts, and ACDV records is increasingly common at the pleading stage.  
- **Discovery focus.** ACDV records, account-level documentation, dispute-handling scripts, e-OSCAR logs, training materials, internal audit findings, and prior consumer complaints. Plaintiffs frequently take 30(b)(6) depositions on the furnisher's § 1022.42 policies and procedures.  
- **Common defenses.** No CRA notice (preventing § 1681s-2(b) trigger); reasonable investigation as a matter of law; no actual inaccuracy; preemption (state-law claims); statute of limitations (§ 1681p).  
- **Willfulness.** Under *Safeco Ins. Co. v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007), willfulness requires "reckless disregard" — an objectively unreasonable reading of the statute exposing the defendant to risk substantially greater than the risk of a merely careless reading. Repeated failures to adopt account-level documentation review after *Hinkle*, or to dispute-flag after *Saunders*, are increasingly cited as evidence of recklessness.  
- **Damages.** Actual damages (credit denials, higher interest rates, emotional distress, reputational injury); statutory damages of $100 to $1,000 per willful violation under § 1681n(a)(1)(A); punitive damages for willful violations; attorney's fees and costs under § 1681n / § 1681o.
