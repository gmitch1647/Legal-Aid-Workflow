# Cortez v. Trans Union, LLC, 617 F.3d 688 (3d Cir. 2010)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Julio Cortez, Appellant, v. Trans Union, LLC, 617 F.3d 688 (3d Cir. 2010)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit
**Date:** August 3, 2010
**Case No:** 09-2827
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania
**Panel:** Judges Scirica, Rendell, and Fuentes
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy), § 1681i(a) (reinvestigation duties), § 1681n (willful noncompliance / punitive damages), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance)
**Damages Affirmed:** $1,800,000 in punitive damages against Trans Union, LLC

---

## Facts

Julio Cortez held a mortgage on his home. The mortgage was current and had never been delinquent. However, Trans Union's credit report on Cortez inaccurately reported that his mortgage was delinquent and/or in a negative status. This inaccuracy appeared on Cortez's Trans Union credit file and was disseminated to third parties who pulled his credit.

**The Pattern of Disputes and Failures:**

Cortez discovered the inaccuracy and initiated a dispute with Trans Union. He clearly identified the error — his mortgage was current, not delinquent — and provided information to support his position. Trans Union conducted what it characterized as a "reinvestigation."

After Trans Union's purported reinvestigation, **the inaccuracy remained on the report**. Cortez disputed again. And again. Over the course of the dispute process, Cortez submitted **multiple disputes** to Trans Union, each time clearly identifying the same inaccuracy and explaining that his mortgage was current.

**Trans Union's repeated failures:**

Despite receiving multiple clear notices from Cortez that the information was wrong, Trans Union:

1. Failed to correct the inaccuracy after the first dispute
2. Failed to correct the inaccuracy after the second dispute
3. Failed to correct the inaccuracy after subsequent disputes
4. Continued to disseminate the inaccurate information to third parties during the entire period
5. Failed to implement procedures that would prevent the continued reporting of information it had been told was wrong
6. Relied on the furnisher's verification without independent analysis, despite the consumer's repeated insistence that the data was wrong

**Impact on Cortez:**

The inaccurate mortgage delinquency reporting damaged Cortez's creditworthiness and ability to obtain credit on favorable terms. Mortgage delinquency is one of the most damaging items that can appear on a credit report, as it signals to potential creditors that the consumer cannot be trusted to make their most fundamental financial obligation — their home payment.

---

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (E.D. Pa.):** Case proceeded to trial. Jury found Trans Union liable under both § 1681e(b) (failure to maintain reasonable procedures) and § 1681i(a) (failure to conduct a reasonable reinvestigation). Jury awarded:
   - Compensatory/actual damages to Cortez
   - **$1,800,000 in punitive damages** against Trans Union

2. **Post-Trial Motions:** Trans Union moved for judgment as a matter of law and/or remittitur on the punitive damages award, arguing the award was excessive and unsupported by the evidence of willfulness.

3. **Third Circuit:** AFFIRMED the punitive damages award in full — holding that Trans Union's pattern of ignoring multiple consumer disputes constituted willful noncompliance supporting the $1.8 million punitive award.

---

## Holdings

### Holding 1: Pattern of Ignoring Multiple Disputes Constitutes Willfulness

> "Trans Union's repeated failure to correct inaccurate information despite multiple notices from the consumer constitutes willful noncompliance under § 1681n. When a CRA has clear notice that information on a consumer's file is disputed and potentially inaccurate, and the CRA fails to take meaningful corrective action through multiple dispute cycles, a jury may reasonably conclude that the CRA acted with reckless disregard for the consumer's rights under the FCRA."

**Translation:** One mistake might be negligence. But when a consumer tells you MULTIPLE TIMES that your report is wrong, and you keep reporting the same false information, that crosses the line from negligence into willfulness. The jury can infer that the CRA simply does not care — and that constitutes the "reckless disregard" required for willfulness under *Safeco*.

### Holding 2: $1.8 Million Punitive Damages Award Is Not Excessive

> "The punitive damages award of $1,800,000 is supported by the evidence and does not violate due process. The ratio of punitive to compensatory damages, while substantial, is justified by the reprehensibility of Trans Union's conduct — specifically its repeated failure to correct information after receiving clear notice from the consumer, the vulnerability of the consumer, and the financial condition of the defendant."

The court applied the *BMW of North America v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996), and *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408 (2003), guideposts:

1. **Reprehensibility:** HIGH — Trans Union had repeated notice and failed to act, affecting a fundamental financial record (mortgage status)
2. **Ratio:** While substantial, justified by the egregiousness of repeated, knowing failures
3. **Comparable penalties:** FCRA statutory framework contemplates significant penalties for willful violations

### Holding 3: CRA Had Clear Notice and Failed to Correct

> "A CRA has 'clear notice' of a potential inaccuracy when the consumer has submitted a dispute identifying specific information as incorrect. After such notice, the CRA's obligation to ensure accuracy is heightened. The continued reporting of disputed information after clear notice, without meaningful additional investigation, supports a finding of willfulness."

**Translation:** The consumer's dispute letters ARE the "clear notice" that elevates negligence to willfulness. Each subsequent dispute that goes unresolved increases the culpability. You do not need some external source to put the CRA on notice — the consumer's own dispute is sufficient.

### Holding 4: Reinvestigation Must Be Meaningful, Not Perfunctory

The court reaffirmed that § 1681i(a) requires a genuine reinvestigation — not a rubber-stamp process. When multiple disputes produce the same non-result, this is evidence that the CRA's reinvestigation procedures are unreasonable and that the CRA is acting with willful disregard for its statutory obligations.

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### The Willfulness Standard Under § 1681n

The court applied the Supreme Court's framework from *Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007), which held that "willful" violations under § 1681n include not only knowing violations but also violations committed in "reckless disregard" of the statute's requirements. Reckless disregard exists when the defendant's reading of the statute is "objectively unreasonable."

The Third Circuit found that Trans Union's conduct went beyond even reckless disregard — it demonstrated a pattern of deliberate indifference to the consumer's rights:

- **First dispute:** Perhaps excusable as a one-time failure
- **Second dispute:** Puts the CRA on heightened notice that its initial reinvestigation may have been inadequate
- **Third and subsequent disputes:** Creates an overwhelming inference that the CRA is not conducting genuine reinvestigations and is instead simply rubber-stamping the furnisher's response

### The Reprehensibility Analysis

Under *BMW v. Gore* and *State Farm v. Campbell*, the reprehensibility of a defendant's conduct is "the most important indicium of the reasonableness of a punitive damages award." The court found multiple reprehensibility factors present:

1. **The harm was not merely economic but affected Cortez's daily life** — inability to obtain credit, stress, and damage to financial reputation
2. **Trans Union acted with indifference to the health and safety of others** — here, the "safety" interest is financial well-being
3. **The target was financially vulnerable** — consumers are inherently in a weaker position than major CRAs
4. **The conduct was repeated** — not a single isolated incident but a pattern across multiple dispute cycles
5. **The harm resulted from intentional malice, trickery, or deceit, or mere accident** — the pattern suggested deliberate corporate indifference, not accident

### Ratio Analysis

The court acknowledged that the Supreme Court has suggested that single-digit ratios (9:1 or less) are more likely to pass due process muster. However, the court noted that when compensatory damages are relatively small (as they often are in FCRA cases where actual harm is primarily emotional distress and lost credit opportunities), higher ratios may be appropriate because:

- A low punitive award would not adequately deter a multi-billion-dollar corporation
- The actual harm to the consumer may be difficult to quantify but very real
- FCRA violations affect millions of consumers, and punitive damages serve a deterrent function beyond the individual case

### Corporate Policy vs. Individual Error

The court emphasized that Trans Union's failures were not the result of a single employee's mistake but reflected **systemic inadequacies** in the company's dispute handling process. Evidence showed that Trans Union's reinvestigation procedures were designed to process disputes quickly and cheaply rather than accurately — a corporate cost-benefit analysis that prioritized efficiency over consumer protection.

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Cortez v. Trans Union

Cite *Cortez* in any case where:

1. **Client disputed multiple times and the CRA failed to correct** — This is the core *Cortez* fact pattern. If your client sent 2, 3, or more dispute letters and the CRA kept reporting the same inaccuracy, *Cortez* directly supports willfulness.

2. **Arguing for punitive damages** — *Cortez* is the strongest appellate case affirming a substantial FCRA punitive damages award. The $1.8 million figure demonstrates that significant punitive awards are available and will be upheld.

3. **Opposing defense arguments that "one mistake isn't willful"** — Defense counsel routinely argues that a single reporting error cannot constitute willfulness. *Cortez* demonstrates that repeated failure to correct after notice transforms negligence into willfulness.

4. **Supporting the argument that consumer disputes constitute "clear notice"** — Defense counsel may argue that the CRA did not "know" the information was wrong. *Cortez* establishes that the consumer's dispute itself provides sufficient notice.

5. **Bifurcation motions** — When the defense moves to bifurcate punitive damages, cite *Cortez* for the proposition that evidence of the CRA's pattern of conduct (multiple ignored disputes) is relevant to both liability and punitive damages, making bifurcation inappropriate.

6. **Motions in limine to exclude evidence of CRA's net worth** — *Cortez* supports admitting evidence of the CRA's financial condition for punitive damages purposes, as this is a *Gore*/*Campbell* factor.

### Pleading Language

**For willfulness based on multiple ignored disputes:**

> "Defendant Trans Union's violations were willful within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681n. Plaintiff submitted [NUMBER] written disputes to Defendant, each clearly identifying the inaccurate [TRADELINE/INFORMATION] and explaining that it was erroneous. Despite receiving this clear and repeated notice, Defendant failed to correct the inaccuracy and continued to report false information to third parties. This pattern of repeated failure to correct known inaccuracies demonstrates reckless disregard for Plaintiff's rights under the FCRA. See Cortez v. Trans Union, LLC, 617 F.3d 688, 708 (3d Cir. 2010) (affirming $1.8 million punitive damages award where CRA repeatedly ignored consumer disputes); Safeco Ins. Co. of Am. v. Burr, 551 U.S. 47, 57 (2007) (willfulness includes reckless disregard of statutory duty)."

**For punitive damages prayer:**

> "Defendant's willful noncompliance entitles Plaintiff to punitive damages under 15 U.S.C. § 1681n(a)(2). Defendant received clear notice of the inaccuracy through Plaintiff's multiple disputes, yet continued to report the same false information without meaningful corrective action. Such a pattern of deliberate indifference to consumer rights warrants substantial punitive damages to punish Defendant's misconduct and deter similar conduct in the future. See Cortez v. Trans Union, LLC, 617 F.3d 688 (3d Cir. 2010) (upholding $1.8 million punitive damages award against Trans Union for pattern of ignoring consumer disputes)."

**For opposing summary judgment on willfulness:**

> "Defendant's motion for summary judgment on willfulness must be denied. The evidence establishes that Plaintiff submitted multiple disputes, each clearly notifying Defendant of the inaccuracy. Defendant's repeated failure to correct the information after receiving this notice creates a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether Defendant acted with reckless disregard for Plaintiff's statutory rights. See Cortez v. Trans Union, LLC, 617 F.3d 688, 708 (3d Cir. 2010) (holding that pattern of ignoring multiple disputes supports jury finding of willfulness). The question of willfulness is for the jury."

**For Daubert/expert motions regarding punitive damages:**

> "The $1.8 million punitive damages award in Cortez v. Trans Union, LLC, 617 F.3d 688 (3d Cir. 2010), demonstrates that substantial punitive awards are appropriate in FCRA cases involving CRA willfulness. There, as here, the CRA received multiple clear notices of inaccuracy and failed to take corrective action. Plaintiff's punitive damages claim is supported by evidence of Defendant's reprehensible conduct, its vast financial resources, and the need to deter similar conduct affecting millions of consumers."

### Discovery Requests Supported by Cortez

**Interrogatories:**

1. "For each dispute submitted by Plaintiff, describe in detail the reinvestigation steps Defendant undertook, including: (a) the date the dispute was received; (b) the employee(s) assigned; (c) the sources contacted; (d) the documents reviewed; (e) the analysis performed; (f) the conclusion reached; and (g) the date the results were communicated to Plaintiff."

2. "State whether Defendant's reinvestigation of any of Plaintiff's disputes resulted in a different conclusion than the prior dispute regarding the same information. If not, explain why multiple reinvestigations produced identical results."

3. "Identify all internal communications, memoranda, reports, or correspondence regarding Plaintiff's disputes, including any communications reflecting awareness that the consumer had disputed the same information multiple times."

4. "State the total number of disputes Defendant received from all consumers during [RELEVANT YEAR] regarding [THIS FURNISHER'S] reporting, and the percentage that resulted in correction or deletion of the disputed information."

**Requests for Production:**

5. "Produce all documents reflecting Defendant's awareness that Plaintiff had submitted multiple disputes regarding the same information, including but not limited to dispute tracking records, escalation logs, and supervisor review notes."

6. "Produce Defendant's policies and procedures for handling repeat disputes — i.e., disputes from the same consumer regarding the same tradeline or the same inaccuracy."

7. "Produce all training materials provided to Defendant's dispute resolution employees regarding the handling of multiple disputes from the same consumer."

8. "Produce documents sufficient to show Defendant's net worth, annual revenue, and net income for the three most recent fiscal years. (For punitive damages calculation — supported by Cortez, 617 F.3d at 714.)"

9. "Produce all communications between Defendant and [FURNISHER] regarding Plaintiff's disputes, including all e-OSCAR ACDVs, AUD requests, and any non-automated communications."

10. "Produce any internal audits, compliance reviews, or quality assurance reports regarding Defendant's dispute handling procedures for the period [DATE] through [DATE]."

**30(b)(6) Deposition Topics:**

11. "Defendant's policies and procedures for escalating or differently handling disputes when a consumer has disputed the same information multiple times."

12. "Whether Defendant tracks or monitors the number of times a consumer disputes the same information, and if so, what action (if any) is triggered by multiple disputes."

13. "The factors Defendant considers in determining whether its reinvestigation conclusion is correct when a consumer disputes the same information repeatedly."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Build your paper trail:** *Cortez* is strongest when the consumer has sent multiple, clear dispute letters. Advise clients to dispute in writing (not phone), keep copies, send via certified mail, and be specific about what is wrong. Each ignored dispute strengthens the willfulness case.

2. **Document every dispute cycle:** Create a timeline showing (1) date of dispute, (2) CRA's response, (3) whether the inaccuracy was corrected, (4) date of next credit pull showing continued inaccuracy. This timeline IS your willfulness evidence.

3. **NDGA judges and punitive damages:** Northern District of Georgia judges have been receptive to FCRA punitive damages claims where the evidence shows repeated corporate indifference. Cite *Cortez* alongside *Cushman* and 11th Circuit willfulness standards from *Cahlin v. General Motors Acceptance Corp.*, 936 F.2d 1151 (11th Cir. 1991).

4. **Multiplier effect:** In your damages model, use *Cortez*'s $1.8M as an anchor point. Even if your case warrants a smaller award, the existence of a $1.8M appellate-upheld FCRA punitive award shifts the defense's settlement calculus significantly.

5. **Pre-suit demand strategy:** Before filing, send a final demand letter referencing the multiple unanswered disputes and noting that courts have upheld seven-figure punitive awards for this exact conduct pattern. This can facilitate pre-litigation settlement.

6. **Pair with Equifax/Experian data:** If your client disputed with multiple CRAs and one corrected while another did not, this strengthens the willfulness argument — the correcting CRA's action shows the error was detectable, making the non-correcting CRA's failure more culpable.

7. **Corporate representative deposition:** Depose the CRA's 30(b)(6) witness on whether the company tracks repeat disputes and what (if any) escalation procedures exist. If no escalation procedures exist, this supports systemic indifference — the *Cortez* theory.

8. **Avoid the "low actual damages" trap:** Defense will argue that punitive damages are excessive relative to actual damages. Counter with *Cortez* (where the ratio was high and affirmed) and *BMW v. Gore* language that higher ratios are permissible when actual damages are difficult to quantify but the conduct is highly reprehensible.

## Related Cases

- **Safeco Ins. Co. of Am. v. Burr**, 551 U.S. 47 (2007) — Defines "willful" to include reckless disregard; foundation for *Cortez*'s willfulness analysis
- **Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.**, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997) — Reinvestigation must be meaningful; perfunctory procedures are insufficient
- **BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore**, 517 U.S. 559 (1996) — Three guideposts for evaluating punitive damages: reprehensibility, ratio, comparable penalties
- **State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell**, 538 U.S. 408 (2003) — Refines *Gore* punitive damages analysis
- **Dalton v. Capital Associated Industries**, 257 F.3d 409 (4th Cir. 2001) — Even single inaccuracy can support willfulness when CRA had clear notice
- **Dennis v. BEH-1, LLC**, 520 F.3d 1066 (9th Cir. 2008) — Reinsertion violations and reinvestigation inadequacy
- **Philbin v. Trans Union Corp.**, 101 F.3d 957 (3d Cir. 1996) — CRA liability for reporting disputed information without adequate procedures
- **Guimond v. Trans Union Credit Info. Co.**, 45 F.3d 1329 (9th Cir. 1995) — Jury question on reinvestigation adequacy; punitive damages available

## Key Takeaway for Practice

*Cortez v. Trans Union* is the single strongest punitive damages case in FCRA consumer litigation. The $1.8 million award — upheld on appeal — establishes that CRAs face massive financial exposure when they repeatedly ignore consumer disputes. For NDGA practice, this case transforms every multiple-dispute scenario from a "small" FCRA case into a potentially seven-figure case. Build your dispute paper trail, document each failure cycle, and use *Cortez* as both a litigation weapon and a settlement lever. Defense counsel at Equifax, Experian, and Trans Union know this case — invoking it signals that you are pursuing punitive damages seriously.
