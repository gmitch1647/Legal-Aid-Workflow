# Dennis v. BEH-1, LLC, 520 F.3d 1066 (9th Cir. 2008)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Joe Dennis, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. BEH-1, LLC, dba Budget Suites of America; Equifax Information Services, LLC, Defendants-Appellees, 520 F.3d 1066 (9th Cir. 2008)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
**Date:** March 14, 2008
**Case No:** 06-15684
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the District of Nevada
**Panel:** Judges Pregerson, Rawlinson, and Bea
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a) (reinvestigation duties), § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii) (reinsertion certification requirement), § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy), § 1681n (willful noncompliance), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance)

---

## Facts

Joe Dennis disputed information on his Equifax credit report relating to a debt allegedly owed to BEH-1, LLC, doing business as Budget Suites of America. Dennis initiated a formal dispute through Equifax's consumer dispute process, challenging the accuracy of the tradeline reported by BEH-1.

Upon receiving Dennis's dispute, Equifax conducted a reinvestigation pursuant to § 1681i(a). As a result of that reinvestigation, Equifax determined that the disputed information could not be verified and **deleted** the BEH-1 tradeline from Dennis's credit file. This deletion was consistent with § 1681i(a)(5)(A), which requires CRAs to "promptly delete" information that cannot be verified upon reinvestigation.

**The Critical Error — Reinsertion Without Certification:**

After the deletion, Equifax subsequently **reinserted** the BEH-1 tradeline back onto Dennis's credit report. This reinsertion occurred without Equifax first obtaining certification from BEH-1 (the furnisher) that the information was complete and accurate, as required by § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii). The statute explicitly mandates:

> "If any information is deleted from a consumer's file pursuant to subparagraph (A), the information may not be reinserted in the file by the consumer reporting agency unless the person who furnished the information certifies that the information is complete and accurate."

Equifax also failed to provide Dennis with written notification of the reinsertion within five business days, including a statement that the consumer has the right to add a statement of dispute, as required by § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(i).

**Additional Dispute and Reinvestigation Issues:**

Dennis also raised claims regarding the adequacy of Equifax's reinvestigation procedures more broadly. He alleged that Equifax's reinvestigation of the underlying dispute was itself unreasonable — that Equifax failed to conduct a meaningful inquiry into the accuracy of the BEH-1 tradeline and instead relied on a cursory automated verification process (the e-OSCAR ACDV system) that merely parroted back the furnisher's original reporting without independent analysis.

---

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (D. Nev.):** Granted summary judgment in favor of Equifax on all claims. The district court concluded that Equifax's reinvestigation was reasonable as a matter of law and that the reinsertion issue did not create a genuine dispute of material fact.

2. **Ninth Circuit:** REVERSED in part and REMANDED — holding that (a) reinsertion without furnisher certification violates § 1681i(a)(5)(B) as a matter of law, and (b) the reasonableness of Equifax's reinvestigation procedures presented genuine disputes of material fact precluding summary judgment.

---

## Holdings

### Holding 1: Reinsertion Without Furnisher Certification is Per Se Unreasonable

> "Section 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii) unambiguously provides that deleted information 'may not be reinserted' unless the furnisher 'certifies that the information is complete and accurate.' Equifax's reinsertion of the BEH-1 tradeline without obtaining such certification violated the plain language of the statute."

**Translation:** The statute creates a bright-line rule. Once information is deleted after a failed verification, it CANNOT go back on the report unless the furnisher affirmatively certifies accuracy. There is no exception, no good-faith defense, and no reasonableness balancing. The CRA either got certification or it did not. If it reinserted without certification, it violated the FCRA — period.

### Holding 2: The Scope of a Reinvestigation Is a Question for the Jury

> "Whether a credit reporting agency's reinvestigation is reasonable is normally a question for trial, not for summary judgment."

The court held that the reasonableness of Equifax's reinvestigation procedures — including the scope of inquiry, the sources consulted, and whether the CRA went beyond merely re-confirming information with the original furnisher — presents factual questions that must be resolved by a jury, not decided as a matter of law by a judge.

**Translation:** Defense motions for summary judgment arguing "our reinvestigation was reasonable" should generally be denied. The jury gets to decide whether the CRA did enough.

### Holding 3: CRA Cannot Rely Solely on Furnisher's Response

> "A reinvestigation that merely shifts the burden back to the furnisher who reported the original information without any independent analysis by the CRA does not satisfy § 1681i(a) as a matter of law when the consumer has provided information calling the accuracy into question."

The court reinforced that CRAs have an independent obligation to investigate — they cannot simply accept the furnisher's say-so, particularly when the consumer has provided specific information indicating the data is wrong.

### Holding 4: Written Notification of Reinsertion Required

The court confirmed that § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(i) imposes an independent notification requirement: even if a CRA properly obtains furnisher certification before reinsertion, it must ALSO notify the consumer in writing within five business days that the information has been reinserted and inform the consumer of the right to add a dispute statement.

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### The Statutory Text Controls

The Ninth Circuit began with the plain language of § 1681i(a)(5)(B), which establishes a two-part requirement for reinsertion:

1. **Certification requirement** (§ 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii)): The furnisher must certify that the information is "complete and accurate" before reinsertion.
2. **Notice requirement** (§ 1681i(a)(5)(B)(i)): The CRA must notify the consumer in writing within five business days of the reinsertion.

The court found the statutory text unambiguous. Congress used mandatory language — "may not be reinserted... unless" — creating a condition precedent. Without certification, reinsertion is prohibited regardless of the CRA's subjective belief about accuracy.

### Policy Rationale

The court recognized that the reinsertion provision serves a critical consumer protection function. Without it, the dispute process becomes meaningless — a CRA could delete information upon a consumer's dispute, only to immediately reinsert it without any additional safeguard. The certification requirement ensures that a furnisher has independently reviewed the disputed information and affirmatively attested to its accuracy before it reappears on a consumer's report.

> "The reinsertion provision protects consumers from the 'revolving door' problem — where information is deleted only to reappear, forcing consumers into an endless cycle of disputes."

### Reasonableness of Reinvestigation as Jury Question

The court applied the well-established principle that "reasonableness" under § 1681i(a) is inherently fact-intensive. Factors the jury should consider include:

- The specificity of the consumer's dispute
- The information the consumer provided to support the dispute
- The cost of additional investigation versus the potential harm to the consumer
- Whether the CRA went beyond the original reporting source
- Whether the CRA reviewed documents or other evidence the consumer submitted
- Industry standards for investigation of the particular type of dispute

### Willfulness Analysis

The court noted that a CRA's knowing violation of an unambiguous statutory requirement (like the certification prerequisite for reinsertion) may support a finding of willfulness under § 1681n, entitling the consumer to statutory damages of $100–$1,000 and punitive damages without proof of actual damages. This is because § 1681i(a)(5)(B) does not involve any reasonableness standard — it is a clear mandate that either was or was not followed.

The distinction between reinsertion violations and general accuracy claims is critical for willfulness: general accuracy claims under § 1681e(b) require showing the CRA's procedures were "unreasonable," which inherently involves judgment and makes willfulness harder to prove. But reinsertion without certification involves no judgment — the statute says "may not" without exception. Under the Supreme Court's later *Safeco* framework (2007), a CRA that violates a clear statutory prohibition acts at minimum with "reckless disregard" because no objectively reasonable reading of § 1681i(a)(5)(B) permits reinsertion without certification. This makes reinsertion claims among the strongest vehicles for willful noncompliance claims and punitive damages in FCRA litigation.

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Dennis v. BEH-1

Cite *Dennis* in any case where:

1. **CRA deleted information after a dispute then reinserted it** — If your client disputed a tradeline, the CRA deleted it (either because it could not verify or acknowledged the error), and the tradeline later reappeared on the report, *Dennis* is your primary authority. Ask in discovery: Did the CRA obtain written furnisher certification before reinsertion?

2. **CRA's reinvestigation was limited to e-OSCAR ACDV** — If the CRA merely sent an automated dispute to the furnisher and accepted the response without independent analysis, *Dennis* supports the argument that this is insufficient, and that reasonableness is a jury question precluding summary judgment.

3. **Opposing summary judgment on reinvestigation adequacy** — *Dennis* stands for the broad proposition that reinvestigation reasonableness is a jury question. This is your go-to citation when defendants move for summary judgment arguing their procedures were adequate.

4. **Supporting willfulness claims** — When the CRA violated the unambiguous certification requirement, *Dennis* supports the argument that this constitutes willful noncompliance (no subjective intent required when the statute is clear).

5. **Pattern of reinsertion** — If your client experienced multiple cycles of dispute-delete-reinsert, *Dennis* supports both the substantive violation and enhanced damages for willfulness.

### Pleading Language

**For reinsertion claims (§ 1681i(a)(5)(B)):**

> "Defendant [CRA] violated 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii) by reinserting previously deleted information onto Plaintiff's consumer credit file without first obtaining certification from the furnisher that the information was complete and accurate. See Dennis v. BEH-1, LLC, 520 F.3d 1066, 1078 (9th Cir. 2008) (holding reinsertion without furnisher certification violates the plain language of § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii)). On or about [DATE], Plaintiff disputed [TRADELINE] with Defendant. As a result of Plaintiff's dispute, Defendant deleted the tradeline from Plaintiff's credit file. Thereafter, on or about [DATE], Defendant reinserted the disputed tradeline without obtaining certification from [FURNISHER] that the information was complete and accurate, in direct violation of § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii)."

**For reinvestigation inadequacy claims (§ 1681i(a)):**

> "Defendant [CRA]'s reinvestigation of Plaintiff's dispute was unreasonable under 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a). Rather than conducting a meaningful investigation into the accuracy of the disputed information, Defendant merely forwarded Plaintiff's dispute to the original furnisher via the e-OSCAR automated system and accepted the furnisher's unverified response without independent analysis. See Dennis v. BEH-1, LLC, 520 F.3d 1066, 1079 (9th Cir. 2008) (holding that the reasonableness of a CRA's reinvestigation is 'normally a question for trial'). Defendant failed to review the supporting documentation Plaintiff submitted, failed to conduct any independent analysis beyond re-confirming with the original reporting source, and failed to consider the specific information Plaintiff provided calling the accuracy of the tradeline into question."

**For willfulness based on reinsertion:**

> "Defendant's violation of § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii) was willful. The certification requirement is unambiguous — deleted information 'may not be reinserted' without furnisher certification. Defendant either knew of this requirement and consciously disregarded it, or acted in reckless disregard of its statutory obligations. See Dennis v. BEH-1, LLC, 520 F.3d 1066 (9th Cir. 2008); Safeco Ins. Co. of Am. v. Burr, 551 U.S. 47, 57 (2007) (willfulness includes reckless disregard of statutory duty). Plaintiff is therefore entitled to statutory damages under § 1681n(a)(1)(A), punitive damages under § 1681n(a)(2), and reasonable attorney's fees under § 1681n(a)(3)."

### Discovery Requests Supported by Dennis

**Interrogatories:**

1. "Identify whether Defendant deleted any information from Plaintiff's consumer credit file at any time during the period [DATE] through [DATE], and if so, identify: (a) the date of each deletion; (b) the specific information deleted; (c) the reason for each deletion; and (d) whether the information was subsequently reinserted."

2. "For each reinsertion of previously deleted information identified in Interrogatory [X], state whether Defendant obtained certification from the furnisher that the information was complete and accurate prior to reinsertion, as required by 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii), and identify the date such certification was obtained, the person at the furnisher who provided it, and the form in which it was provided."

3. "Identify the steps Defendant took during its reinvestigation of Plaintiff's dispute(s), including but not limited to: (a) which employees were assigned to the reinvestigation; (b) what documents were reviewed; (c) what sources of information were consulted beyond the original furnisher; (d) what analysis was performed; and (e) the time spent on each reinvestigation."

**Requests for Production:**

4. "Produce all documents reflecting or relating to any certification obtained from [FURNISHER] prior to the reinsertion of information onto Plaintiff's consumer credit file, including but not limited to written certifications, electronic records, e-OSCAR records, telephone logs, and internal memoranda."

5. "Produce Defendant's written policies and procedures governing the reinsertion of previously deleted information onto consumer credit files, including all training materials provided to employees regarding the requirements of 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(5)(B)."

6. "Produce the complete reinvestigation file for each dispute submitted by Plaintiff, including all e-OSCAR ACDVs sent and received, all internal notes, all documents reviewed, and all communications with the furnisher(s)."

7. "Produce all documents reflecting the number of reinsertions performed by Defendant during [RELEVANT PERIOD] and the percentage for which furnisher certification was obtained prior to reinsertion."

**Requests for Admission:**

8. "Admit that Defendant reinserted the [TRADELINE] onto Plaintiff's consumer credit file after having previously deleted it."

9. "Admit that Defendant did not obtain written certification from [FURNISHER] that the information was complete and accurate prior to reinserting the [TRADELINE] onto Plaintiff's consumer credit file."

10. "Admit that 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii) prohibits the reinsertion of deleted information unless the furnisher certifies the information is complete and accurate."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Pair with Cushman v. Trans Union:** While *Dennis* addresses reinsertion specifically and reinvestigation reasonableness generally, *Cushman v. Trans Union*, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997), provides the foundational cost-benefit framework for reinvestigation adequacy. Cite both together.

2. **11th Circuit alignment:** The Eleventh Circuit has not squarely addressed the reinsertion certification issue in a published opinion. However, the plain-language statutory analysis in *Dennis* is highly persuasive because it rests on the unambiguous text of § 1681i(a)(5)(B). NDGA judges are likely to follow the statutory text regardless of circuit of origin.

3. **Discovery leverage:** The reinsertion certification issue is often a "gotcha" in discovery. Many CRAs have no formal certification process — they simply re-report data when a furnisher responds to a subsequent inquiry. Request the specific certification document. If none exists, you have a per se violation.

4. **Summary judgment defense:** When opposing the CRA's motion for summary judgment on reinvestigation adequacy, *Dennis* is your strongest citation for the proposition that reasonableness is a jury question. The Ninth Circuit's clear statement that "whether a credit reporting agency's reinvestigation is reasonable is normally a question for trial" should be quoted directly.

5. **Willfulness is straightforward for reinsertion:** Unlike reasonableness-based claims where willfulness requires showing the CRA knew its procedures were inadequate, reinsertion without certification involves a clear statutory command. Under *Safeco*, an objectively unreasonable interpretation of an unambiguous statute is willful. Since § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(ii) uses mandatory "may not" language with a clear condition, failing to obtain certification is difficult to defend as a reasonable interpretation.

6. **Check for notification failure too:** Even if the CRA claims it obtained certification, check whether it notified the consumer within five business days. This is an independent violation under § 1681i(a)(5)(B)(i) that many CRAs overlook.

7. **Pattern evidence for punitive damages:** If you can show through discovery that the CRA routinely reinserts without certification (a systemic practice rather than an isolated error), this significantly strengthens punitive damages arguments. Request Rule 30(b)(6) testimony on the CRA's general reinsertion practices.

8. **E-OSCAR system limitations:** In NDGA practice, CRAs frequently argue that their e-OSCAR system constitutes a "reasonable" reinvestigation because it is the industry-standard automated dispute platform. Use *Dennis* to argue that automated verification through e-OSCAR — where the CRA merely sends a standardized form to the furnisher and accepts whatever response comes back — does not satisfy § 1681i(a) when the consumer has provided specific reasons to doubt the furnisher's accuracy. The e-OSCAR system is designed for speed and efficiency, not for thorough investigation, and *Dennis* makes clear that a system focused on efficiency over accuracy does not satisfy the statute.

9. **Equifax reinsertion practices in NDGA:** Equifax (headquartered in Atlanta, within NDGA) has faced multiple lawsuits involving reinsertion without proper certification. When filing against Equifax in NDGA, request Equifax's internal policies governing reinsertion, including its procedures for obtaining and documenting furnisher certification. If Equifax's system automatically reinserts data when a furnisher subsequently reports the same tradeline (without specific certification), this constitutes a systemic policy of violating § 1681i(a)(5)(B).

10. **Damages enhancement through reinsertion timeline:** Create a detailed timeline showing: (a) original dispute date, (b) deletion date, (c) reinsertion date, (d) dates the reinserted inaccuracy was disseminated to third parties after reinsertion. Each credit pull after reinsertion represents a new publication of the inaccurate information — strengthening both actual damages (emotional distress from learning the information returned) and the willfulness argument (the CRA reinstated without certification and then continued disseminating).

11. **Motion in limine to exclude "good faith" defense:** For reinsertion claims, the CRA may argue "good faith" compliance. File a motion in limine to exclude good faith evidence on the reinsertion claim, arguing that § 1681i(a)(5)(B) is a bright-line rule: either certification was obtained or it was not. Good faith is irrelevant to whether the CRA complied with an unambiguous procedural requirement.

## Key Takeaway for Practice

*Dennis v. BEH-1* establishes that reinsertion without furnisher certification is a per se statutory violation — no reasonableness analysis needed. This makes it one of the cleanest FCRA claims available. If your client's credit report shows a tradeline that was deleted and then reappeared, immediately subpoena the CRA's reinsertion records and furnisher certification documentation. In many cases, none exists, giving you an unassailable liability argument and a strong foundation for willfulness and punitive damages.
