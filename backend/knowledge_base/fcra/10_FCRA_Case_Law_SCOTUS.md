# FCRA Case Law: U.S. Supreme Court Decisions  
  
This file collects the Supreme Court decisions that have shaped Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) litigation. They are presented in chronological order of decision (with one earlier statute-of-limitations case noted at the end, because it predates the others but remains foundational on the timing of FCRA claims).  
  
---  
  
## Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr, 551 U.S. 47 (2007)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2007  
**Vote:** Unanimous as to judgment; Justice Souter writing for the Court, with various concurrences and partial dissents (Stevens, Ginsburg, Thomas)  
**Author:** Justice David Souter  
  
### Holding  
A "willful" violation of the FCRA under 15 U.S.C. § 1681n covers both knowing and reckless disregard of the statute's requirements. A defendant has not acted in reckless disregard, however, if its reading of the statute was not "objectively unreasonable" — meaning it had a foundation in the statutory text and there was no contrary, authoritative interpretation (e.g., from an appellate court or the FTC).  
  
### Facts  
Two consolidated cases, Safeco v. Burr and GEICO v. Edo, presented the question of when an insurer's failure to send an "adverse action" notice under § 1681m(a) after charging a consumer a higher rate based on a credit-based insurance score amounts to a willful violation supporting statutory and punitive damages under § 1681n. Safeco interpreted "adverse action" not to include the initial rate offered to a new customer; GEICO interpreted it differently but argued its reading was at least reasonable. Plaintiffs in both cases sought class-wide statutory and punitive damages.  
  
### Key reasoning  
The Court first construed "willfully" against the common-law backdrop, where the term reaches both knowing and reckless violations of legal duty. Drawing on the civil and criminal recklessness traditions, the Court held that "reckless disregard" requires action that entails "an unjustifiably high risk of harm that is either known or so obvious that it should be known." Mere negligence is not enough.  
  
Second, the Court fashioned the "objectively unreasonable" benchmark that now governs willfulness defenses. A company does not act in reckless disregard of the FCRA when its reading of the statute, although ultimately wrong, was not objectively unreasonable. The reading must have a foundation in the text, and there must be no contrary authority from a court of appeals or definitive guidance from the FTC (now CFPB) at the time of the conduct that should have warned the defendant off the interpretation.  
  
Applying that test, the Court held that Safeco's reading of "adverse action" (excluding initial rate decisions) was not objectively unreasonable, so it had not acted willfully — even though the Court rejected that reading on the merits. GEICO had not even committed a violation under the proper reading because its score-based price was not higher than the price Edo would have received without credit information.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- The "objectively unreasonable" test is the single most important defense to a § 1681n willfulness claim. A defendant with a colorable, text-based reading of an unsettled FCRA provision will almost always escape statutory and punitive damages, even if it loses on the merits.  
- Plaintiffs seeking statutory or punitive damages must show either (a) actual knowledge of the violation or (b) recklessness under the "unjustifiably high risk" standard.  
- The defense is weakest where there is binding circuit precedent, a definitive CFPB/FTC interpretation, or unambiguous statutory text against the defendant's reading.  
- Negligent violations remain actionable under § 1681o, but recover only actual damages plus attorney's fees — no statutory or punitive damages.  
- Practical litigation point: defendants often try to convert willfulness into a question of law decided at summary judgment by framing their interpretation as objectively reasonable; plaintiffs respond by identifying contrary authority known to the defendant.  
  
### Related statute sections  
15 U.S.C. § 1681n (civil liability for willful noncompliance); § 1681o (negligent noncompliance); § 1681m (adverse-action notice requirements, as in effect at the time)

## Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330 (2016)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2016  
**Vote:** 6-2 (Ginsburg and Sotomayor dissenting)  
**Author:** Justice Samuel Alito  
  
### Holding  
Article III standing requires an injury in fact that is both "concrete" and "particularized." A bare procedural violation of a statute, divorced from any concrete harm to the plaintiff, is insufficient — even where Congress has created a statutory right and a cause of action. Intangible harms (including risk of real harm) can be concrete, but the lower court must independently analyze concreteness; it cannot collapse the inquiry into particularization.  
  
### Facts  
Thomas Robins discovered that Spokeo's "people search" website displayed an inaccurate profile of him: it listed him as employed, married, with children, in his fifties, and affluent — none of which he claimed was true. He brought a putative class action alleging Spokeo had willfully violated FCRA's accuracy and procedural requirements applicable to consumer reporting agencies. The Ninth Circuit held he had standing based on the alleged statutory violations and the asserted personalized harm. The Supreme Court vacated and remanded.  
  
### Key reasoning  
The Court reaffirmed the irreducible Article III triad: injury in fact, causation, and redressability. Injury in fact requires both particularization (affecting the plaintiff in a personal and individual way) and concreteness (actual existence, in fact, in the real world). The Ninth Circuit had analyzed particularization but skipped the separate concreteness requirement.  
  
The Court emphasized that "concrete" does not mean "tangible." Intangible harms — reputational injury, restrictions on free exercise of religion, denial of access to information — can be concrete. Congress's judgment that a particular harm is real and legally cognizable is "instructive and important," and Congress may elevate previously inadequate harms to the status of cognizable injuries. But Congress cannot wholly dispense with the concreteness requirement: "Article III standing requires a concrete injury even in the context of a statutory violation."  
  
A bare procedural violation, divorced from any concrete harm, cannot satisfy Article III. The Court offered examples: a consumer reporting agency failing to provide notice to a user could be a procedural violation that "may result in no harm" (e.g., if the user got the information from another source) or could in some cases cause concrete harm (e.g., if it led to a materially inaccurate report). The Ninth Circuit had to make this determination as to Robins's specific allegations on remand.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- Plaintiffs must plead and prove concrete harm — not merely a statutory violation. "Naked" procedural-violation theories are foreclosed in federal court.  
- Spokeo opened the door to widespread "no standing" defenses in FCRA class actions; many cases were sent back to state court or dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.  
- Intangible harms count if they have a "close relationship" to harms traditionally recognized at common law (defamation, intrusion upon seclusion, etc.) — this language became the template TransUnion later applied more rigorously.  
- The case did not decide the merits, only standing. On remand the Ninth Circuit found Robins did have standing because the alleged inaccuracies (employment, marital status) created a real risk of harm.  
- For defense counsel: standing is a renewable challenge that can be raised at the pleading stage, at class certification, at summary judgment, at trial, and on appeal.  
  
### Related statute sections  
15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures for accuracy); § 1681n (willful noncompliance); Article III of the U.S. Constitution

## TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413 (2021)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2021  
**Vote:** 5-4 (Thomas, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan dissenting)  
**Author:** Justice Brett Kavanaugh  
  
### Holding  
"No concrete harm, no standing." Each member of a class seeking damages in federal court must have suffered a concrete harm — risk of future harm, without materialization, is generally insufficient in a damages action. The mere existence of inaccurate information in a credit reporting agency's internal database, without dissemination to a third party, is not a concrete injury.  
  
### Facts  
TransUnion sold lenders a product that flagged consumer files when the consumer's name matched a name on the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) list of suspected terrorists, drug traffickers, and other criminals. The matching algorithm used only first and last names and produced many false positives. Sergio Ramirez, who is not on the OFAC list, was identified as a potential match when he tried to buy a car, and the dealership refused to sell to him. He brought a class action on behalf of 8,185 consumers TransUnion had flagged as OFAC matches. Of those, the parties stipulated that 1,853 had their misleading reports actually disseminated to a third-party business during the class period; the remaining 6,332 had inaccurate flags sitting in TransUnion's internal files but no third-party dissemination.  
  
### Key reasoning  
The Court applied and extended Spokeo. It reaffirmed that Article III concreteness requires an injury with a "close relationship" to a harm traditionally recognized at common law. Reputational injury — like defamation — qualifies, but defamation traditionally required publication. The 1,853 class members whose reports were sent to third parties had a close analogue to a defamation injury and therefore had standing. The 6,332 class members whose misleading internal files were never published lacked a close common-law analogue and therefore lacked standing, even though TransUnion had violated the FCRA as to them.  
  
The Court rejected the argument that the mere risk of future harm — that the inaccurate report might be disclosed someday — was sufficient in a suit for damages. Risk of future harm may support standing for injunctive relief but, absent materialization, does not constitute concrete harm for a damages claim.  
  
The Court also rejected the so-called "format" claims (alleging that TransUnion sent OFAC notices in confusing formats and on separate mailings) as not supplying concrete harm beyond technical statutory violations. The Court treated standing as a class-wide requirement: every class member must independently satisfy Article III; named-plaintiff standing is not enough at the damages stage.  
  
The dissenters (Thomas, joined by Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan) argued that Congress's creation of a private right against a defendant for invasion of a private right traditionally suffices for Article III; Justice Kagan separately argued Spokeo had been too narrowly extended.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- TransUnion is the most consequential FCRA standing decision. Plaintiffs must allege and ultimately prove that inaccurate information was actually disclosed to a third party, or that they otherwise suffered a real-world consequence (denied credit, lost job, emotional distress with downstream effects, etc.).  
- Class certification is significantly harder: courts must screen for absent class members who lack standing, which can defeat predominance or commonality. Many post-TransUnion classes are decertified or narrowed.  
- The "close historical or common-law analogue" test now governs the concreteness analysis. Plaintiffs map their FCRA claim onto torts such as defamation (publication), intrusion upon seclusion (privacy), or breach of confidence.  
- Internal database inaccuracies, standing alone, no longer support federal damages actions — even where the FCRA was clearly violated.  
- Risk-of-future-harm theories survive for injunctive relief but not damages, after materialization.  
- Standing arguments can push FCRA cases to state court (where Article III does not apply) — a strategic consideration for both sides.  
  
### Related statute sections  
15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures); § 1681g(a)(1), (c)(2) (disclosure to consumer; summary of rights); Article III of the U.S. Constitution

## Department of Agriculture Rural Development Rural Housing Service v. Kirtz, 601 U.S. 42 (2024)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2024  
**Vote:** Unanimous (9-0)  
**Author:** Justice Neil Gorsuch  
  
### Holding  
The FCRA unequivocally waives the sovereign immunity of the United States. Because the Act authorizes consumer suits for damages against "any person" who willfully or negligently fails to comply, and because "person" is defined to include "any . . . government or governmental subdivision or agency," consumers may sue federal agencies directly for FCRA violations.  
  
### Facts  
Reginald Kirtz held a loan from the USDA's Rural Housing Service. After paying off the loan, he alleged USDA continued to report the account as past due to TransUnion, damaging his credit. Kirtz also alleged a private lender, AgChoice Farm Credit, made the same kind of error. He sued both under § 1681s-2(b) for failing to conduct reasonable investigations after notice of dispute. USDA moved to dismiss on sovereign-immunity grounds. The district court dismissed; the Third Circuit reversed, joining the Seventh Circuit and creating a split with the D.C. and Ninth Circuits.  
  
### Key reasoning  
Justice Gorsuch's opinion proceeded from the foundational rule that the United States is immune from suit unless Congress has clearly waived immunity. Two FCRA provisions, read together, supply that clear statement: § 1681n and § 1681o authorize consumer suits against "any person" for willful or negligent FCRA noncompliance, and § 1681a(b) defines "person" to include "any individual, partnership, corporation, trust, estate, cooperative, association, government or governmental subdivision or agency, or other entity."  
  
The government argued that the term "government" should be limited to state governments and political subdivisions, that the FCRA's enforcement provisions were aimed at private actors, and that other federal statutes show Congress knows how to be more explicit. The Court rejected each argument: nothing in the text limits "government" to nonfederal entities; the enforcement and definitional provisions must be read together; and clear-statement rules do not demand "magic words."  
  
The Court also rejected USDA's appeal to constitutional-avoidance principles (the suggestion that allowing suits against the United States would raise serious separation-of-powers questions). The waiver question turned on ordinary statutory interpretation.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- Federal agencies that furnish information to credit reporting agencies — USDA, SBA, IRS (for some categories), VA, Education Department (federal student loans), DOJ, etc. — are subject to suit under the FCRA for both negligent and willful violations.  
- Damages, including punitive damages for willful violations, are available against the United States under § 1681n.  
- The decision is especially important for federal-student-loan borrowers, USDA rural-housing borrowers, and SBA borrowers, who frequently encounter furnishing errors.  
- The CFPB's longstanding position (that federal furnishers are covered) is now Supreme Court law.  
- Practical pleading point: § 1681s-2(b) furnisher claims must still come through a CRA's notice of dispute — direct disputes to the agency alone are not enough for a private suit.  
  
### Related statute sections  
15 U.S.C. § 1681a(b) (definition of "person"); § 1681n (willful noncompliance, civil liability); § 1681o (negligent noncompliance); § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duties on dispute); sovereign immunity doctrine

## TRW Inc. v. Andrews, 534 U.S. 19 (2001)  
  
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States  
**Year:** 2001  
**Vote:** Unanimous (9-0)  
**Author:** Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg (Justice Scalia concurring in the judgment)  
  
### Holding  
Under the pre-2003 version of FCRA's statute of limitations, the two-year clock generally began to run from the date of the violation, not from the date the consumer discovered it. The single statutory exception — for cases involving material misrepresentation by the defendant that the consumer would have discovered — does not imply a general federal discovery rule. (Note: Congress amended § 1681p in 2003 by FACTA, partially overruling this case by adopting a discovery rule. The decision still matters for reading FCRA limitations text strictly and for older claims.)  
  
### Facts  
TRW (then a consumer reporting agency) furnished four copies of Adelaide Andrews's credit report containing inaccurate information based on an identity thief's use of her Social Security number. Andrews did not learn of the disclosures until more than a year and a half after the earliest disclosure, when she tried to refinance her home. She sued more than two years after the first two disclosures but within two years of discovery. The Ninth Circuit held a general federal discovery rule applied; the Supreme Court reversed.  
  
### Key reasoning  
The Court rejected any presumption that all federal statutes of limitations are subject to an implicit discovery rule. It emphasized that § 1681p, as then written, contained a specific statutory exception triggering discovery accrual in "material and willful misrepresentation" cases — and applying a general discovery rule on top of that exception would render the exception superfluous. Where Congress has expressly delineated when discovery delays accrual, courts cannot enlarge that exception by judicial gloss.  
  
### Impact / what the bot should know  
- The 2003 FACTA amendment changed § 1681p to provide a discovery rule for all FCRA claims: the limitations period is the earlier of "2 years after the date of discovery by the plaintiff of the violation that is the basis for such liability" or "5 years after the date on which the violation that is the basis for such liability occurs."  
- For modern claims, plaintiffs typically have 2 years from discovery (capped by 5 years from the violation). TRW still matters as a textualist anchor and for pre-2003 conduct.  
- TRW also reinforces a methodological point: courts read FCRA's text closely and reject judge-made overlays not supported by Congress.  
  
### Related statute sections  
15 U.S.C. § 1681p (jurisdiction and limitations); FACTA amendments (Pub. L. 108-159) (2003)
