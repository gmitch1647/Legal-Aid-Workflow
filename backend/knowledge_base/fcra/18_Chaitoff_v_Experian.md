# Chaitoff v. Experian Information Solutions, Inc., No. 21-2632 (7th Cir. 2023)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Chaitoff v. Experian Information Solutions, Inc., No. 21-2632 (7th Cir. Aug. 14, 2023)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
**Date:** August 14, 2023
**Judges:** Hamilton, St. Eve, and Kirsch, Circuit Judges (Kirsch writing)
**Appeal From:** N.D. Illinois, Eastern Division (No. 1:18-cv-7259, Judge Virginia M. Kendall)
**Statutes:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b), § 1681i(a)(1)(A), § 1681i(a)(4), § 1681i(c), § 1681n, § 1681o

---

## Key Holdings

1. **Omission of material information is actionable under the FCRA** — A credit report is "inaccurate" under §1681e(b) and §1681i(a) if it omits accurate information that could reasonably be expected to adversely affect a consumer's creditworthiness
2. **Factual omission vs. legal dispute** — Reporting the EXISTENCE of a forbearance/modification agreement is a factual matter within a CRA's competency, not a "legal dispute" immune from FCRA liability
3. **Reinvestigation must include cost-effective steps** — A reasonable jury could find Experian's reinvestigation unreasonable where a cost-effective step (reviewing documents submitted by consumer) would have revealed the omitted information
4. **§1681i(c) dispute notation mandatory** — When a dispute is not resolved, the CRA MUST note the dispute in subsequent reports; failure to do so is an independent FCRA violation
5. **ACDV-only reinvestigation may be unreasonable** — Merely sending an ACDV to the furnisher and accepting the response without reviewing consumer-submitted documentation can be unreasonable

---

## Facts

**Background:** Jeffrey Chaitoff purchased a home in 1995 and refinanced through Ocwen Loan Servicing in 2012. He lost his job in 2016 and fell behind on payments, becoming at least six months delinquent from October 2016 to August 2017.

**The Trial Period Plan (TPP):** In April 2017, Ocwen offered Chaitoff a Trial Period Plan — if he made three reduced payments (May, June, July 2017), his monthly payment would be permanently reduced and his account brought current. Chaitoff completed the plan successfully, and his loan was modified.

**The Reporting Problem:** Despite completing the TPP:
- Experian reported Chaitoff as DELINQUENT through May-July 2017 (the plan payments were applied to older delinquent months, so technically he was still behind during those months)
- Experian COMPLETELY OMITTED any mention of the TPP/modification agreement from the credit report
- A consumer reading the report would see only delinquency with no context — no indication that Chaitoff had entered a payment plan and was performing as agreed

**The impact:** Chaitoff was DENIED a mortgage to purchase another home based on the Experian report showing only delinquency with no modification context.

**The disputes:** Chaitoff disputed twice (May 2018 and July 2018):
- Provided detailed dispute letters explaining the TPP
- Attached Ocwen's confirmation letter showing TPP payments were made on time
- Attached Ocwen's original TPP offer letter
- Explicitly stated the omission was "misleading lenders into believing that I did not make payments"

**Experian's response:** Both times, Experian:
- Processed via ACDV (Automated Consumer Dispute Verification)
- Sent to Ocwen, which confirmed the delinquency reporting was "correct"
- Did NOT independently review Chaitoff's attached documents
- Did NOT note the dispute on subsequent reports as required by §1681i(c)

## Court's Analysis

### 1. Omissions ARE Inaccuracies Under FCRA

> "A credit report is inaccurate under § 1681e(b) and § 1681i(a) if it omits accurate information that could reasonably be expected to adversely affect a consumer's creditworthiness."

The court held that "accurate" under FCRA means more than "technically correct" — it requires completeness where omission would mislead. Reporting delinquency without reporting the modification agreement that resolved it creates a misleading impression.

### 2. This Is a Factual Omission, Not a Legal Dispute

The district court had ruled that whether the TPP affected reporting was a "legal question" beyond Experian's competency. The Seventh Circuit reversed:

> "Reporting the EXISTENCE of the agreement did not involve the application of law to facts, so was not a legal error."

Whether an agreement EXISTS is a factual question. Whether the agreement has specific legal EFFECTS on the obligation may be legal. But simply reporting "consumer entered into a modification agreement" is squarely within a CRA's factual competency.

### 3. ACDV-Only Reinvestigation Can Be Unreasonable

The court found that a jury could conclude Experian's reinvestigation was unreasonable:

> "A reasonable jury could find that there was a cost-effective step Experian could have taken that would have discovered the agreement's existence."

Chaitoff attached the TPP documentation to his dispute. Experian could have reviewed those documents (a low-cost step) and discovered the omission. Instead, it just sent an ACDV to Ocwen and accepted the response — the same "perfunctory" process condemned in *Cushman*.

### 4. §1681i(c) Dispute Notation Is Mandatory

When Chaitoff's dispute was not resolved in his favor, §1681i(c) REQUIRED Experian to note the dispute in subsequent reports. Experian failed to do this — an independent violation.

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Chaitoff

**1. Omission Cases (Most Common Application)**

Cite *Chaitoff* whenever a CRA's report is TECHNICALLY accurate but MISLEADINGLY INCOMPLETE:
- Reporting a charge-off without reporting that the consumer entered a payment plan
- Reporting delinquency without reporting a forbearance agreement
- Reporting a collection without reporting the original creditor's settlement offer
- Reporting a balance without reporting that it's disputed
- Reporting late payments without reporting the consumer was affected by a natural disaster (CARES Act)

**Pleading language:**
> "Defendant's credit report was inaccurate within the meaning of §1681e(b) because it omitted material information — specifically, [the modification agreement / settlement / forbearance / dispute status] — that could reasonably be expected to adversely affect Plaintiff's creditworthiness. Under *Chaitoff v. Experian Information Solutions, Inc.*, No. 21-2632 (7th Cir. 2023), the omission of material information renders a technically accurate report inaccurate for FCRA purposes."

**2. Reinvestigation Cases (ACDV Rubber-Stamping)**

Cite *Chaitoff* alongside *Cushman* when the CRA:
- Only sent an ACDV and accepted the furnisher's response
- Did not review documents the consumer attached to the dispute
- Had a "cost-effective step" available but didn't take it

**Pleading language:**
> "Defendant's reinvestigation was unreasonable under §1681i(a)(1)(A). Plaintiff submitted [specific documents] with [his/her] dispute demonstrating the inaccuracy. Rather than review these documents — a cost-effective step that would have revealed the error — Defendant merely transmitted an ACDV to the furnisher and accepted the response without independent review. See *Chaitoff v. Experian*, No. 21-2632 (7th Cir. 2023) (finding reinvestigation unreasonable where 'cost-effective step' would have revealed omitted information)."

**3. Dispute Notation Claims (§1681i(c))**

Cite *Chaitoff* for any case where the CRA failed to note the consumer's dispute on subsequent reports:

**Pleading language:**
> "After Plaintiff's dispute was not resolved in Plaintiff's favor, Defendant was required under 15 U.S.C. §1681i(c) to 'clearly note that [the information] is disputed by the consumer' in any subsequent consumer report. Defendant failed to include any dispute notation, in violation of §1681i(c). See *Chaitoff v. Experian*, No. 21-2632 (7th Cir. 2023) (affirming §1681i(c) claim where Experian failed to note dispute)."

**4. Factual vs. Legal Dispute Argument**

When defendants argue the dispute involves a "legal question" beyond their competency:

> "Defendant's characterization of this as a 'legal dispute' is incorrect. Under *Chaitoff*, the existence of [the agreement/modification/settlement] is a factual matter within Defendant's competency to report. Whether a given fact EXISTS is not a legal question — only the legal EFFECT of that fact could potentially be beyond a CRA's competency. Reporting that '[consumer] entered into a modification agreement' requires no legal analysis."

## Strategic Notes for the Attorney

### Strengthening Your Cases with Chaitoff

1. **Always check for OMISSIONS, not just false statements.** Most attorneys focus on "wrong balance" or "wrong status" — but *Chaitoff* opens a whole category of claims based on what's MISSING from the report. Look for:
   - Forbearance/modification agreements not reported
   - Dispute flags (XB compliance condition code) not applied
   - Consumer statements not included after request
   - Payment plans not reflected
   - Settlement agreements not noted
   - CARES Act accommodations not reported

2. **Attach all documentation to your dispute letters.** *Chaitoff* specifically noted that the consumer attached supporting documentation. This creates the factual record that Experian HAD the information and COULD have reviewed it at low cost. Without attachments, the CRA can argue it didn't have enough information to investigate further.

3. **Always add a §1681i(c) claim.** This is often overlooked but it's nearly automatic — if the dispute wasn't resolved in your client's favor and the CRA didn't note the dispute on subsequent reports, that's a freebie violation. Check subsequent reports pulled AFTER the dispute for the absence of "consumer disputes this account" notation.

4. **Frame ACDV-only reinvestigation as unreasonable.** After *Chaitoff* + *Cushman*, the argument is clear: sending an ACDV and accepting the response without reviewing consumer-submitted documents is the definition of unreasonable reinvestigation when a cost-effective alternative existed.

5. **Use "cost-effective step" language in willfulness arguments.** If reviewing the consumer's attachments would have cost Experian essentially nothing (employee already handling the file), the failure to do so while processing millions of disputes suggests a corporate policy of cutting corners — supporting willfulness under §1681n.

## Discovery Requests Informed by Chaitoff

**Interrogatories:**
1. "Did Defendant review the documents Plaintiff submitted with [his/her] dispute letter dated [date]? If not, why not?"
2. "Describe the steps Defendant took beyond transmitting an ACDV to the furnisher during its reinvestigation."
3. "What is Defendant's policy regarding review of consumer-submitted documentation during reinvestigation?"
4. "Identify any notations Defendant placed on Plaintiff's file after the dispute indicating the information was disputed by the consumer."

**RFPs:**
1. "All documents, notes, and system records reflecting Defendant's reinvestigation of Plaintiff's dispute dated [date]"
2. "Defendant's policies and procedures for reviewing consumer-submitted documentation during reinvestigation"
3. "All versions of Plaintiff's credit file generated after [dispute date], showing whether a dispute notation was included"
4. "Training materials provided to reinvestigation personnel regarding when to review consumer attachments vs. relying solely on ACDV responses"

## Key Takeaway for the AI Drafter

*Chaitoff* establishes three things the drafter must always consider:
1. **Check for omissions** — a report missing material context (modification, forbearance, settlement, dispute flag) is "inaccurate" under FCRA even if every stated fact is technically true
2. **ACDV alone isn't enough** — when the consumer provides documents, the CRA must review them; the drafter should always allege failure to review consumer-submitted documentation
3. **Always plead §1681i(c)** — it's a near-automatic claim when the dispute wasn't resolved and subsequent reports lack dispute notation
