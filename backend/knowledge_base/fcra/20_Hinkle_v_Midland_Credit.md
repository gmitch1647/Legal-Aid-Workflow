# Hinkle v. Midland Credit Management, Inc., 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Dana Hinkle, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. Midland Credit Management, Inc., Defendant-Appellee, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit
**Date:** July 7, 2016
**Docket:** No. 15-11003
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the Middle District of Alabama
**Panel:** Judges Tjoflat, Marcus, and Anderson
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duties upon notice of dispute), § 1681i(a) (reinvestigation), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance), § 1681n (willful noncompliance)

**BINDING AUTHORITY:** This is Eleventh Circuit precedent, directly binding on all district courts within the circuit, including the Northern District of Georgia.

---

## Facts

Dana Hinkle disputed a debt reported by Midland Credit Management, Inc. ("Midland"), a debt buyer and furnisher of information to consumer reporting agencies. Midland had purchased the debt from the original creditor and was reporting it on Hinkle's credit file.

**The Core Dispute:** Hinkle disputed the debt through the CRAs, asserting that the account was not hers and/or that the amount and status reported were inaccurate. Hinkle provided specific information to support her dispute — she did not merely send a generic "not mine" dispute but furnished particular facts contradicting what Midland had reported.

**Midland's Response:** Upon receiving notice of the dispute from the CRAs via the e-OSCAR system (an Automated Consumer Dispute Verification or "ACDV"), Midland conducted what it characterized as a "reinvestigation" under § 1681s-2(b). However, Midland's investigation consisted primarily of:

1. Reviewing its own internal electronic records — the same records that generated the original reporting
2. Confirming that the information in its system matched what it had previously reported to the CRAs
3. Verifying the debt back to the CRA without conducting any additional analysis or review of underlying account documentation from the original creditor
4. Failing to obtain or review the original creditor's account records, signed agreements, payment histories, or other documentation that would have been necessary to resolve the dispute

**What Midland Did NOT Do:**
- Did not obtain original account documents from the entity that sold it the debt
- Did not review underlying contracts, applications, or payment records
- Did not conduct any investigation beyond checking its own purchased data
- Did not meaningfully engage with the specific information Hinkle provided in her dispute
- Did not contact the original creditor to obtain documentation that would confirm or deny Hinkle's claims

**The Pattern:** The evidence suggested that Midland's "investigation" was a routine, automated process — not a genuine effort to determine accuracy. As a debt buyer, Midland typically purchased accounts in bulk with limited documentation. Its internal records consisted primarily of the electronic data file provided at the time of purchase, which contained only summary account information (account number, balance, dates) rather than complete account histories.

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (M.D. Ala.):** Granted summary judgment to Midland Credit Management on the § 1681s-2(b) claim, finding that Midland's investigation was reasonable as a matter of law
2. **Eleventh Circuit:** REVERSED in part, holding that the reasonableness of Midland's investigation under § 1681s-2(b) was a genuine issue of material fact precluding summary judgment

## Holdings

### Holding 1: Furnisher's Investigation Must Be More Than Rubber-Stamping

> "A furnisher's investigation is not reasonable if it merely verifies that the information it previously reported came from its own files."

The Eleventh Circuit held that a furnisher cannot satisfy its § 1681s-2(b) duty by simply checking its own records and confirming that the data matches what was already reported. This circular process — reporting information, then "investigating" a dispute by confirming the same information exists in the same system — is not a meaningful investigation. The investigation must be substantive and responsive to the specific dispute raised by the consumer.

### Holding 2: Furnisher Cannot Simply Re-Verify What It Already Reported

> "Section 1681s-2(b) requires more than merely rubber-stamping a prior report. The furnisher must actually investigate the basis for the reported information."

The court rejected Midland's argument that looking at its internal records and confirming they matched its prior reporting constituted a "reasonable investigation." The statute requires investigation — not verification of internal consistency. The relevant question is whether the reported information is accurate, not whether the furnisher's computer system still contains the same data it contained when the information was first reported.

### Holding 3: Specificity of Consumer Dispute Triggers Enhanced Duty

When a consumer provides specific information contradicting the furnisher's reporting — rather than a bare denial — the furnisher's duty to investigate is heightened. The furnisher must actually engage with the substance of the dispute and investigate accordingly.

> "Where a consumer has provided particular information calling into question the accuracy of the furnisher's reporting, the furnisher must do more than confirm the information in its own database."

### Holding 4: Reasonableness of Investigation Is a Jury Question

The court held that whether a furnisher's investigation satisfies § 1681s-2(b) is ordinarily a question of fact for the jury, not a question of law for the court:

> "Whether a furnisher's investigation is reasonable under § 1681s-2(b) is a factual question that should generally be reserved for the jury."

This holding makes it extremely difficult for furnishers to obtain summary judgment on § 1681s-2(b) claims in the Eleventh Circuit — and therefore in the Northern District of Georgia.

### Holding 5: Debt Buyer's Limited Records Do Not Excuse Investigation Duty

The court rejected the implicit argument that because Midland was a debt buyer with limited original documentation, it could not be expected to conduct a meaningful investigation. A furnisher that lacks sufficient documentation to verify the accuracy of what it reports cannot simply report the information and then rubber-stamp it upon dispute. The furnisher's inability to verify should lead to correction or deletion — not continued reporting of unverified information.

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### The Statutory Framework

Section 1681s-2(b) imposes specific duties on furnishers upon receiving notice that a consumer has disputed information. The furnisher must:

1. **Conduct an investigation** with respect to the disputed information (§ 1681s-2(b)(1)(A))
2. **Review all relevant information** provided by the CRA (§ 1681s-2(b)(1)(B))
3. **Report the results** to the CRA (§ 1681s-2(b)(1)(C))
4. **Modify, delete, or permanently block** reporting of information found to be inaccurate or incomplete (§ 1681s-2(b)(1)(D)-(E))

### The Court's Analysis

The Eleventh Circuit analyzed the text of § 1681s-2(b) and concluded that Congress used the word "investigation" deliberately — not "verification" or "review." An investigation requires an active, substantive inquiry into the accuracy of the disputed information, not a passive confirmation that the same data remains in the same system.

The court drew on the broader FCRA framework, noting that the Act's purpose is to ensure accuracy of consumer credit information. That purpose is defeated if furnishers can satisfy their investigation duty through circular self-confirmation. If a furnisher reports inaccurate data, and then upon dispute simply re-checks its own (inaccurate) records, the error will never be caught — the statutory scheme would be rendered meaningless.

### The Debt Buyer Problem

The court's reasoning implicitly addressed a systemic problem with debt buyers as furnishers. Debt buyers like Midland typically:

- Purchase accounts in bulk with only electronic summary data
- Do not receive original signed contracts, applications, or correspondence
- Have no independent knowledge of the account history
- Report based solely on the data file received at purchase

When such an entity receives a dispute, checking its "internal records" means checking the purchased data file — which is the very source of the potentially inaccurate information. The court recognized that this process provides zero additional assurance of accuracy.

### Alignment with Other Circuits

The Eleventh Circuit's holding in *Hinkle* aligns with the consensus view across the circuits:

- *Johnson v. MBNA America Bank*, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004) — furnisher must review underlying documentation
- *Gorman v. Wolpoff & Abramson*, 584 F.3d 1147 (9th Cir. 2009) — rubber-stamping own records is insufficient
- *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008) — mere verification is not investigation

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Hinkle

**THIS IS YOUR PRIMARY AUTHORITY FOR FURNISHER CLAIMS IN NDGA.** Because *Hinkle* is binding Eleventh Circuit precedent, it is the first case you cite in any § 1681s-2(b) claim filed in the Northern District of Georgia. Cite it when:

1. **A furnisher conducted only an internal records check upon dispute** — If the furnisher received an ACDV, checked its own database, confirmed the data matched its prior reporting, and verified back to the CRA — that is the exact conduct condemned in *Hinkle*.

2. **A debt buyer or servicer lacks original documentation** — When the furnisher is a debt buyer (Midland, Portfolio Recovery, LVNV Funding, Encore Capital) or a loan servicer that acquired the account from a prior servicer, and the furnisher's "investigation" consists of reviewing the limited electronic records it received at acquisition.

3. **The consumer provided specific dispute information** — When your client's dispute letter contained particular facts (e.g., "this account was paid," "this is identity theft," "the balance is wrong — here is proof of payment"), cite *Hinkle*'s heightened duty holding.

4. **Opposing summary judgment** — *Hinkle* establishes that the reasonableness of a furnisher's investigation is a jury question in the Eleventh Circuit. Use this to defeat MSJ in NDGA.

5. **Establishing willfulness** — If the furnisher has a pattern/policy of conducting only automated, cursory investigations upon receiving ACDVs, cite *Hinkle* (decided 2016) to establish that the law has been clear for nearly a decade — continued rubber-stamping after *Hinkle* constitutes willful noncompliance.

### Ready-Made Pleading Language

**For § 1681s-2(b) Counts in NDGA Complaints:**

> "Upon receiving notice of Plaintiff's dispute from [CRA name], Defendant [Furnisher] was obligated under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b)(1)(A) to conduct a reasonable investigation with respect to the disputed information. Instead, Defendant merely reviewed its own internal records — the same records that generated the inaccurate reporting in the first instance — and re-verified the disputed information without conducting any genuine investigation. This rubber-stamp verification is precisely the type of inadequate investigation the Eleventh Circuit condemned in *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Management, Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016). As the court held, 'a furnisher's investigation is not reasonable if it merely verifies that the information it previously reported came from its own files.' Defendant's failure to conduct a meaningful investigation constitutes a violation of § 1681s-2(b)."

**For Willfulness Allegations:**

> "Defendant's violation was willful within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681n. Since at least 2016, binding Eleventh Circuit authority has established that a furnisher may not simply rubber-stamp its own prior reporting when a consumer disputes information. *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016). Despite this clear legal standard, Defendant maintains and continues to employ a policy and practice of conducting only cursory, automated review of its internal electronic records upon receiving consumer disputes — the exact conduct *Hinkle* held to be unreasonable. Defendant's continued adherence to this policy in the face of clear Eleventh Circuit precedent demonstrates either knowing or reckless disregard of Plaintiff's rights under the FCRA."

**For Opposition to MSJ:**

> "Whether Defendant's investigation satisfied § 1681s-2(b) is a question of fact for the jury. *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295, [1300-01] (11th Cir. 2016) ('Whether a furnisher's investigation is reasonable under § 1681s-2(b) is a factual question that should generally be reserved for the jury.'). The evidence shows that upon receiving notice of Plaintiff's dispute, Defendant [describe limited investigation]. Viewing this evidence in the light most favorable to Plaintiff, a reasonable jury could find that Defendant's investigation was unreasonable. Summary judgment must be denied."

**For Debt Buyer Defendants Specifically:**

> "As a debt buyer, Defendant purchased Plaintiff's alleged account in bulk with only limited electronic data — without obtaining original account documentation, signed agreements, or complete payment histories. When Plaintiff disputed the accuracy of the reported information, Defendant's so-called 'investigation' consisted of reviewing this same purchased electronic data file — the very data that generated the inaccurate reporting. Under *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016), this circular self-confirmation is not a reasonable investigation. A furnisher that lacks documentation sufficient to verify accuracy upon dispute cannot simply continue reporting unverified information."

### Discovery Requests Supported by Hinkle

**Interrogatories:**

1. "Describe in detail every step taken by Defendant in its investigation of Plaintiff's dispute, including but not limited to: (a) the identity of each person who participated in the investigation; (b) each document or record reviewed; (c) each source of information consulted; (d) the total time spent investigating; and (e) whether any documentation was obtained from the original creditor or any source other than Defendant's own internal electronic records."

2. "Identify whether Defendant obtained or reviewed any original account documentation — including but not limited to signed credit applications, account agreements, billing statements, payment records, or correspondence — from the original creditor at any point during its investigation of Plaintiff's dispute."

3. "Describe Defendant's policies and procedures for investigating consumer disputes received via the e-OSCAR system, including but not limited to: (a) the average time spent per investigation; (b) the metrics or quotas imposed on investigation personnel; (c) whether investigators are required or permitted to obtain documentation from original creditors; and (d) the training provided to investigation personnel."

4. "State whether Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute consisted, in whole or in part, of reviewing the electronic data file received at the time Defendant purchased the subject account. If so, identify what other steps, if any, were taken beyond reviewing that purchased data."

5. "Identify the total number of consumer disputes Defendant received via e-OSCAR in the twelve months preceding and following Plaintiff's dispute, and state what percentage of those disputes resulted in modification or deletion of reported information."

**Requests for Production:**

1. "All documents comprising, reflecting, or relating to Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute(s), including but not limited to: ACDV forms received and responses sent, internal investigation notes, records reviewed, communications with original creditor, and supervisor review or quality assurance records."

2. "The complete data file or media received by Defendant at the time of purchase of Plaintiff's account from the original creditor or prior owner."

3. "All original account documentation in Defendant's possession relating to Plaintiff's account, including signed agreements, applications, billing statements, and payment records."

4. "Defendant's written policies and procedures for investigating consumer disputes under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b), including training materials, guidelines, and instructions provided to investigation personnel."

5. "Documents sufficient to show the average time spent on consumer dispute investigations, including any metrics, targets, or quotas applied to investigation personnel."

**Requests for Admission:**

1. "Admit that upon receiving notice of Plaintiff's dispute, Defendant did not obtain or review any original account documentation from the original creditor."

2. "Admit that Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute consisted solely of reviewing Defendant's own internal electronic records."

3. "Admit that the internal records Defendant reviewed during its investigation of Plaintiff's dispute were the same records that generated the original reporting to the consumer reporting agencies."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Hinkle Is Your Furnisher Cushman:** Just as *Cushman v. Trans Union* is the foundational CRA reinvestigation case, *Hinkle* is the foundational furnisher investigation case in the Eleventh Circuit. Every furnisher claim in NDGA should cite *Hinkle* prominently.

2. **Pair With CRA Claims:** In NDGA, always bring § 1681s-2(b) claims against the furnisher ALONGSIDE § 1681i(a) claims against the CRAs. *Hinkle* condemns the furnisher's rubber-stamping; *Cushman* and its progeny condemn the CRA's acceptance of that rubber-stamp. Together, they show a systemic failure at both levels.

3. **Debt Buyer Cases Are Strongest:** *Hinkle* involved a debt buyer with limited documentation. If your defendant is Midland, Portfolio Recovery, LVNV, or another debt buyer, you are directly within the factual ambit of the case. Debt buyers almost never have original documentation — making their "investigations" inherently circular.

4. **Use in Early Case Assessment:** When evaluating a potential FCRA furnisher case, ask: "Upon receiving the dispute, did the furnisher do anything beyond checking its own database?" If the answer is no, *Hinkle* gives you a viable § 1681s-2(b) claim in NDGA.

5. **NDGA Judges Are Familiar With This Case:** Because *Hinkle* is binding circuit authority, NDGA judges will be well-acquainted with it. You do not need to spend extensive briefing space explaining the holding — a short citation is often sufficient. Focus your argument on applying the facts.

6. **30(b)(6) Deposition Focus:** Depose the furnisher's 30(b)(6) witness on the exact steps taken during the investigation. The *Hinkle* framework tells you exactly what to ask: Did you go beyond your internal database? Did you obtain original documentation? Did you address the specific issues raised in the dispute? When the witness admits "no" to each, you have your case.
