# Johnson v. MBNA America Bank, NA, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Wanda Johnson, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. MBNA America Bank, NA, Defendant-Appellee, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit
**Date:** January 23, 2004
**Docket:** No. 02-2263
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia
**Panel:** Judges Widener, Michael, and Shedd
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duties upon notice of dispute), § 1681s-2(a) (duty to furnish accurate information), § 1681i(a) (CRA reinvestigation), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance), § 1681n (willful noncompliance)

**PERSUASIVE AUTHORITY:** Fourth Circuit precedent. This is the earliest and most foundational circuit court case establishing furnisher investigation duties under § 1681s-2(b). Not binding in the Eleventh Circuit but universally cited in all circuits, including by the Eleventh Circuit in developing its own standards. This is the "grandfather" case for furnisher investigation claims.

---

## Facts

Wanda Johnson held a credit card account with MBNA America Bank, NA ("MBNA"). A dispute arose regarding the accuracy of MBNA's reporting to the consumer reporting agencies about Johnson's account.

**The Account Dispute:** Johnson contended that MBNA was inaccurately reporting the status of her account — specifically, that the account was being reported as delinquent or charged off when Johnson believed the account had been settled, paid, or was otherwise inaccurately characterized. Johnson raised specific factual issues: she asserted that she had made payments, that the balance was incorrect, and/or that the account status did not reflect the actual state of affairs.

**Johnson's Dispute Process:** Johnson disputed the inaccurate information through the consumer reporting agencies. The CRAs, upon receiving her dispute, forwarded notice to MBNA via the automated dispute verification system (the precursor to modern e-OSCAR). This notice triggered MBNA's duties under § 1681s-2(b).

**MBNA's Response:** Upon receiving the dispute notification from the CRA, MBNA:

1. Reviewed its internal computer records showing account status, balance, and payment history as reflected in its electronic system
2. Confirmed that the electronic system showed the same information previously reported to the CRAs
3. Verified the disputed information back to the CRA without change
4. Did NOT obtain or review the underlying account documents — original credit card applications, account agreements, billing statements, payment processing records, correspondence, or notes from account representatives

**The Critical Failing:** MBNA had access to complete account documentation — signed applications, monthly statements, payment records, correspondence, and internal notes. These records would have provided the factual basis to evaluate Johnson's specific claims. Instead of reviewing this documentation, MBNA simply confirmed that its electronic reporting system showed the same data it had previously reported. The court found this to be an insufficient investigation.

**The Pattern:** Evidence indicated that MBNA's dispute handling process was highly automated and volume-driven. Dispute handlers processed large numbers of ACDVs with limited time per dispute. The standard process was to pull up the electronic account screen, confirm the data matched the prior report, and verify. There was no standard procedure for pulling original documentation or conducting individualized analysis of the specific issues raised by the consumer.

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (E.D. Va.):** Granted summary judgment to MBNA on all FCRA claims, concluding that MBNA had fulfilled its investigation obligation as a matter of law
2. **Fourth Circuit:** REVERSED and REMANDED — holding that MBNA's investigation was not reasonable where it consisted only of confirming its own electronic records without reviewing underlying account documentation

## Holdings

### Holding 1: Furnisher Has Duty to Review Underlying Account Documents

> "A creditor's cursory review of its own records to confirm the disputed information does not constitute an adequate investigation under § 1681s-2(b). The investigation must include meaningful consideration of the consumer's dispute by examining relevant account documents."

The Fourth Circuit established the foundational principle that a furnisher's investigation under § 1681s-2(b) must include review of the underlying account documentation — not merely the electronic summary used for CRA reporting. This was the first circuit court to squarely hold that reviewing one's own electronic records is insufficient.

### Holding 2: Cannot Simply Confirm What Was Already Reported

> "The purpose of § 1681s-2(b) is to provide a mechanism for the correction of inaccurate credit information. A furnisher that merely confirms its prior report without examining the basis for that report defeats this purpose."

The court held that a furnisher cannot satisfy § 1681s-2(b) by confirming that its current records match its prior reporting. This circular process — reporting data, then "investigating" by confirming the same data still exists — provides no opportunity for error correction. Congress intended § 1681s-2(b) to be a corrective mechanism, not a confirmation mechanism.

### Holding 3: The Nature of "Investigation" Requires Substantive Inquiry

> "An 'investigation' that warrants that name requires some degree of careful inquiry. It is not sufficient to merely 'rubber stamp' prior reporting by rechecking a computer screen."

The court interpreted the statutory term "investigation" to require substantive, meaningful inquiry into the accuracy of the disputed information. This requires affirmative steps beyond what the furnisher does in its ordinary course of business (routine data maintenance and reporting).

### Holding 4: Private Right of Action Encompasses Investigation Quality

The court confirmed that § 1681s-2(b) creates a private right of action that encompasses not only whether the furnisher investigated at all, but whether the investigation conducted was adequate:

> "The statute imposes a duty to conduct an investigation — not merely to go through the motions. A consumer may challenge both the existence and the adequacy of the furnisher's investigation."

### Holding 5: Reasonableness Depends on Circumstances Including Available Resources

The court held that what constitutes a "reasonable" investigation depends on the circumstances, including:
- The resources available to the furnisher
- The nature and specificity of the consumer's dispute
- The type of documentation readily accessible to the furnisher
- The potential consequences to the consumer of continued inaccurate reporting

> "What is reasonable must be determined in context. A large bank with complete account records at its disposal faces a different standard than an entity with limited information. But all furnishers must do more than simply confirm their prior reports."

### Holding 6: Automated Processing Does Not Excuse Inadequate Investigation

The court rejected the argument that the volume of disputes or the automated nature of the ACDV system excuses a cursory investigation:

> "The efficiency of the system does not relieve the furnisher of its statutory obligation. Section 1681s-2(b) requires an investigation — and the use of automated processes does not transform a non-investigation into an investigation."

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### Historical Significance

*Johnson v. MBNA* was decided in 2004 and is the earliest circuit court decision to squarely address the adequacy of a furnisher's investigation under § 1681s-2(b). Before *Johnson*, there was limited appellate authority on what § 1681s-2(b) actually required. Lower courts often granted summary judgment to furnishers based on the mere fact that some investigation occurred. *Johnson* changed this by establishing that the quality — not merely the existence — of the investigation matters.

### The Statutory Purpose Analysis

The Fourth Circuit grounded its holding in the purpose of the FCRA's dispute mechanism:

1. **The Problem Congress Identified:** Inaccurate credit reporting harms consumers
2. **The Solution Congress Created:** A multi-step dispute process culminating in furnisher investigation
3. **The Logic:** If furnishers simply confirm their own records, errors are never corrected
4. **The Conclusion:** Congress must have intended "investigation" to mean something more than "confirmation"

The court reasoned that interpreting § 1681s-2(b) to require only confirmation of prior reporting would render the statute "superfluous" — Congress would not have created a statutory investigation requirement if mere self-confirmation were sufficient.

### The Documentary Analysis

The court emphasized the importance of document review:

- Electronic account screens reflect SUMMARIZED data — they do not show the complete picture
- Original documents (applications, statements, correspondence) often contain information not captured in electronic summaries
- Errors in electronic systems can be identified only by reference to source documents
- When a consumer raises a specific factual issue ("I made this payment," "I never opened this account," "this balance is wrong"), resolution requires examination of the relevant documentation

### The Institutional Capacity Argument

MBNA was a major national bank with:
- Complete account files maintained for each cardholder
- Original signed applications
- Full payment processing records
- Archived correspondence
- Internal notes from account representatives

The court noted that MBNA had the institutional capacity and documentary resources to conduct a meaningful investigation. Its failure to use these resources — despite their availability — rendered its investigation unreasonable.

### The Consumer Protection Framework

The court situated § 1681s-2(b) within the broader consumer protection framework of the FCRA:

- Consumers have limited ability to prove their claims without access to the furnisher's records
- The dispute mechanism is designed to address this asymmetry by requiring furnishers to investigate
- If furnishers need only confirm their own records, consumers have no effective remedy for furnisher errors
- The investigation duty exists precisely to address situations where the furnisher's records may be wrong

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Johnson

*Johnson* is the foundational case for all furnisher § 1681s-2(b) claims. Cite it in every furnisher case, alongside *Hinkle* (binding 11th Circuit authority). Its historical precedence makes it particularly useful for willfulness arguments — the law has been clear since 2004.

1. **In every § 1681s-2(b) claim** — *Johnson* is the ur-text of furnisher investigation claims. Cite it for historical weight and foundational authority.

2. **When the furnisher is a bank or original creditor with full records** — *Johnson* involved MBNA, a national bank with complete account documentation. When your defendant is a bank, credit card issuer, or original creditor, the factual parallel is exact.

3. **When building a willfulness case** — The law has been clear since *Johnson* was decided in 2004. Any furnisher conducting only cursory investigations after 2004 does so in defiance of over two decades of established law. This is powerful for willfulness under *Safeco*.

4. **When the furnisher's defense is "we checked our records"** — *Johnson* is the first case to reject this defense. Its foundational status gives it rhetorical power: "Courts have rejected this exact argument for over twenty years."

5. **When establishing the multi-circuit consensus** — *Johnson* (4th Cir. 2004) was followed by *Saunders* (4th Cir. 2008), *Gorman* (9th Cir. 2009), and *Hinkle* (11th Cir. 2016). Citing the progression shows the law's development and the depth of consensus.

6. **Against credit card issuers and banks specifically** — Because *Johnson* involved a credit card issuer (MBNA), it is particularly apt against similar defendants: Chase, Capital One, Synchrony, Discover, Citi, and their successors.

**For § 1681s-2(b) Counts (Foundational Citation):**

> "For over two decades, federal courts have uniformly held that a furnisher may not satisfy its investigation duty under § 1681s-2(b) by merely confirming the information in its own electronic records. The seminal case establishing this principle is *Johnson v. MBNA America Bank, NA*, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004), which held that '[a] creditor's cursory review of its own records to confirm the disputed information does not constitute an adequate investigation under § 1681s-2(b).' *Id.* at 431. This principle is now the law of every circuit to address the question. *See Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016); *Gorman v. Here, Defendant's investigation consisted of precisely the same type of cursory, self-confirming review condemned in *Johnson* and its progeny."

**For Willfulness Allegations (Time-Based Argument):**

> "Defendant's violation was willful within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681n. Since 2004, federal appellate courts have held that a furnisher may not satisfy § 1681s-2(b) by simply confirming its own prior reporting. *Johnson v. MBNA America Bank, NA*, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004). This principle has been reaffirmed and extended by every circuit to consider the question. *See Saunders v. **For Opposition to Argument That Investigation Was Reasonable:**

> "Defendant's argument that its investigation was reasonable because it 'reviewed its records' has been rejected by every circuit to consider it. *Johnson v. MBNA America Bank, NA*, 357 F.3d 426, 431 (4th Cir. 2004) (reviewing electronic account records insufficient); *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142, 150 (4th Cir. 2008) ('mere verification' is not investigation); *Gorman v. Wolpoff & Abramson, LLP*, 584 F.3d 1147, 1157 (9th Cir. 2009) (cannot 'rubber-stamp' own records); *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016) (same). 'The purpose of § 1681s-2(b) is to provide a mechanism for the correction of inaccurate credit information. A furnisher that merely confirms its prior report without examining the basis for that report defeats this purpose.' *Johnson*, 357 F.3d at 431."

**For Institutional Capacity Arguments:**

> "As a [large national bank / major credit card issuer / national mortgage servicer], Defendant maintains comprehensive account records including original [applications/contracts], [monthly statements], [payment processing records], and [internal correspondence]. Defendant was uniquely positioned to resolve Plaintiff's dispute by reviewing these records — records that would have revealed the inaccuracy. *See Johnson v. MBNA America Bank, NA*, 357 F.3d 426 (4th Cir. 2004) (bank with full account documentation must review underlying records, not merely electronic summary). Defendant's failure to access and review the documentation at its disposal — despite having the institutional capacity to do so — renders its investigation unreasonable as a matter of law."

### Discovery Requests Supported by Johnson

**Interrogatories:**

1. "Describe Defendant's standard procedure for investigating consumer disputes received via the e-OSCAR system, including each step in the process from receipt of the ACDV to transmission of the response."

2. "Identify whether Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute included review of any of the following: (a) original account application or agreement; (b) monthly billing statements; (c) payment processing records; (d) inbound or outbound correspondence; (e) telephone call recordings or notes; (f) internal account memoranda or notes. For each item not reviewed, explain why."

3. "State the average time Defendant's dispute investigation personnel spend per consumer dispute, and identify any quotas, metrics, or performance targets that apply to those personnel."

4. "Describe what training Defendant provides to dispute investigation personnel regarding the requirement to review underlying account documentation during investigations."

5. "Identify the documents Defendant maintains in its account file for Plaintiff's account that were NOT reviewed during the investigation of Plaintiff's dispute."

**Requests for Production:**

1. "Defendant's complete account file for Plaintiff, including but not limited to: original application, account agreement, all monthly statements, all payment records, all correspondence (inbound and outbound), all telephone notes, all internal memoranda, and all records of account status changes."

2. "All documents reviewed during Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute(s), including screen prints or reproductions of any electronic records viewed."

3. "All documents NOT reviewed during Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute(s) that relate to the subject matter of the dispute."

4. "Defendant's written policies and procedures for investigating consumer disputes under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b), including all versions effective during the relevant time period, training materials, and quality assurance guidelines."

5. "Documents sufficient to show: (a) the total number of ACDV dispute notices Defendant received during the twelve months surrounding Plaintiff's dispute; (b) the average handling time per dispute; (c) the percentage of disputes resulting in verification vs. modification/deletion; and (d) the percentage of disputes in which underlying account documentation was reviewed."

**Requests for Admission:**

1. "Admit that Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute did not include review of the original [account application / credit agreement / promissory note]."

2. "Admit that Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute consisted primarily of reviewing electronic account summary data displayed on Defendant's internal computer system."

3. "Admit that Defendant maintained, at the time of Plaintiff's dispute, account documentation that was not reviewed during the investigation, including [monthly statements / payment records / correspondence / internal notes]."

4. "Admit that review of [specific document] during the investigation of Plaintiff's dispute would have revealed that the information reported to the CRAs was inaccurate."

5. "Admit that Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute took less than [X minutes / hours]."

**30(b)(6) Deposition Topics:**

1. "Defendant's policies and procedures for investigating consumer disputes, including the standard steps taken from receipt of an ACDV to response."
2. "The specific steps taken in investigating Plaintiff's dispute(s)."
3. "The types of account documentation Defendant maintains and whether such documentation is reviewed during dispute investigations."
4. "Metrics, targets, and performance standards applied to dispute investigation personnel."
5. "Training provided to dispute investigation personnel, including training on the requirement to review underlying account documentation."
6. "The technology and systems used in dispute investigations, including the e-OSCAR platform and internal account management systems."
7. "Quality assurance and supervisory review processes for dispute investigations."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Johnson Is the Foundation — Cite It First in the Progression:** When building your multi-circuit consensus argument, start with *Johnson* (2004) and work forward: *Johnson* → *Saunders* → *Gorman* → *Hinkle*. This progression shows that the law has been clear and developing for over two decades, which devastates any willfulness defense.

2. **The Twenty-Year Argument:** *Johnson* was decided in 2004. Any furnisher still conducting rubber-stamp investigations in 2025 or 2026 has been on notice for over twenty years. This time-based argument is uniquely powerful for willfulness: "Defendant has had twenty years to conform its practices to the law. Its continued failure to do so cannot be attributed to good-faith uncertainty."

3. **Pair Johnson With Hinkle for Maximum Effect:** In NDGA, lead with *Hinkle* (binding) and support with *Johnson* (foundational). The combination provides both controlling authority and historical depth. The judge sees: "This is the law in this circuit (*Hinkle*), and it has been the law nationally for twenty years (*Johnson*)."

4. **Against Major Banks:** *Johnson* involved MBNA (now part of Bank of America). If your defendant is a major national bank — Chase, Capital One, Citi, Synchrony, Discover — cite *Johnson* for the proposition that banks with full institutional documentation cannot claim their cursory investigations are reasonable.

5. **The "Purpose" Argument at Trial:** *Johnson*'s reasoning about statutory purpose is compelling to juries: "The whole point of the dispute system is to catch and fix errors. If the company just re-checks its own records and says 'yep, same data,' errors never get fixed. That defeats the entire purpose Congress intended." This is *Johnson*'s holding translated into jury language.

6. **Discovery Sequence Strategy:** Use *Johnson*-based discovery requests to build your record:
   - First: RFAs establishing what was NOT reviewed (lock in the facts)
   - Second: Interrogatories detailing what WAS done (expose the cursory nature)
   - Third: 30(b)(6) deposition on policies and procedures (show the systemic failure)
   - Fourth: RFPs for the underlying documents that should have been reviewed (show what review would have revealed)

7. **Amicus-Level Authority:** *Johnson* is cited so frequently that it has achieved near-universal acceptance. In briefing, you can characterize it as representing "the unanimous view of every circuit to address the question" — which it effectively does, since no circuit has disagreed with its core holding.
