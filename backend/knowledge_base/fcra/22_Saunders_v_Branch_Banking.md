# Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co., 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Sandra Saunders, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. Branch Banking & Trust Company of Virginia, Defendant-Appellee, 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit
**Date:** May 7, 2008
**Docket:** No. 07-1433
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the Western District of Virginia
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duty to investigate upon notice of dispute), § 1681i(a) (CRA reinvestigation), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance), § 1681n (willful noncompliance)

**PERSUASIVE AUTHORITY:** Fourth Circuit precedent. Not binding in the Northern District of Georgia but frequently cited alongside binding Eleventh Circuit authority (*Hinkle*) for the proposition that furnishers must go beyond mere verification.

---

## Facts

Sandra Saunders held a consumer account with Branch Banking & Trust Company of Virginia ("BB&T"). A dispute arose regarding the accuracy of information BB&T was furnishing to the consumer reporting agencies about Saunders's account.

**The Dispute:** Saunders contended that BB&T was inaccurately reporting the status of her account. She disputed the information through the CRAs, providing specific information supporting her position that BB&T's reporting was incorrect. The dispute was not a generic "not mine" denial — Saunders provided particular facts and documentation challenging the accuracy of what BB&T had reported.

**BB&T's Investigation:** Upon receiving notice of Saunders's dispute from the CRA (via the automated dispute verification system), BB&T conducted its investigation. The critical failing was that BB&T's investigation amounted to what the court termed "mere verification" — BB&T essentially checked its own computer system, confirmed that the information it had previously reported matched its internal records, and verified the same information back to the CRA.

**What BB&T Failed to Do:**
- Did not review underlying account documents that would have resolved the dispute
- Did not examine original account records, correspondence, or transaction histories in light of the specific issues Saunders raised
- Did not conduct any investigation proportionate to the specific factual allegations in Saunders's dispute
- Did not go beyond its electronic account summary to examine source documents
- Treated the dispute as a routine verification exercise rather than a genuine investigation into potential error

**The Result:** Because BB&T simply confirmed its own prior reporting without actually investigating the substance of Saunders's dispute, the inaccuracy persisted on her credit report, causing ongoing harm.

---

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (W.D. Va.):** Granted summary judgment to BB&T on the § 1681s-2(b) claim, finding BB&T's investigation was reasonable as a matter of law
2. **Fourth Circuit:** REVERSED and REMANDED — holding that BB&T's investigation was not reasonable where it amounted to mere verification without meaningful review of underlying account records

---

## Holdings

### Holding 1: "Mere Verification" Does Not Satisfy § 1681s-2(b)

> "A furnisher does not fulfill its statutory duty to conduct a reasonable investigation simply by verifying that it previously reported the same information. The statute requires investigation, not verification."

The Fourth Circuit drew a sharp distinction between "verification" and "investigation." Verification asks: "Does this match what we reported?" Investigation asks: "Is what we reported accurate?" Only the latter satisfies § 1681s-2(b).

### Holding 2: Furnisher Must Access and Review Underlying Records

> "A reasonable investigation requires the furnisher to go beyond the information sufficiently reported to the CRA and to examine the underlying records relating to the account in dispute."

The court held that a furnisher with access to original account documentation — contracts, payment records, correspondence, transaction histories — must actually review those records when investigating a consumer's dispute. It is not sufficient to check only the electronic summary or the data previously transmitted to the CRAs.

### Holding 3: Investigation Must Be Proportionate to the Dispute

The scope of the required investigation depends on the nature and specificity of the consumer's dispute. When the consumer raises specific factual issues (e.g., "I made this payment" or "this balance is wrong"), the furnisher must investigate those specific issues — which necessarily requires reviewing the underlying records relevant to those issues.

> "The reasonableness of a furnisher's investigation depends on the nature of the specific complaint. A cursory review of its own files may suffice for a bare denial, but when a consumer provides specific information, the furnisher must conduct a more thorough review."

### Holding 4: Congress Intended "Investigation" to Mean More Than Pro Forma Review

The court examined the statutory language and legislative history of § 1681s-2(b):

> "Congress's use of the term 'investigation' in § 1681s-2(b) signals that more is required than a pro forma review — the furnisher must actually look into the dispute."

The court reasoned that if Congress had intended only verification, it would have used that term (as it does elsewhere in the FCRA with respect to CRA duties). The choice of "investigation" signals a more rigorous, substantive inquiry.

### Holding 5: Furnisher's Access to Records Distinguishes It from CRA

The court noted that furnishers are uniquely positioned to resolve disputes because they have (or should have) direct access to underlying account records — unlike CRAs, which are dependent on information furnished to them. This access creates a correspondingly greater responsibility:

> "A furnisher is in a far better position to make a thorough investigation of a dispute than a CRA, which must rely on information provided by others. This greater capacity supports a requirement that the furnisher actually utilize its access to underlying records."

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### The Textual Argument

The court began with the statutory text. Section 1681s-2(b)(1)(A) requires the furnisher to "conduct an investigation with respect to the disputed information." The court parsed this language:

- **"Conduct"** — an active verb requiring affirmative steps
- **"An investigation"** — not merely a check or review, but a genuine inquiry
- **"With respect to the disputed information"** — the investigation must address the specific dispute raised, not merely confirm internal consistency

This textual analysis led to the conclusion that a furnisher who merely confirms its prior reporting without examining underlying records has not "conducted an investigation" within the meaning of the statute.

### The Structural Argument

The FCRA creates a tiered system of responsibilities:
- CRAs must follow reasonable procedures (§ 1681e(b)) and reinvestigate upon dispute (§ 1681i(a))
- Furnishers must investigate upon notice from CRAs (§ 1681s-2(b))

If furnishers could satisfy their duty through mere verification, the entire dispute mechanism would be circular: the consumer disputes → the CRA sends an ACDV to the furnisher → the furnisher confirms → the CRA maintains the same information. Nothing would ever change. The statute would be a dead letter.

The court recognized that furnishers are the "last line of defense" in the accuracy system. They possess the original records. If they do not actually investigate, no one will — the CRA certainly cannot independently verify account-level accuracy.

### The Policy Argument

The court considered the FCRA's overarching purpose: ensuring the accuracy of consumer credit information. This purpose is served only if the dispute mechanism actually works — that is, if disputes trigger genuine investigations that can identify and correct errors. A system in which furnishers simply rubber-stamp their prior reporting serves no corrective function.

### The Comparative Capacity Argument

The court emphasized that furnishers are uniquely situated to determine accuracy because they:
1. Originated the account relationship
2. Maintain original documentation (applications, contracts, statements)
3. Have complete payment and transaction histories
4. Have access to correspondence and communications with the consumer
5. Can contact internal personnel who managed the account

A CRA has none of these advantages. Therefore, the furnisher bears a commensurately greater burden to actually investigate.

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Saunders

Cite *Saunders* alongside *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Management* in every furnisher § 1681s-2(b) claim. While *Hinkle* is binding in the Eleventh Circuit, *Saunders* provides additional persuasive authority and more detailed reasoning about WHY mere verification fails. The combination is powerful.

1. **When the furnisher is a bank or original creditor with full records** — *Saunders* involved a bank (BB&T) with complete account documentation. If your defendant is a bank, credit union, or original creditor that maintained full records, *Saunders* is directly analogous. The defendant had access to everything it needed to determine accuracy — and failed to look.

2. **When the furnisher's investigation consisted of only electronic records review** — If the furnisher checked only its computer system (the electronic account summary it uses for monthly reporting) without pulling underlying documents (original applications, statements, correspondence), cite *Saunders*.

3. **When the consumer's dispute was specific and detailed** — *Saunders* emphasizes that the investigation must be proportionate to the dispute. When your client sent a detailed dispute letter with specific factual allegations, the furnisher's cursory investigation is even more unreasonable.

4. **When arguing the furnisher/CRA distinction** — *Saunders* provides the strongest articulation of why furnishers bear a GREATER investigation duty than CRAs: because they have direct access to original records. This argument is useful when furnishers try to analogize their duty to the CRA's duty.

5. **When the furnisher argues it "reviewed its records"** — Defendants often try to satisfy *Hinkle* by claiming they "reviewed their records." *Saunders* makes clear that reviewing the SAME electronic records used for reporting is not enough — the furnisher must review UNDERLYING documentation (original contracts, payment receipts, correspondence, etc.).

### Ready-Made Pleading Language

**For § 1681s-2(b) Claims in Complaint:**

> "Upon information and belief, Defendant's so-called 'investigation' of Plaintiff's dispute consisted of nothing more than 'mere verification' — checking its internal computer system to confirm that the information it had previously reported matched its electronic records. This is precisely the type of pro forma review the Fourth Circuit held insufficient in *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008). As the court explained, '[a] furnisher does not fulfill its statutory duty to conduct a reasonable investigation simply by verifying that it previously reported the same information. The statute requires investigation, not verification.' *Id.* at 150. Accord *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016)."

**For Brief in Support of Opposition to MSJ:**

> "Defendant attempts to characterize its investigation as reasonable by pointing to its review of internal records. But the very records Defendant reviewed are the same electronic data that generated the inaccurate reporting in the first instance. Reviewing the source of the error cannot identify the error. As the Fourth Circuit held in *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142, 150 (4th Cir. 2008), 'a reasonable investigation requires the furnisher to go beyond the information sufficiently reported to the CRA and to examine the underlying records relating to the account in dispute.' Defendant failed to review [identify unexamined records — original application, payment stubs, correspondence, etc.], any one of which would have revealed the inaccuracy. This is not a reasonable investigation."

**For Distinguishing "Review" from "Investigation":**

> "Defendant's claim that it 'reviewed its records' does not establish a reasonable investigation. *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008), draws a critical distinction between reviewing the electronic summary data used for CRA reporting and examining the underlying account documentation — original contracts, payment records, account correspondence, and transaction histories. Only the latter constitutes a genuine investigation. Here, Defendant reviewed only [identify what was reviewed] without examining [identify what was not reviewed]. This is mere verification, not investigation."

**For Cases Against Banks/Original Creditors:**

> "As a [bank/original creditor], Defendant maintained comprehensive account documentation including [original applications, monthly statements, payment records, correspondence, internal notes]. Defendant was uniquely positioned to resolve Plaintiff's dispute by examining these records. *See Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142, 151 (4th Cir. 2008) ('A furnisher is in a far better position to make a thorough investigation of a dispute than a CRA, which must rely on information provided by others. This greater capacity supports a requirement that the furnisher actually utilize its access to underlying records.'). Despite possessing the records necessary to determine accuracy, Defendant failed to review them — instead confirming its own electronic reporting without meaningful investigation."

### Discovery Requests Supported by Saunders

**Interrogatories:**

1. "Identify with specificity every document, record, or data source Defendant reviewed during its investigation of Plaintiff's dispute, distinguishing between (a) electronic account summary data used for CRA reporting and (b) underlying account documentation such as original contracts, payment records, statements, correspondence, and transaction histories."

2. "State whether Defendant reviewed any documents beyond its electronic account management system (i.e., the system from which data is extracted for CRA reporting) during its investigation of Plaintiff's dispute. If so, identify each such document."

3. "Describe the difference, if any, between Defendant's process for 'verifying' information reported to CRAs and its process for 'investigating' consumer disputes under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b)."

4. "Identify what original account documentation Defendant maintains for Plaintiff's account, including but not limited to: (a) the original signed application or contract; (b) monthly statements; (c) payment records; (d) correspondence; (e) internal account notes; and (f) any other source documents."

5. "For each item of original account documentation identified in the preceding interrogatory, state whether that document was reviewed during Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute. If not, explain why not."

**Requests for Production:**

1. "All original account documentation maintained by Defendant relating to Plaintiff's account, including but not limited to: original applications, signed contracts/agreements, monthly statements, payment records/receipts, correspondence (inbound and outbound), internal account notes, and records of telephone communications."

2. "All documents reviewed by Defendant during its investigation of Plaintiff's dispute(s), including screen prints of any electronic records viewed."

3. "All documents comprising Defendant's complete investigation file for Plaintiff's dispute(s), including ACDV forms, response forms, investigation notes, and supervisor review records."

4. "Defendant's policies, procedures, and training materials governing the investigation of consumer disputes under § 1681s-2(b), distinguishing between procedures for 'verification' and procedures for 'investigation' if different."

5. "Documents sufficient to show how Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute differed, if at all, from Defendant's routine monthly reporting process for Plaintiff's account."

**Requests for Admission:**

1. "Admit that during Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute, Defendant did not review the original [contract/application/agreement] for Plaintiff's account."

2. "Admit that Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute consisted primarily of reviewing the same electronic records from which Defendant's monthly CRA reporting is generated."

3. "Admit that Defendant maintains original account documentation for Plaintiff's account that was not reviewed during the investigation of Plaintiff's dispute."

4. "Admit that had Defendant reviewed [specific document], it would have revealed that the information reported to the CRAs was inaccurate."

5. "Admit that Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute and Defendant's routine monthly verification of reported data utilize the same electronic records."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Use Saunders to Amplify Hinkle:** In NDGA, *Hinkle* is binding. But *Saunders* provides more detailed reasoning about the verification/investigation distinction. Cite *Hinkle* for the binding holding and *Saunders* for the detailed explanation of WHY mere verification fails. The one-two punch is effective.

2. **The "Underlying Records" Distinction:** *Saunders* gives you a specific factual hook for deposition and discovery. Ask the 30(b)(6) witness: "What did you review?" If the answer is "our system" (the same system that generates the reporting), follow up: "Did you review underlying records — original contracts, payment stubs, correspondence?" When the answer is "no," you have your *Saunders* violation.

3. **Strongest Against Banks and Original Creditors:** While *Hinkle* is strongest against debt buyers (who lack documentation), *Saunders* is strongest against original creditors and banks (who HAVE documentation but fail to review it). Use *Saunders* against ED Financial, Navient, Nationstar/Mr. Cooper, and any bank defendant.

4. **The Proportionality Argument:** *Saunders* establishes that investigation scope must be proportionate to the dispute's specificity. Draft your client's dispute letters to be DETAILED and SPECIFIC — this triggers the heightened investigation duty. Then, when the furnisher does nothing more than a routine check, the gap between duty and performance is larger.

5. **Defeating the "We Reviewed Our Records" Defense:** After *Saunders*, defendants learned to say "we reviewed our records" instead of "we verified." But *Saunders* anticipated this — the court distinguished between reviewing electronic reporting data (mere verification wearing a disguise) and reviewing underlying account documentation (genuine investigation). Press this distinction in depositions and briefing.

6. **Multi-Circuit Consensus Argument:** *Saunders* (4th Cir.), *Hinkle* (11th Cir.), *Gorman* (9th Cir.), and *Johnson* (4th Cir.) all reach the same result. When briefing in NDGA, argue that this multi-circuit consensus confirms *Hinkle*'s correctness and forecloses any argument that the law is unclear (which matters for willfulness under *Safeco*).

## Key Takeaway for the AI Drafter

*Saunders* draws the critical line between verification and investigation. When a furnisher in the Northern District of Georgia receives a consumer dispute and responds by checking its electronic account system — the same system that generated the original reporting — it has engaged in "mere verification," not investigation. To satisfy § 1681s-2(b), the furnisher must go beyond its reporting data and examine underlying account records: original contracts, payment records, correspondence, transaction histories, and other source documents. *Saunders* provides the detailed reasoning to explain WHY this distinction matters, and pairs perfectly with *Hinkle*'s binding Eleventh Circuit holding that rubber-stamping is unreasonable.
