# FCRA Remedies, Damages, and Statute of Limitations  
**Topic:** Civil liability (willful and negligent), criminal penalties, administrative enforcement, statute of limitations, preemption, immunity, standing, class actions, defenses  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1681n, 1681o, 1681p, 1681q, 1681r, 1681s, 1681t, 1681h(e)  
**Cross-references:** All FCRA topical analysis files (08, 09, 10b); case-law file 10  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
## Introduction  
  
The FCRA's enforcement regime is layered: a robust private right of action under §§ 1681n and 1681o, a short statute of limitations under § 1681p, criminal penalties under §§ 1681q and 1681r, administrative enforcement by the FTC, CFPB, federal banking agencies, and state attorneys general under § 1681s, partial preemption of state law under § 1681t, and a narrow CRA tort immunity under § 1681h(e). This file maps each component, the *Safeco* willfulness standard, post-*TransUnion v. Ramirez* standing, and the most-litigated defenses.  
  
## § 1681n — Civil Liability for Willful Noncompliance  
  
Section 1681n(a) provides that any person who willfully fails to comply with any FCRA requirement with respect to a consumer is liable to that consumer for:  
  
- **Actual damages OR statutory damages** of not less than $100 and not more than $1,000 per violation (the consumer's election);  
- **Punitive damages** as the court may allow; and  
- In any successful action, **the costs of the action together with reasonable attorney's fees** as determined by the court.  
  
Section 1681n(b) addresses a narrower scenario: any person who obtains a consumer report under false pretenses or knowingly without a permissible purpose is liable for actual damages or **$1,000, whichever is greater**. This sub-section is litigated against, among others, attorneys and PIs who pull credit reports for litigation purposes outside § 1681b's permissible-purpose list.  
  
### The *Safeco* Willfulness Standard  
  
*Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007), is the controlling Supreme Court decision on willfulness. The Court held that "willfully" in § 1681n encompasses both knowing and *reckless* violations. Recklessness is defined objectively: a defendant's reading of the statute is reckless only if it is "objectively unreasonable" — that is, the reading shows that the defendant ran a risk of violating the law "substantially greater than the risk associated with a reading that was merely careless." Where the statutory text is ambiguous and no court of appeals or agency guidance forecloses the defendant's interpretation, the defendant has not acted in reckless disregard.  
  
The practical consequence is that *Safeco* gives defendants a strong "good-faith ambiguity" defense to willfulness — but only when the text is genuinely ambiguous *and* authoritative guidance is genuinely absent. Once a circuit court resolves the issue, the prior-ambiguity defense evaporates prospectively. *Syed v. M-I, LLC*, 853 F.3d 492 (9th Cir. 2017), illustrates the limits: the court held that including a liability waiver in a disclosure was objectively unreasonable because the statutory text ("consists solely of the disclosure") was unambiguous.  
  
## § 1681o — Civil Liability for Negligent Noncompliance  
  
Section 1681o provides that any person who is negligent in failing to comply with any FCRA requirement is liable for:  
  
- **Actual damages** sustained by the consumer as a result of the failure; and  
- In any successful action, **costs and reasonable attorney's fees**.  
  
Negligent violations support **no statutory damages and no punitive damages**. Plaintiffs must prove out-of-pocket loss, emotional distress, or other concrete harm.  
  
## What Counts as "Actual Damages"  
  
Actual damages under §§ 1681n and 1681o are broad. Categories recognized across the circuits include:  
  
- **Out-of-pocket loss** — denial of credit, higher interest rates paid, lost deposits, fees for credit-repair services, costs of certified mail and obtaining reports;  
- **Lost opportunities** — lost employment, lost housing, lost business opportunities (subject to causation proof);  
- **Time spent** — courts allow recovery of time spent disputing inaccurate information, though valuations vary;  
- **Emotional distress / mental anguish** — recognized in most circuits without need for medical evidence, though "garden-variety" emotional distress requires more than conclusory allegations. See, e.g., *Sloane v. Equifax Info. Servs., LLC*, 510 F.3d 495 (4th Cir. 2007); *Bach v. First Union Nat'l Bank*, 149 F. App'x 354 (6th Cir. 2005).  
  
Damages causation is often the most contested element. Plaintiffs must link the inaccurate reporting (or other violation) to the specific harm.  
  
## § 1681p — Jurisdiction and Statute of Limitations  
  
Section 1681p provides that an FCRA action may be brought in any appropriate United States district court without regard to amount in controversy, or in any other court of competent jurisdiction — meaning state courts have *concurrent jurisdiction*. There is no federal-question removal bar; defendants routinely remove from state to federal court.  
  
The statute of limitations is the *earlier* of:  
  
- **2 years from the date of discovery** by the plaintiff of the violation that is the basis for liability; or  
- **5 years from the date on which the violation that is the basis for liability occurs**.  
  
The discovery-rule formulation was added by FACTA in 2003. Before that, the original 1970 text imposed a flat two-year limitation from the date "liability arose," which the Supreme Court in *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19 (2001), held did not generally incorporate a discovery rule (rejecting one narrow exception). Congress legislatively superseded *TRW* in FACTA. The current statute is therefore best understood as a discovery rule (2 years from discovery) capped by a 5-year repose period.  
  
### Discovery-rule mechanics  
  
Most circuits hold that discovery occurs when the plaintiff knows or, with reasonable diligence, should have known of the violation — not merely of the underlying inaccuracy. For furnisher-reporting cases, this typically dates from the consumer's receipt of a credit report or an adverse action notice identifying the furnisher. For impermissible-pull cases, discovery may date from when the consumer learns the report was pulled (often through an AnnualCreditReport disclosure showing an inquiry).  
  
## § 1681q — Criminal Penalty: False Pretenses  
  
Section 1681q makes it a federal crime for any person to knowingly and willfully obtain information on a consumer from a CRA under false pretenses. Punishable by fine, imprisonment up to **two years**, or both. Prosecutions are rare but the provision provides leverage for civil claims; conduct meeting § 1681q's standard will typically also satisfy § 1681n(b)'s $1,000-minimum civil remedy.  
  
## § 1681r — Criminal Penalty: Unauthorized Disclosure by CRA Officers and Employees  
  
Section 1681r criminalizes the knowing and willful provision of information from a CRA's files by any officer or employee to a person not authorized to receive it. Same penalty structure as § 1681q.  
  
## § 1681s — Administrative Enforcement  
  
Section 1681s allocates administrative-enforcement authority:  
  
- **§ 1681s(a)** — Federal Trade Commission has general civil-penalty authority for "knowing violations, which constitute a pattern or practice of violations," with penalties up to the statutory cap (currently adjusted annually for inflation). The CFPB has overlapping FCRA enforcement authority over "covered persons" (consumer financial services).  
- **§ 1681s(b)** — Federal banking agencies (OCC, FDIC, Federal Reserve, NCUA) enforce the FCRA against the institutions they supervise.  
- **§ 1681s(c)** — State attorneys general may bring civil actions on behalf of state residents to enforce the FCRA, with limitations on overlap with federal actions.  
- **§ 1681s(e)** — Coordination of regulations among the agencies.  
  
The Dodd-Frank Act (2010) transferred most consumer-facing FCRA rulemaking authority from the FTC to the CFPB, which now maintains Reg V (12 C.F.R. Part 1022).  
  
## § 1681t — Preemption / Relation to State Laws  
  
Preemption under § 1681t is partial and complex. The two key provisions:  
  
- **§ 1681t(a)** — General savings clause: state laws are preempted only "to the extent that those laws are inconsistent with any provision of this subchapter, and then only to the extent of the inconsistency."  
- **§ 1681t(b)** — Express preemption of state law with respect to specified subject matters, expanded substantially by FACTA in 2003. Subject-matter areas include the duties of furnishers (§ 1681t(b)(1)(F)), the subject matter of § 1681m (adverse action notices), prescreening, identity-theft-related provisions, and several others.  
  
The CFPB issued a 2022 interpretive rule on FCRA preemption emphasizing a narrow reading: "with respect to" and "relating to" in § 1681t(b) should be read to leave states substantial room to impose additional obligations not in direct conflict. The interpretation has been controversial, particularly in the furnisher context. [VERIFY current status: the 2022 interpretive rule has been subject to challenge and successive administrations have signaled differing positions.]  
  
Notable preempted areas: most state-law tort claims based on inaccurate furnishing are preempted (e.g., state defamation claims against furnishers) under § 1681t(b)(1)(F), subject to circuit-by-circuit nuances. Conversely, state security-freeze laws were largely *superseded* (not strictly preempted) by EGRRCPA's 2018 federal security-freeze regime; state laws regulating non-FCRA aspects (data security, ID theft remedies) generally survive.  
  
## § 1681h(e) — Limited Tort Immunity for CRAs and Users  
  
Section 1681h(e) provides that no consumer may bring an action or proceeding in the nature of defamation, invasion of privacy, or negligence with respect to the reporting of information against any CRA, user, or furnisher, except as to false information furnished with **malice or willful intent to injure** the consumer. This narrow immunity does not bar claims under §§ 1681n or 1681o themselves; it bars common-law tort claims (state defamation, etc.) absent malice or willfulness. Together with § 1681t(b)(1)(F)'s preemption of state-law furnisher claims, § 1681h(e) leaves the FCRA's federal civil-liability provisions as the dominant remedy.  
  
## Standing After *TransUnion v. Ramirez*  
  
*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), held that Article III requires every plaintiff — class members included — to demonstrate a *concrete* injury. Statutory violations alone do not satisfy concreteness; the harm must bear a "close relationship to a harm traditionally recognized as providing a basis for a lawsuit in American courts." On the facts, only the 1,853 class members whose erroneous OFAC-list reports were actually disseminated to third parties had standing; the remaining 6,332 class members, whose reports were never disclosed, did not.  
  
The post-*TransUnion* landscape:  
  
- **Inaccurate-file claims under § 1681e(b)** — concrete harm requires dissemination; the mere existence of an inaccuracy in the file is not enough.  
- **Disclosure-form claims under § 1681b(b)(2)** — courts have split; many plaintiffs survive by alleging concrete confusion or informational injury, but bare procedural violations are vulnerable.  
- **Dispute-investigation claims under § 1681i / § 1681s-2(b)** — concrete harm typically requires either persistent inaccuracy with downstream consequences or wasted dispute effort.  
  
State-court actions may not require Article III standing, and plaintiffs may strategically file in state court to avoid removal-and-dismissal sequences (though removal is generally available and dismissal for lack of standing in federal court results in remand, not dismissal of the case).  
  
## Class Action Mechanics — § 1681n(a)(2)  
  
Section 1681n permits class actions. The $100–$1,000 statutory-damages range applies on a *per-violation, per-plaintiff* basis; the statute does not cap classwide statutory damages. However, courts have occasionally reduced grossly disproportionate aggregate awards on due-process grounds (the *Parker v. Time Warner* line, in the TCPA context, has been argued by analogy in FCRA cases — with mixed reception).  
  
*TransUnion v. Ramirez* requires that each class member have standing; a representative's concrete harm is not enough. This has reshaped class definitions: classes are now typically defined to include only those members who can show dissemination or other concrete consequences.  
  
## Common Defenses  
  
1. **No private right of action** — § 1681m violations after FACTA (see topical analysis file 08); § 1681s-2(a) furnisher accuracy duties (private enforcement runs only through § 1681s-2(b) following a CRA dispute).  
2. **No willfulness (*Safeco*)** — the defendant's reading of an ambiguous statute was not objectively unreasonable. Effective where neither circuit precedent nor agency guidance forecloses the position.  
3. **Lack of standing (*TransUnion*)** — particularly for inaccurate-file claims without dissemination and for procedural-disclosure claims without downstream harm.  
4. **Statute of limitations** — earlier of 2 years from discovery or 5 years from violation.  
5. **Reasonable procedures defense** — under § 1681e(b), CRAs are liable only if their procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy were unreasonable; courts emphasize feasibility and proportionality to risk.  
6. **No causation of actual damages** — particularly in negligence claims where statutory damages are unavailable.  
  
### Bona-fide error — NOT generally available  
  
Unlike the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(c), the FCRA does **not** contain a general bona-fide-error defense. Some narrow contexts (e.g., the reseller provisions, certain CRA procedures) embed reasonableness standards that operate similarly, but a defendant cannot invoke an FDCPA-style affirmative bona-fide-error defense across the FCRA. Practitioners frequently conflate the two; this is incorrect.  
  
## Practical Litigation Notes  
  
1. **For plaintiffs**: assess willfulness under *Safeco* before betting the case on statutory damages — without willfulness, recovery is limited to actual damages plus fees. Quantify emotional distress and time-spent damages carefully and corroborate where possible.  
2. **For defendants**: build a *Safeco* record — written legal advice, reliance on agency guidance, contemporaneous training materials — to support good-faith ambiguity. Audit class-definitions for *TransUnion* standing gaps as part of class-cert opposition.  
3. **Removal strategy**: defendants frequently remove FCRA cases to federal court, then move to dismiss for lack of Article III standing. The resulting remand can leave plaintiffs in state court with statutory damages but reduced procedural leverage.  
4. **Statute-of-limitations triggers**: file as soon as inaccuracies surface; do not assume the discovery rule will save a stale claim. The 5-year repose is absolute.  
5. **State-law claim pleading**: pair FCRA claims with state UDAP / consumer-protection claims and state ID-theft statutes where § 1681t preemption does not apply; § 1681h(e) immunity does not extend to UDAP claims based on conduct beyond mere reporting.  
6. **Attorney's fees**: § 1681n(a)(3) and § 1681o(a)(2) entitle prevailing consumers to fees. Section 1681n(c) also permits fees against attorneys for filing pleadings in bad faith or for harassment, a fee-shifting provision occasionally invoked by defendants.
