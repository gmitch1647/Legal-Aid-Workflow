# FCRA Key Definitions (15 U.S.C. § 1681a)  
  
**Topic:** Analytical explainer of the core defined terms in the Fair Credit Reporting Act  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a (FCRA § 603); with cross-cutting references to § 1681s-2  
**Cross-references:** 12 C.F.R. Part 1022 (Regulation V); FTC, *40 Years of Experience with the Fair Credit Reporting Act* (July 2011)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
The FCRA's substantive obligations (permissible purpose, accuracy, dispute, disclosure, furnisher duties) are all gated by the definitions in § 1681a. Whether the FCRA applies to a given communication or entity almost always turns on whether (a) the communication is a "consumer report," and (b) the source is a "consumer reporting agency." A great deal of FCRA litigation begins and ends with those two questions. The other defined terms — "file," "employment purposes," "adverse action," and "identity theft" — drive specific compliance regimes.  
  
This file walks each key term in the order most often encountered in practice. Every operative excerpt below is from the U.S. Code (Title 15) as compiled by the Office of the Law Revision Counsel and mirrored on Cornell LII and Justia.  
  
---  
  
## 1. Consumer report — § 1681a(d)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(d)(1); source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1681a; https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=(title:15+section:1681a+edition:prelim)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "The term 'consumer report' means any written, oral, or other communication of any information by a consumer reporting agency bearing on a consumer's credit worthiness, credit standing, credit capacity, character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living which is used or expected to be used or collected in whole or in part for the purpose of serving as a factor in establishing the consumer's eligibility for — (A) credit or insurance to be used primarily for personal, family, or household purposes; (B) employment purposes; or (C) any other purpose authorized under section 1681b of this title."  
  
**Plain English:** A "consumer report" is any communication of information by a CRA that touches on one of seven specified factors about a consumer and that is used or expected to be used to evaluate the consumer for credit, insurance, employment, or another § 1681b purpose.  
  
**The seven factors:** (1) credit worthiness, (2) credit standing, (3) credit capacity, (4) character, (5) general reputation, (6) personal characteristics, (7) mode of living.  
  
**Exclusions — § 1681a(d)(2):** The definition expressly excludes (A) reports containing only transaction or experience information between the consumer and the person making the report (the "transactions and experiences" exclusion); (B) certain communications among affiliated companies if the consumer has been given notice and an opportunity to opt out; and (C) communications by a person who is not a CRA from sources other than CRAs to a person who procures a report for employment purposes if certain disclosures are made (the "investigative" / employer-investigation carve-out, expanded by FACTA in 2003).  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- The seven factors are construed broadly. Information about a consumer's residence history, criminal records, employment history, and even social-media-derived character information can qualify.  
- Liability turns heavily on the "used or expected to be used" element. A communication delivered for a § 1681b purpose is a consumer report even if the recipient does not actually use it.  
- The transactions-and-experiences exclusion is the principal reason that a creditor reporting its own account history to a CRA is generally treated as a "furnisher" rather than a CRA.  
- Tenant screening reports, employment background reports, check-writing reports, insurance-claims reports (CLUE/A-PLUS), and medical-payment reports (MIB) are consumer reports — and the agencies that produce them are CRAs.

## 2. Consumer reporting agency — § 1681a(f)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(f); source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1681a  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "The term 'consumer reporting agency' means any person which, for monetary fees, dues, or on a cooperative nonprofit basis, regularly engages in whole or in part in the practice of assembling or evaluating consumer credit information or other information on consumers for the purpose of furnishing consumer reports to third parties, and which uses any means or facility of interstate commerce for the purpose of preparing or furnishing consumer reports."  
  
**Plain English:** A CRA is any entity that (1) acts for fees or on a cooperative nonprofit basis, (2) regularly assembles or evaluates consumer information, (3) for the purpose of furnishing consumer reports to third parties, and (4) uses interstate commerce.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- The "regularly engages" element keeps an occasional informal information-sharer out of CRA status, but courts have read it broadly.  
- The "purpose of furnishing to third parties" element is why a creditor that supplies data to the Big Three is a furnisher, not a CRA — its purpose is internal credit decisioning plus reporting, not assembling reports for others.  
- A reseller (§ 1681a(u)) is a sub-species of CRA that resells data without maintaining a database.  
  
---  
  
## 3. Consumer — § 1681a(c)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(c)  
  
**Operative language:** "The term 'consumer' means an individual."  
  
**Plain English:** Only natural persons. Corporations, partnerships, trusts, and other entities are not "consumers" and cannot bring claims under the FCRA's consumer-facing provisions.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Business credit reports (e.g., D&B) are outside the FCRA because they concern entities, not individuals.  
- Sole-proprietor borrowers occupy a gray area where the question is whether the credit was extended to the individual or to the business; courts look at the nature of the application and the credit.  
  
---  
  
## 4. Person — § 1681a(b)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(b)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "The term 'person' means any individual, partnership, corporation, trust, estate, cooperative, association, government or governmental subdivision or agency, or other entity."  
  
**Plain English:** The broadest possible umbrella. Anyone who can be a defendant under the FCRA is a "person."  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Important because § 1681n and § 1681o impose civil liability on any "person" who violates the Act — including state and local governments to the extent sovereign immunity is waived. Federal-agency suability has been the subject of recent litigation (see *Department of Agriculture Rural Housing Service v. Kirtz*, 601 U.S. 42 (2024)).  
  
---  
  
## 5. File — § 1681a(g)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(g)  
  
**Operative language:** "The term 'file,' when used in connection with information on any consumer, means all of the information on that consumer recorded and retained by a consumer reporting agency regardless of how the information is stored."  
  
**Plain English:** Everything the CRA has on the consumer, in whatever format.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Critical for § 1681g disclosures: the CRA's obligation runs to the entire "file," not merely what the CRA chooses to put on a marketed credit report.  
- Includes archived data, internal codes, dispute records, and metadata. The Ninth Circuit's *Cortez v. Trans Union, LLC*, 617 F.3d 688 (3d Cir. 2010) (originally written about file disclosure) and similar cases have pushed CRAs to disclose more rather than less.  
  
---  
  
## 6. Employment purposes — § 1681a(h)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(h)  
  
**Operative language:** "The term 'employment purposes,' when used in connection with a consumer report, means a report used for the purpose of evaluating a consumer for employment, promotion, reassignment, or retention as an employee."  
  
**Plain English:** Any use of a consumer report in the employment lifecycle — hiring, promotion, reassignment, or retention.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Triggers the heightened procedural rules in § 1681b(b): stand-alone disclosure, written authorization, pre-adverse-action notice with a copy of the report and the Summary of Rights.  
- Volunteer screening, independent-contractor vetting, and screening of gig workers are factually contested. Most circuits treat them as "employment purposes" when the relationship looks employment-like.  
- "Retention as an employee" sweeps in periodic re-screening of current employees, which has been the basis for several large class actions.  
  
---  
  
## 7. Investigative consumer report — § 1681a(e)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(e)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "The term 'investigative consumer report' means a consumer report or portion thereof in which information on a consumer's character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living is obtained through personal interviews with neighbors, friends, or associates of the consumer reported on or with others with whom he is acquainted or who may have knowledge concerning any such items of information."  
  
**Plain English:** A subset of consumer report that relies on personal interviews (rather than database queries) for the character/reputation/personal-characteristics/mode-of-living factors. Information obtained "directly from a creditor of the consumer or from the consumer" is excluded.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Triggers extra disclosure under § 1681d: the user must provide a separate notice to the consumer no later than three days after the report is requested and inform the consumer of the right to request additional disclosures.  
- Tenant-screening and employment-screening reports increasingly mix database and interview methods; the interview portions are "investigative" even if the bulk is database-driven.  
  
---  
  
## 8. Medical information — § 1681a(i)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(i)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "The term 'medical information' (1) means information or data, whether oral or recorded, in any form or medium, created by or derived from a health care provider or the consumer, that relates to (A) the past, present, or future physical, mental, or behavioral health or condition of an individual; (B) the provision of health care to an individual; or (C) the payment for the provision of health care to an individual."  
  
The definition expressly excludes age, gender, demographic information (including residence/email), and other information that does not relate to physical, mental, or behavioral health.  
  
**Plain English:** Health-care-derived information about a consumer's physical, mental, or behavioral health, or about the provision or payment of care.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Used as the trigger for § 1681b(g)'s restrictions on furnishing medical information for credit/employment/insurance, the § 1681c(a)(6) coding requirement for medical-debt tradelines, and the Reg V medical-information rules (12 C.F.R. § 1022.30).  
- The line between "medical information" and a plain account tradeline is contested. The CFPB and FTC have treated medical-debt collection tradelines as containing medical information when the creditor identity reveals the medical nature of the debt.  
  
---  
  
## 9. Adverse action — § 1681a(k)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(k)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "The term 'adverse action' (1)(A) has the same meaning as in section 1691(d)(6) of this title [ECOA]; and (B) means — (i) a denial or cancellation of, an increase in any charge for, or a reduction or other adverse or unfavorable change in the terms of coverage or amount of, any insurance, existing or applied for, in connection with the underwriting of insurance; (ii) a denial of employment or any other decision for employment purposes that adversely affects any current or prospective employee; (iii) a denial or cancellation of, an increase in any charge for, or any other adverse or unfavorable change in the terms of, any license or benefit described in section 1681b(a)(3)(D); and (iv) an action taken or determination that is — (I) made in connection with an application that was made by, or a transaction that was initiated by, any consumer, or in connection with a review of an account under section 1681b(a)(3)(F)(ii); and (II) adverse to the interests of the consumer."  
  
**Plain English:** Four categories: (a) the ECOA credit definition (denial, revocation, unfavorable change in credit terms); (b) insurance underwriting denials/changes; (c) employment denials and adverse employment actions; (d) license/government-benefit denials; (e) any other adverse action tied to a consumer-initiated transaction or an account review.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Adverse action triggers the user-side notice obligations in § 1681m(a) — disclose the CRA's name/address/phone, that the CRA did not make the decision, and the consumer's right to a free file disclosure and to dispute accuracy.  
- *Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007), held that "willful" noncompliance includes recklessness — and resolved the question whether initial insurance rates can be "adverse action" (yes, when based on a credit-based insurance score).  
  
---  
  
## 10. Identity theft — § 1681a(q)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(q)(3)  
  
**Operative language:** "The term 'identity theft' means a fraud committed using the identifying information of another person, subject to such further definition as the Bureau may prescribe, by regulation."  
  
**Plain English:** Use of another person's identifying information to commit fraud. The CFPB (and previously the FTC) has provided additional regulatory definitional refinement at 16 C.F.R. § 603.2 / 12 C.F.R. § 1022.3.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Triggers identity-theft block rights under § 1681c-2, fraud alerts under § 1681c-1, and free file disclosures under § 1681j(d).  
- Proper identity-theft report (FTC Form 14039 / IdentityTheft.gov report) is the gateway document; "[VERIFY]" the precise regulatory definition at 16 C.F.R. § 603.2 / 12 C.F.R. § 1022.3 if a litigation citation is needed.

## 11. Nationwide consumer reporting agency — § 1681a(p)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(p)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "The term 'consumer reporting agency that compiles and maintains files on consumers on a nationwide basis' means a consumer reporting agency that regularly engages in the practice of assembling or evaluating, and maintaining, for the purpose of furnishing consumer reports to third parties bearing on a consumer's credit worthiness, credit standing, or credit capacity, each of the following regarding consumers residing nationwide: (1) Public record information. (2) Credit account information from persons who furnish that information regularly and in the ordinary course of business."  
  
**Plain English:** The Big Three — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Maintains nationwide files combining public records and tradeline data.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Subject to § 1681i full reinvestigation duties, § 1681j(a) free-annual-disclosure obligations via AnnualCreditReport.com, and § 1681c-1 free security freezes.  
- The functional test (not size or revenue) is what matters; in theory a fourth entrant could qualify.  
  
---  
  
## 12. Nationwide specialty consumer reporting agency — § 1681a(x)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(x) (note: this is at subsection (x), not (w) — § 1681a(w) is "active duty military consumer")  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "The term 'nationwide specialty consumer reporting agency' means a consumer reporting agency that compiles and maintains files on consumers on a nationwide basis relating to — (1) medical records or payments; (2) residential or tenant history; (3) check writing history; (4) employment history; or (5) insurance claims."  
  
**Plain English:** A CRA that is nationwide in scope but specializes in one of five vertical categories: medical, tenant, check, employment, or insurance.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Examples: LexisNexis C.L.U.E. (insurance claims); ChexSystems (check-writing); The Work Number / Equifax Workforce Solutions (employment); MIB Group (medical/insurance underwriting); CoreLogic Rental Property Solutions and TransUnion SmartMove (tenant).  
- Subject to § 1681j(a)(1)(C) requirement to provide free annual disclosure on request, though access mechanisms vary by agency.

## 13. Furnisher — derived term, explained via § 1681s-2  
  
**Citation:** Not defined in § 1681a. The operative duties live in 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2 (FCRA § 623); source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1681s-2  
  
**Plain English:** A "furnisher" is any entity that provides information to a CRA. The term is descriptive — § 1681s-2 simply addresses "any person who furnishes information to a consumer reporting agency."  
  
**Operative duties (summary):**  
- § 1681s-2(a) — prohibition on furnishing information the furnisher knows or has reasonable cause to believe is inaccurate; duty to correct and update; duty to notify of consumer disputes received directly; duty to notify CRAs of voluntary account closures by consumer.  
- § 1681s-2(b) — duty, upon receipt of a § 1681i indirect dispute notice from a CRA, to (A) investigate, (B) review information given to the CRA by the consumer, (C) report results to the CRA, (D) if information is found incomplete/inaccurate, report results to all other nationwide CRAs to which the furnisher provided the information, and (E) modify, delete, or permanently block the disputed information if necessary.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- The principal private right of action against furnishers is for violations of § 1681s-2(b); § 1681s-2(a) violations are generally enforceable only by regulators, not private plaintiffs (*Nelson v. Chase Manhattan Mortgage Corp.*, 282 F.3d 1057 (9th Cir. 2002), and its progeny).  
- The "investigation" required by § 1681s-2(b) must be "reasonable" — purely procedural "did we report what's in our system" investigations have been rejected by the Fourth, Seventh, Ninth, and Eleventh Circuits.

## Practical litigation/compliance notes  
  
- **Threshold-of-applicability disputes.** The two most common motions to dismiss are (a) "this wasn't a consumer report" and (b) "we weren't a CRA." Both turn on § 1681a(d) and § 1681a(f). Plaintiffs should plead the seven-factor element and the § 1681b purpose element specifically.  
- **Affiliate-sharing exclusion.** § 1681a(d)(2)(A)(iii) is the affiliate-sharing exception. Compliance hinges on whether notice and opt-out were properly offered — many class actions have arisen from defective opt-out notices.  
- **Employer-investigation exclusion.** Post-FACTA § 1681a(x) [old numbering] / § 1681a(y) covers employer investigations of suspected misconduct; check the exact subsection in the current code before pleading, since the lettering has shifted.  
- **"Used or expected to be used."** The collection prong is often overlooked: a CRA's intent at the time of collection can convert otherwise innocuous data into a consumer report.  
- **Medical information / debt collection.** The interaction between § 1681a(i) and the 2022 NCAP changes (medical debts under $500 not reported; one-year delay before reporting; paid medical debts removed) is a frequent area of confusion. The statutory definition has not changed; CRA practices have.
