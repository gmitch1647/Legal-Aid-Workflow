# FCRA Accuracy Obligations and CRA Duties  
**Topic:** Compliance procedures and accuracy obligations of consumer reporting agencies (CRAs) under the Fair Credit Reporting Act  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681e (FCRA § 607); 15 U.S.C. § 1681k (FCRA § 613)  
**Cross-references:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b (permissible purposes); 15 U.S.C. § 1681c (information excluded from consumer reports); 15 U.S.C. § 1681i (reinvestigation); 12 C.F.R. Part 1022 (Regulation V)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
## Introduction  
  
Section 607 of the FCRA, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1681e, establishes the foundational compliance and accuracy obligations of consumer reporting agencies. It is the single most litigated CRA-side provision in the statute, principally because subsection (b) — the "maximum possible accuracy" duty — supports a private right of action and has spawned decades of circuit-level interpretation. This file unpacks each subsection of § 1681e, addresses the related public-record duty in § 1681k, surveys the leading appellate doctrines (mixed-file claims, technically-accurate-but-misleading reporting, name-only matching), and clarifies the critical doctrinal distinction between § 1681e(b) liability and § 1681i reinvestigation liability — two independent claim tracks that consumer-law plaintiffs frequently plead in tandem.  
  
## § 1681e — Compliance Procedures  
  
### § 1681e(a) — User certification and permissible-purpose limitation  
  
Section 1681e(a) requires every CRA to "maintain reasonable procedures designed to avoid violations of section 1681c of this title and to limit the furnishing of consumer reports to the purposes listed under section 1681b of this title." The procedures must require that prospective users (1) identify themselves, (2) certify the permissible purpose for which the report is sought, and (3) certify that the information "will be used for no other purpose." The statute also requires that the CRA make a "reasonable effort to verify the identity of a new prospective user and the uses certified by such prospective user prior to furnishing such user a consumer report." 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(a).  
  
The provision creates a gatekeeping duty: the CRA must operate a vetting regime at the front end, not merely respond to misuse after the fact. The reasonableness standard parallels — but is distinct from — the accuracy standard in subsection (b). A CRA can violate § 1681e(a) even when its reports are accurate, if it furnishes those accurate reports to recipients lacking a permissible purpose under § 1681b. Conversely, the FTC's longstanding guidance has been that "reasonable procedures" do not require the CRA to police every request; rather, the procedures must be objectively reasonable in light of foreseeable misuse.  
  
### § 1681e(b) — "Maximum possible accuracy"  
  
Section 1681e(b) is the bedrock accuracy provision. It states: "Whenever a consumer reporting agency prepares a consumer report it shall follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy of the information concerning the individual about whom the report relates." 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b).  
  
**Negligence-based, not strict liability.** Despite the phrase "maximum possible accuracy," every circuit to address the question has held that § 1681e(b) is not a strict-liability standard. The Seventh Circuit in *Sarver v. Experian Information Solutions*, 390 F.3d 969 (7th Cir. 2004), squarely rejected strict liability: the FCRA "does not make reporting agencies strictly liable for all inaccuracies," and liability attaches only where the CRA "failed to follow reasonable procedures to assure the accuracy of its credit reports." See *Sarver*, 390 F.3d at 972. The plaintiff must prove (1) that the CRA reported inaccurate information about the consumer, and (2) that the inaccuracy resulted from the CRA's failure to follow reasonable procedures.  
  
**Technically-accurate-but-misleading doctrine.** The D.C. Circuit in *Koropoulos v. Credit Bureau, Inc.*, 734 F.2d 37 (D.C. Cir. 1984), held that a report can violate § 1681e(b) even where every individual data point is technically true, if the overall impression conveyed is misleading or materially incomplete. The court rejected the narrow reading of "accuracy" that some district courts had adopted, observing that "Congress did not limit the Act's mandate to reasonable procedures to assure only technical accuracy" — the statute requires procedures to assure "maximum" accuracy, which encompasses freedom from misleading presentation. *Koropoulos*, 734 F.2d at 40. The case involved a "9-0" tradeline code that, while technically reflecting an underlying status, suggested an unpaid loss when the debt had in fact been discharged through other means. Most circuits have since adopted the *Koropoulos* framing; see, e.g., *Sepulvado v. CSC Credit Servs.*, 158 F.3d 890 (5th Cir. 1998); *Saunders v. Branch Banking & Trust Co.*, 526 F.3d 142 (4th Cir. 2008) (applying the same "misleading in such a way and to such an extent that it can be expected to adversely affect credit decisions" standard).  
  
**Mixed files.** A "mixed file" exists when a CRA combines information about two different consumers into a single file, typically because of partial identifier overlap (same or similar name, partial Social Security number match, shared address). Mixed-file claims are quintessential § 1681e(b) cases because the inaccuracy is the very attribution of another person's data to the consumer. *Williams v. First Advantage Background Services Corp.*, 947 F.3d 735 (11th Cir. 2020), is the leading modern decision. There, the background screening company twice attributed criminal-history records of a different "Ricky Williams" to the plaintiff, who lost two job offers as a result. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed a $250,000 compensatory damages award and held that punitive damages were available but reduced the $3.3 million punitive award to $1 million on constitutional excessiveness grounds. The case is also significant because the defendant's own VP of Compliance testified that its internal "third-identifier" matching rule was "kind of aspirational" — evidence the jury was entitled to credit on the willfulness question.  
  
**Name-only matching.** The FTC, in its 2011 report *40 Years of Experience with the Fair Credit Reporting Act: An FTC Staff Report with Summary of Interpretations*, criticized matching algorithms that rely on name alone or on a small number of partial identifiers. The staff report observed that name-only matching is a leading cause of mixed-file errors and that reasonable procedures under § 1681e(b) ordinarily require corroboration through additional identifiers such as date of birth, full Social Security number, or address history. Plaintiffs' counsel routinely cite the report in expert disclosures and in opposition to summary judgment motions, even though the report is not binding regulation.  
  
**Public-record information and § 1681k.** Section 1681k addresses CRAs that report public-record information likely to have an adverse effect on the consumer's ability to obtain employment. The CRA must either (1) notify the consumer at the time the information is reported to the user, or (2) maintain "strict procedures designed to insure that whenever public record information which is likely to have an adverse effect on a consumer's ability to obtain employment is reported it is complete and up to date." 15 U.S.C. § 1681k(a). The Seventh Circuit's *Henson v. CSC Credit Services*, 29 F.3d 280 (7th Cir. 1994), held that a CRA is generally entitled to rely on the accuracy of court records as initially reported in public docket sources, at least where the consumer has not previously disputed the information. *Henson* is frequently cited by defendant CRAs to limit accuracy claims based on docketing errors, but later decisions have narrowed its reach where the CRA had reason to know the public record was unreliable or where the dispute mechanism in § 1681i has already been invoked.  
  
### § 1681e(c) — Disclosure of investigative consumer reports  
  
Section 1681e(c) restricts the use and disclosure of "investigative consumer reports" — reports based on personal interviews with neighbors, associates, or other third parties about the consumer's character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living (defined at § 1681a(e)). It requires that the CRA not prepare such a report unless it has reasonable procedures to provide the consumer with the disclosures required by § 1681d. ### § 1681e(d) — Notice to furnisher; furnisher disclosures  
  
Section 1681e(d) addresses the notice that a CRA must provide to persons who furnish information about consumers. The CRA must provide a "clear and conspicuous" notice to furnishers of their responsibilities under the FCRA. The FTC and CFPB have published model notices that satisfy this requirement; furnisher-side compliance frequently turns on whether the furnisher received and acknowledged such notice. The provision intersects with § 1681s-2's furnisher duties, which are the subject of a separate file in this corpus.  
  
### § 1681e(e) — Procurers of reports for resale; identification of end users  
  
Section 1681e(e) addresses procurers who obtain consumer reports for the purpose of reselling them. Resellers must (1) disclose to the CRA the identity of the end user of the report and the permissible purpose for which the end user will use it, and (2) establish and follow reasonable procedures designed to ensure that the resold report is not used for any purpose other than the certified purpose. The provision was added to address the proliferation of "specialty CRAs" and information brokers that resell consumer report data.  
  
## Regulation V Subpart B and Subpart M obligations on CRAs  
  
Regulation V, 12 C.F.R. Part 1022, implements multiple FCRA provisions and is administered by the CFPB. Subpart B (12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.20–.27) governs duties relating to the use of consumer reports and includes affiliate-marketing opt-out rules. Subpart M addresses the duty of CRAs to provide consumer disclosures and free annual reports under § 1681j. ## Distinguishing § 1681e(b) from § 1681i: independent claims  
  
A recurring source of confusion in FCRA litigation is the relationship between § 1681e(b) (the accuracy-procedures duty) and § 1681i (the reinvestigation duty triggered by a consumer dispute). They are independent claims with independent elements and independent defenses.  
  
- **§ 1681e(b)** is a duty *at the time of report preparation*. The plaintiff need not have submitted a pre-suit dispute to bring a § 1681e(b) claim. Liability turns on whether the CRA's initial procedures were reasonable; a single inaccurate report is sufficient to trigger the claim if causation and damages are established.  
- **§ 1681i** is a duty *after* the consumer disputes information in the file. The plaintiff must have first submitted a dispute to the CRA. Liability turns on whether the CRA conducted a reasonable reinvestigation — not on whether the original report was accurate.  
  
Plaintiffs typically plead both claims in the alternative, and courts have consistently held that recovery under one does not preclude recovery under the other. See *Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.*, 115 F.3d 220, 223–24 (3d Cir. 1997) (treating § 1681e(b) and § 1681i(a) as analytically distinct duties evaluated under separate reasonableness inquiries).  
  
## Practical litigation/compliance notes  
  
- **Pleading § 1681e(b).** Allege (1) the specific inaccuracy in the consumer's report, (2) facts showing the inaccuracy resulted from a procedural failure (e.g., name-only matching, failure to use SSN, reliance on unverified court records), (3) causation, and (4) damages. Specificity at the pleading stage matters; conclusory allegations of "unreasonable procedures" are routinely dismissed.  
- **Discovery focus.** Matching algorithms, vendor agreements with public-record suppliers, internal QA audits, and prior consumer complaints about similar errors are core discovery targets. Plaintiffs frequently subpoena the FTC and CFPB enforcement files and depose the CRA's data-quality compliance officer.  
- **Willfulness for statutory damages.** Under § 1681n, the plaintiff may recover statutory damages of $100 to $1,000 per violation plus punitive damages for willful violations. *Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007), set the willfulness standard as "reckless disregard" — an objectively unreasonable reading of the statute that exposes the defendant to risk substantially greater than the risk associated with a merely careless reading. *Williams v. First Advantage* applied this standard in a § 1681e(b) context.  
- **CRA defenses.** "Reasonable procedures" as a matter of law; immunity for relying on public records (where applicable post-*Henson*); preemption of state-law claims under § 1681t; lack of an "inaccuracy" in the strict sense (a hard sell after *Koropoulos* in most circuits).  
- **Damages.** Actual damages include credit denial, higher interest rates, emotional distress (recoverable in most circuits), and reputational harm. Punitive damages under *Williams* may be substantial but are constrained by the *State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408 (2003), due-process ratio analysis.
