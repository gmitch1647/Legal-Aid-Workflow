# FCRA Adverse Action and Risk-Based Pricing  
**Topic:** Notice obligations of users of consumer reports — adverse action, risk-based pricing, negative information, employment pre-adverse action  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681m; 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)  
**Cross-references:** Reg V Subpart H (12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.70–.75); ECOA / Reg B (12 C.F.R. § 1002.9); FCRA case law file (10); FCRA Employment file (10b)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
## Introduction  
  
Section 1681m of the FCRA imposes affirmative notice duties on *users* of consumer reports — the lenders, insurers, employers, and landlords who pull credit and background reports — rather than on the consumer reporting agencies (CRAs) themselves. The provision is one of the FCRA's central transparency mechanisms: when a report is used against a consumer, the consumer must be told and pointed toward the channels for dispute and free disclosure. The provision has been heavily amended by FACTA (2003), with critical consequences for private enforcement under § 1681m(h)(8).  
  
This file addresses § 1681m in depth, the parallel pre-adverse action regime for employment under § 1681b(b)(3), and the interaction with the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) and its Regulation B.  
  
## § 1681m(a) — Adverse Action Notices  
  
### Trigger: "adverse action" defined  
  
"Adverse action" is defined in § 1681a(k) and is materially broader than outright denial of credit. It encompasses (i) a denial or revocation of credit, a change in the terms of an existing credit arrangement, or a refusal to grant credit in substantially the amount or on substantially the terms requested; (ii) a denial or cancellation of, an increase in any charge for, or any other adverse or unfavorable change in the terms of any insurance, existing or applied for; (iii) a denial of employment or any other decision for employment purposes that adversely affects any current or prospective employee; and (iv) an action taken or determination made that is adverse to the interests of the consumer in connection with any application or transaction initiated by the consumer.  
  
This breadth is consequential. An *increase* in an interest rate, a *decrease* in a credit limit, or any *unfavorable change* in terms can trigger § 1681m(a) when based in whole or in part on a consumer report.  
  
### Required contents of the notice  
  
When a person takes adverse action with respect to a consumer based in whole or in part on information in a consumer report, § 1681m(a) requires that person to:  
  
- Provide oral, written, or electronic notice of the adverse action to the consumer;  
- Provide the name, address, and telephone number of the CRA that furnished the report (including a toll-free number for nationwide CRAs);  
- Include a statement that the CRA did not make the adverse decision and is unable to provide the specific reasons for it;  
- Notify the consumer of the right to obtain a free copy of the consumer report from the CRA within 60 days; and  
- Notify the consumer of the right to dispute the accuracy or completeness of any information in the report with the CRA.  
  
### Credit-score disclosure  
  
Section 1681m(a) was supplemented by FACTA to require that, when a numerical credit score was used in taking adverse action on credit, the user must provide the consumer with the score, the range of possible scores, key factors that adversely affected the score (typically the top four or five "reason codes"), the date the score was created, and the name of the entity that provided the score. The CFPB's "Notice to Users" model form summarizes these requirements.  
  
### Intersection with ECOA / Regulation B  
  
ECOA, 15 U.S.C. § 1691(d), and its implementing Regulation B, 12 C.F.R. § 1002.9, also require notice of adverse action in connection with credit. The FCRA and ECOA notices overlap but are distinct:  
  
- ECOA/Reg B applies to credit applications and requires a statement of *specific reasons* for the adverse action (or notice of the right to obtain them).  
- FCRA § 1681m applies whenever a consumer *report* (consumer, employment, insurance, etc.) is used and does not require specific reasons — only the CRA information and the right-to-dispute language.  
  
Most creditors meet both obligations with a combined form. ECOA's specific-reasons requirement is satisfied with reason codes (e.g., "Insufficient credit history," "Too many recent inquiries"); FCRA's notice requirement is satisfied with the CRA contact block and rights statements.  
  
## § 1681m(b) — Adverse Action Based on Information from Third Parties (Non-CRAs)  
  
Section 1681m(b) addresses a narrower channel: adverse action based on information from a *non-CRA* third party (for example, a prior employer's verbal reference). On the consumer's written request within 60 days of receiving notice, the user must disclose to the consumer "the nature" of the information that triggered the adverse action. This duty does not require disclosure of the *source*, and it is far less litigated than § 1681m(a).  
  
## § 1681m(g) — Negative Information Furnisher Notice  
  
Section 1681m(g) requires a financial institution that extends credit and regularly furnishes information to a nationwide CRA to provide a one-time notice to the consumer either before furnishing negative information or within 30 days after doing so. The CFPB has prescribed a model notice in Reg V (Appendix B to Part 1022). The notice is typically embedded in account statements and welcome materials.  
  
## § 1681m(h) — Risk-Based Pricing Notice  
  
Section 1681m(h) was added by FACTA to capture a gap in § 1681m(a): when a consumer receives credit but on *materially less favorable* terms than the most favorable terms available to a substantial proportion of consumers based on a consumer report, the consumer often did not receive any FCRA notice because there was no formal "adverse action." Section 1681m(h) closes that gap.  
  
The implementing regulation is Reg V Subpart H, 12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.70–1022.75, jointly issued by the FTC and the Federal Reserve Board (and now administered for many entities by the CFPB). The rule allows several methods of compliance, including a direct comparison method, a credit-score-proxy method, and a tiered-pricing method. Compliance can also be achieved by giving every applicant a credit score disclosure exception notice — most large lenders use this method.  
  
### CRITICAL: § 1681m(h)(8) — No Private Right of Action  
  
Section 1681m(h)(8) provides that violations of § 1681m(h) "shall be enforced exclusively under section 1681s of this title by the Federal agencies and officials and the State officials identified in section 1681s of this title." Courts have read this as eliminating any private right of action for risk-based-pricing-notice violations.  
  
More broadly, the Seventh Circuit and most district courts to address the question have held that § 1681m(h)(8) eliminates the private right of action for violations of § 1681m *in its entirety*, not just § 1681m(h) — that is, even the § 1681m(a) adverse-action notice itself is privately unenforceable. See *Perry v. First Nat'l Bank*, 459 F.3d 816 (7th Cir. 2006). Plaintiffs in those circuits must instead piggyback on § 1681b (impermissible purpose) or § 1681e/§ 1681i (accuracy/dispute) theories. The split among courts is not fully resolved, but plaintiffs' counsel typically treat § 1681m claims as carrying real enforcement risk only via the government channels in § 1681s. [VERIFY: a minority of district courts have read § 1681m(h)(8) more narrowly; check current circuit status.]  
  
## § 1681b(b)(3) — Pre-Adverse Action Notice (Employment)  
  
A separate and frequently litigated regime applies in the *employment* context. Before an employer takes adverse action based in whole or in part on a consumer report, § 1681b(b)(3) requires the employer to provide:  
  
- A copy of the consumer report; and  
- A description in writing of the consumer's rights under the FCRA (the "Summary of Your Rights Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act" prescribed by the CFPB and provided by the CRA under § 1681g(c)).  
  
The pre-adverse action notice must precede the final adverse action by a "reasonable" interval — long enough to give the applicant a meaningful opportunity to dispute the report. The FTC's longstanding informal guidance suggests roughly five business days, and the *Reardon v. ClosetMaid* line of cases has treated eight days as comfortably reasonable. There is no bright-line rule.  
  
Crucially, courts have widely held that § 1681b(b)(3) creates its own *private right of action* — courts treat it as separate from § 1681m. See *Long v. Tommy Hilfiger U.S.A., Inc.*, 671 F.3d 371 (3d Cir. 2012); *Goode v. LexisNexis Risk & Info. Analytics Sols., Inc.*, 284 F.R.D. 238 (E.D. Pa. 2012). Post-*TransUnion v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), federal courts have grappled with whether a § 1681b(b)(3) violation by itself satisfies Article III standing or whether the plaintiff must show concrete consequences (denial of the job, lost wages, emotional harm from the procedural failure).  
  
## Major Cases on Adverse Action and Notice  
  
- **Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr**, 551 U.S. 47 (2007). Established the "objectively unreasonable" recklessness standard for willfulness under § 1681n. In the risk-based-pricing/insurance-pricing context, the Court held an insurer's reading of an ambiguous § 1681m provision was not objectively unreasonable. The case is the controlling willfulness precedent for *all* FCRA notice violations.  
- **Reardon v. ClosetMaid Corp.**, No. 2:08-CV-01730, 2013 WL 6231606 (W.D. Pa. Dec. 2, 2013). Addressed both stand-alone-disclosure violations under § 1681b(b)(2) and the timing of pre-adverse action notice; eight days deemed a reasonable interval. The case is frequently cited in class actions challenging combined disclosure/waiver forms.  
- **Long v. Tommy Hilfiger U.S.A., Inc.**, 671 F.3d 371 (3d Cir. 2012). Recognized § 1681b(b)(3) as a discrete cause of action with its own elements; clarifying that "adverse action based in whole or in part" includes employment decisions and that the statutory text mandates a copy of the report before adverse action.  
- **Goode v. LexisNexis Risk & Info. Analytics Sols., Inc.**, 284 F.R.D. 238 (E.D. Pa. 2012). Important for procedural mechanics in class certification of pre-adverse action claims.  
  
## Common Violation Patterns (CFPB / FTC)  
  
CFPB and FTC enforcement and supervisory findings repeatedly identify the following:  
  
- Sending the adverse action notice without the CRA contact information, or with the wrong CRA listed (e.g., the reseller rather than the source bureau).  
- Failing to include the right to a free disclosure and the right to dispute.  
- Omitting the credit-score disclosure when a score was used.  
- Skipping the pre-adverse action notice in employment screening, or compressing the interval so the applicant has no realistic chance to dispute.  
- Failing to send a risk-based pricing notice when offering credit on materially less-favorable terms, or sending a credit-score-exception notice that does not meet Reg V's content requirements.  
- Treating a "counteroffer" (different rate, smaller line) as a non-adverse action; in many cases this is still adverse action under § 1681a(k).  
  
## Practical Compliance and Litigation Notes  
  
1. **Build the FCRA notice into the credit-decision workflow, not as a back-office afterthought.** The most common failure mode is operational, not legal.  
2. **Use a combined ECOA/FCRA adverse action form** that contains specific reasons (ECOA), the CRA contact block (FCRA), the right-to-free-disclosure and right-to-dispute language, and the credit-score disclosure when applicable.  
3. **In the employment context, structure the workflow as two letters**: a pre-adverse action letter with the report and Summary of Rights, then — after a reasonable waiting period — a final adverse action letter. Do not collapse them.  
4. **Risk-based pricing**: most lenders use the credit-score-disclosure-exception method (12 C.F.R. § 1022.74) to all applicants, which is operationally simpler than per-applicant tier comparisons.  
5. **Private-enforcement strategy**: plaintiffs' counsel should not file § 1681m claims standing alone in circuits following *Perry v. First Nat'l Bank*; pair with § 1681b or § 1681e claims, or with § 1681b(b)(3) in employment.  
6. **Post-*TransUnion v. Ramirez*** standing: a procedural § 1681b(b)(3) violation may not, by itself, satisfy Article III; plead concrete harm (lost job, emotional distress, time spent disputing, inability to correct errors).
