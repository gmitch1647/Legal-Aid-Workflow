# TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. ___, 141 S. Ct. 2190 (2021)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. ___, 141 S. Ct. 2190, 210 L. Ed. 2d 568 (2021)
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Date:** June 25, 2021
**No.:** 20-297
**Author:** Justice Kavanaugh (majority); Thomas, J. dissenting; Kagan, J. dissenting
**Vote:** 5-4 (Kavanaugh, Roberts, Alito, Gorsuch, Barrett vs. Thomas, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan)
**Appeal From:** Ninth Circuit (951 F.3d 1008)
**Statutes:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures), § 1681g(a)(1) (file disclosure), § 1681g(c)(2) (summary of rights), § 1681n(a) (willful noncompliance)

---

## Facts

TransUnion is one of the "Big Three" credit reporting agencies. Beginning in 2002, TransUnion introduced an add-on product called "OFAC Name Screen Alert." When a business opted into this service, TransUnion would compare the consumer's name against the U.S. Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) list of terrorists, drug traffickers, and other serious criminals.

**The critical flaw:** TransUnion compared ONLY first and last names — no other identifying data (date of birth, SSN, address, middle name). This meant any consumer whose first and last name matched someone on the OFAC list was flagged as a "potential match" to a terrorist or drug trafficker.

**Sergio Ramirez's experience:** When Ramirez tried to purchase a car, the dealership pulled his credit report and received an OFAC alert flagging him as a potential match to a terrorist. The dealer told Ramirez in front of his wife and father-in-law that he could not purchase the vehicle because his name was on a "terrorist list." He suffered humiliation and could not complete the transaction.

**Class definition:** A class of 8,185 individuals with OFAC alerts in their credit files sued TransUnion. The parties stipulated that:
- **1,853 class members** had their misleading credit reports (containing OFAC alerts) actually PROVIDED TO THIRD PARTIES during the 7-month class period
- **6,332 class members** had OFAC alerts in their INTERNAL credit files but those files were NOT provided to any third party during the class period

**Additional claims:** All 8,185 class members also complained about formatting defects in mailings sent by TransUnion — specifically that TransUnion sent required disclosures in a confusing "dual mailing" format rather than a single clear communication.

---

## Procedural History

1. **District Court:** Ruled all 8,185 class members had Article III standing on all three claims. Jury returned verdict for plaintiffs — awarded statutory damages and punitive damages to every class member (~$40 million total).
2. **Ninth Circuit:** Affirmed (divided panel — Judge McKeown dissented on standing).
3. **Supreme Court:** REVERSED and REMANDED — only 1,853 class members with disseminated reports have standing on the §1681e(b) claim; only Ramirez himself has standing on the mailing-format claims.

## Holdings

### Holding 1: Article III Requires Concrete Harm Even for Statutory Violations

> "Article III standing requires a concrete injury even in the context of a statutory violation." A plaintiff does NOT automatically satisfy injury-in-fact merely because a statute grants them a right and authorizes suit. "An injury in law is not an injury in fact."

**Translation:** You can't sue just because a statute was violated. You must show the violation actually HARMED you in a concrete way.

### Holding 2: Dissemination to Third Parties = Concrete Harm (1,853 class members)

The 1,853 class members whose misleading OFAC-alert credit reports were provided to third-party businesses suffered a harm with a "close relationship" to the tort of defamation. Being labeled a "potential match" to a terrorist in a report sent to a creditor is reputational injury — concrete harm exists.

> "Under longstanding American law, a person is injured when a defamatory statement 'that would subject him to hatred, contempt, or ridicule' is published to a third party."

### Holding 3: No Dissemination = No Concrete Harm (6,332 class members)

The 6,332 class members whose files contained OFAC alerts that were NEVER sent to anyone do NOT have standing.

> "The mere existence of inaccurate information, absent dissemination, traditionally has not provided the basis for a lawsuit in American courts."

**Critical distinction:** Internal inaccuracy alone ≠ injury. The inaccuracy must be PUBLISHED (sent to a third party) to constitute concrete harm analogous to defamation.

### Holding 4: Risk of Future Harm Insufficient for Damages Claims

The 6,332 plaintiffs argued they faced a "risk" that the misleading info might be disseminated in the future. The Court rejected this:

> "The mere risk of future harm, without more, cannot qualify as a concrete harm in a suit for damages."

**Note:** Risk of future harm MAY support standing for INJUNCTIVE RELIEF claims (prospective relief to prevent harm) — but NOT for damages claims (backward-looking monetary recovery).

### Holding 5: Formatting Violations Without Downstream Harm = No Standing

The mailing-format claims (wrong format for disclosures) failed because the class members could not show the formatting error actually CAUSED them any concrete harm — they still received the information, just in a confusing format, and made no showing that the format prevented them from taking corrective action.

## The Standing Framework After TransUnion v. Ramirez

To establish Article III standing in an FCRA case, a plaintiff must show:

1. **A statutory violation occurred** (§1681e(b), §1681i, etc.)
2. **The violation caused CONCRETE harm** — assessed by whether the harm has a "close relationship" to a harm traditionally recognized at common law
3. **For §1681e(b) accuracy claims:** the inaccurate report must have been DISSEMINATED to a third party (publication = concrete harm via defamation analogy)
4. **For procedural violations** (formatting, timing, notice): must show the procedural failure caused actual downstream harm — not just a "bare procedural violation"
5. **Risk of future harm** alone is insufficient for damages — only supports injunctive relief standing

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When TransUnion v. Ramirez HELPS Your Case

1. **Your client's inaccurate report WAS sent to a third party** — If a lender, landlord, or employer pulled the report and saw the inaccuracy, your client has clear standing under *Ramirez*. The 1,853 class members who had reports disseminated clearly had standing. Most of your cases involve exactly this scenario (credit application denied, report pulled by dealer, etc.).

2. **Strengthens damages for humiliation** — Ramirez himself suffered humiliation when told in front of family he was on a "terrorist list." Use this to support emotional distress damages in cases where the client was denied in an embarrassing manner.

3. **§1681e(b) claims are strong when dissemination is proven** — *Ramirez* confirms that inaccurate information + publication to third party = concrete injury as a matter of law.

### When TransUnion v. Ramirez Creates a CHALLENGE

1. **If your client's inaccurate report was NEVER pulled** — If no third party ever saw the inaccuracy (no applications submitted, no inquiries during the period), your client may lack standing for damages. Consider:
   - Was there ANY inquiry during the period? Pull the credit report to check.
   - Did the client APPLY for credit and get denied? That's dissemination.
   - Was the inaccuracy on the file when an existing creditor did a periodic review? That may count.

2. **Pure procedural claims** (§1681i(a)(6) notice timing, §1681g formatting) — Must show the procedural failure caused actual harm, not just that the statute was technically violated.

3. **Reinvestigation claims where the report was never re-pulled** — If the CRA failed to properly reinvestigate but no one ever SAW the continued inaccuracy, standing is questionable after *Ramirez*.

### Pleading Strategies Post-Ramirez

**Always allege dissemination explicitly:**
> "During the period the inaccurate information was present on Plaintiff's credit file, the following third parties obtained Plaintiff's consumer report: [list creditors/inquiries]. Each of these third parties received a report containing the inaccurate information, causing Plaintiff reputational harm analogous to defamation."

**Pull the credit report and identify ALL inquiries during the inaccuracy period.** Each inquiry = a publication = concrete harm. List them in the complaint.

**For denied applications, plead the denial specifically:**
> "On [date], Plaintiff applied for [credit product] with [lender]. [Lender] obtained Plaintiff's consumer report from Defendant [CRA], which contained the inaccurate [description]. As a direct result of the inaccuracy, Plaintiff's application was [denied / offered less favorable terms], causing concrete financial and reputational harm."

**For emotional distress (Ramirez-style humiliation):**
> "Plaintiff was informed of the denial in [embarrassing circumstances — at a car dealership in front of family, by a landlord in front of roommates, etc.], causing humiliation, embarrassment, and emotional distress."

## Discovery Based on Ramirez

**Key discovery to establish standing:**
1. Request ALL subscriber inquiries during the period the inaccuracy was present
2. Request the actual credit reports that were furnished to third parties (to prove they contained the inaccuracy)
3. Request the dates and recipients of every credit report generated for the plaintiff
4. Request any adverse action notices the plaintiff received

**Interrogatory:**
> "Identify every person or entity to whom you furnished a consumer report concerning Plaintiff during the period [first reporting of inaccuracy] through [date of correction], including the date of each furnishing and the content of each report with respect to [the disputed account/information]."

## Distinguishing Ramirez (When It Doesn't Apply)

*Ramirez* does NOT bar claims where:
- The report WAS disseminated (most real cases)
- The consumer is seeking injunctive relief (risk of future harm supports injunctive standing)
- The harm is monetary (denied credit = financial harm = always concrete)
- The consumer can show downstream effects of procedural violations

*Ramirez* is a standing case — it does NOT change the substantive FCRA requirements. CRAs must still follow reasonable procedures even if a particular plaintiff can't show dissemination.
