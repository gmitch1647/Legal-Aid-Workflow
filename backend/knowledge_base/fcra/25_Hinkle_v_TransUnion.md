# Hinkle v. TransUnion LLC — The CRA Companion Case

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Dana Hinkle, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. Midland Credit Management, Inc.; TransUnion LLC, Defendants-Appellees, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit
**Date:** July 7, 2016
**Docket:** No. 15-11003
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the Middle District of Alabama
**Panel:** Judges Tjoflat, Marcus, and Anderson
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a) (CRA reinvestigation duty), § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy), § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher investigation duty), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance), § 1681n (willful noncompliance)

**BINDING AUTHORITY:** This is Eleventh Circuit precedent, directly binding on all district courts within the circuit, including the Northern District of Georgia.

**NOTE ON CASE IDENTITY:** *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Management* and *Hinkle v. TransUnion* arise from the same underlying action. Dana Hinkle sued BOTH the furnisher (Midland Credit Management) AND the consumer reporting agency (TransUnion) in the same lawsuit. The Eleventh Circuit addressed claims against both defendants in the same opinion at 827 F.3d 1295. This brief focuses on the CRA-specific holdings — the claims and analysis directed at TransUnion's conduct under § 1681i(a) and § 1681e(b). The companion brief (File 20) addresses the furnisher-specific holdings against Midland.

## Facts (CRA-Specific Focus)

Dana Hinkle disputed information appearing on her TransUnion credit report. The disputed information had been furnished by Midland Credit Management, a debt buyer. Hinkle contended that the reported debt was inaccurate — either not hers, in the wrong amount, or with an incorrect status.

**Hinkle's Dispute to TransUnion:** Hinkle submitted a dispute to TransUnion challenging the accuracy of the Midland tradeline on her credit report. Her dispute was not a bare denial — she provided specific information explaining why the reported information was inaccurate.

**TransUnion's Reinvestigation:** Upon receiving Hinkle's dispute, TransUnion initiated its reinvestigation under § 1681i(a). TransUnion's reinvestigation process consisted of:

1. Transmitting an Automated Consumer Dispute Verification (ACDV) to Midland Credit Management via the e-OSCAR system
2. The ACDV contained coded information about the nature of the dispute but did not necessarily convey all of the specific information Hinkle provided
3. Midland received the ACDV, checked its own internal records (the purchased debt file), and verified the information back to TransUnion
4. TransUnion accepted Midland's verification and concluded its reinvestigation — maintaining the disputed information on Hinkle's credit report without change

**What TransUnion Did NOT Do:**
- Did not request underlying account documentation from Midland
- Did not independently investigate the accuracy of the reported information
- Did not go beyond the automated ACDV/e-OSCAR process
- Did not conduct human review of Hinkle's specific dispute and the consumer-submitted information
- Did not follow up when the furnisher (a debt buyer with limited records) simply verified without providing substantiation
- Did not consider whether a debt buyer's verification — based solely on a purchased data file — was reliable under the circumstances

**The Systemic Problem:** TransUnion's reinvestigation was entirely dependent on Midland's response to the ACDV. When Midland (which itself had only a purchased electronic data file and no original documentation) verified the information, TransUnion treated that verification as dispositive — without questioning whether a debt buyer's verification, based on limited purchased data, was reliable or sufficient to resolve the consumer's specific dispute.

---

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (M.D. Ala.):** Granted summary judgment to both TransUnion and Midland, finding the CRA's reinvestigation and the furnisher's investigation were reasonable as a matter of law
2. **Eleventh Circuit:** REVERSED and REMANDED as to both defendants — holding that genuine issues of material fact existed regarding both TransUnion's reinvestigation under § 1681i(a) and Midland's investigation under § 1681s-2(b)

## Holdings (CRA-Specific)

### Holding 1: CRA's Reinvestigation Cannot Rely Solely on Furnisher Verification via e-OSCAR

> "A CRA's reinvestigation is not necessarily reasonable merely because it sent an ACDV to the furnisher and the furnisher verified. The CRA has an independent obligation to conduct a reasonable reinvestigation."

The court held that TransUnion could not satisfy its § 1681i(a) obligation by simply sending an ACDV to Midland, accepting Midland's verification, and closing the file. The CRA has its own, independent reinvestigation duty — it cannot outsource that duty entirely to the furnisher through the automated e-OSCAR system.

### Holding 2: CRA Must Consider Reliability of Furnisher's Verification

When the furnisher is a debt buyer or other entity with limited documentation, the CRA must consider whether the furnisher's verification is reliable:

> "The reasonableness of a CRA's reinvestigation may depend on the reliability of the source it consults. Where a CRA relies on verification from a furnisher that may lack adequate documentation to confirm accuracy, the CRA may need to take additional steps."

This holding is particularly significant for cases involving debt buyer furnishers. When Midland, Portfolio Recovery, LVNV, or similar debt buyers verify disputed information, and TransUnion/Equifax/Experian know (or should know) that debt buyers often lack original documentation, accepting that verification without further inquiry may be unreasonable.

### Holding 3: Reasonableness of CRA Reinvestigation Is a Jury Question

> "Whether a CRA's reinvestigation was reasonable under § 1681i(a) is a factual question for the jury."

Consistent with the court's holding on furnisher investigation (and later reaffirmed in *Losch v. Nationstar Mortgage LLC*, 995 F.3d 937 (11th Cir. 2021)), the reasonableness of the CRA's reinvestigation is for the jury to decide. Summary judgment for the CRA is inappropriate where the adequacy of the reinvestigation is disputed.

### Holding 4: ACDV System Limitations Are Relevant to Reasonableness

The court recognized that the e-OSCAR ACDV system has inherent limitations:
- ACDVs use limited coded categories that may not convey the full specificity of the consumer's dispute
- The system is designed for speed and volume, not thoroughness
- Consumer-submitted documentation may not be forwarded (or may be forwarded in limited form)
- The system incentivizes quick verification rather than meaningful investigation

These limitations are relevant to whether reliance on the ACDV process alone constitutes a reasonable reinvestigation, particularly when the consumer has provided detailed dispute information that goes beyond what the ACDV coding can convey.

### Holding 5: CRA's Procedures Must Account for Known Weaknesses in the Furnisher System

The court implied that CRAs have a duty to account for known weaknesses in the furnisher verification system. When a CRA knows that a particular category of furnisher (e.g., debt buyers) routinely verifies based on limited records, the CRA's procedures should account for this known unreliability rather than treating all verifications as equally reliable.

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### The Independent Duty Framework

The FCRA creates SEPARATE duties for CRAs and furnishers. The CRA's duty under § 1681i(a) is independent of the furnisher's duty under § 1681s-2(b). A CRA cannot satisfy its obligation merely by delegating the investigation to the furnisher:

- **§ 1681i(a)** requires the CRA to conduct a "reinvestigation" of disputed information
- The CRA must "record the current status of the disputed information" or "delete the item"
- The reinvestigation must be completed within 30 days (or 45 days with extensions)
- The CRA must "consider all relevant information submitted by the consumer"

The court reasoned that if Congress intended the CRA's duty to be satisfied by simply forwarding the dispute to the furnisher and accepting the response, Congress would have written the statute differently. The use of "reinvestigation" — implying the CRA's own inquiry — signals an independent duty.

### The e-OSCAR Problem

The court's analysis implicitly critiques the e-OSCAR system as it is commonly used:

1. **Oversimplification:** The ACDV system reduces complex consumer disputes to limited codes. A consumer's detailed dispute letter ("I never opened this account — here is proof I was in a different state") becomes a coded ACDV that may say only "consumer disputes account — not his/her account."

2. **Information Loss:** Detailed consumer-submitted documentation (proof of payment, identity theft affidavits, account statements) may not be forwarded to the furnisher — or may be forwarded as an attachment that the furnisher's automated system does not review.

3. **Speed Over Accuracy:** The system is designed for rapid processing. Furnishers respond to ACDVs quickly (often within days), which is inconsistent with the kind of meaningful investigation that would require document review.

4. **One-Size-Fits-All:** The system treats all furnisher verifications equally — whether from a major bank with complete records or a debt buyer with only a purchased data file.

### The Cost-Benefit Analysis

Drawing on the *Cushman* framework, the court considered the cost-benefit balance:

- **Cost to the CRA of additional steps:** Minimal — requesting documentation from the furnisher, flagging debt buyer verifications for human review, actually reviewing consumer-submitted documents
- **Potential harm to the consumer:** Significant — inaccurate negative tradelines cause credit denials, higher interest rates, and reputational harm
- **When the balance tips:** When the consumer provides specific information contradicting the reported data and the furnisher verification comes from a source with known documentation limitations (debt buyers), the balance clearly favors additional CRA inquiry

### Synthesis With the Furnisher Holding

The court's opinion addresses BOTH the CRA (TransUnion) and the furnisher (Midland) in an integrated analysis. The key insight is that when BOTH entities fail:

- The furnisher (Midland) rubber-stamps its purchased data → violation of § 1681s-2(b)
- The CRA (TransUnion) accepts that rubber-stamp without question → violation of § 1681i(a)

The result is a system where inaccurate information is never corrected because neither party actually investigates. This systemic failure is exactly what the FCRA's dispute mechanism was designed to prevent.

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Hinkle v. TransUnion (CRA Holdings)

**USE THIS CASE AGAINST CRAs IN NDGA.** When suing Equifax, Experian, or TransUnion in the Northern District of Georgia for reinvestigation failures under § 1681i(a), this is your primary authority.

1. **When the CRA's reinvestigation consisted solely of sending an ACDV and accepting the response** — This is the exact conduct the court found potentially unreasonable. If the CRA sent an ACDV to the furnisher, accepted the furnisher's verification, and closed the dispute — without human review, without document review, without follow-up — cite *Hinkle*.

2. **When the furnisher is a debt buyer** — The combination of a debt buyer furnisher and a CRA that accepts the debt buyer's verification without question is the exact factual scenario in *Hinkle*. Use this when the furnisher is Midland, Portfolio Recovery, LVNV, Encore, or any debt buyer.

3. **When consumer-submitted documentation was ignored** — If your client included documentation with the dispute (proof of payment, identity theft report, account statements, letters from the original creditor) and the CRA did not meaningfully review or act on that documentation, cite *Hinkle*.

4. **When arguing that the CRA has an independent duty beyond the ACDV** — Whenever a CRA defends by saying "we sent the ACDV and the furnisher verified — what else were we supposed to do?" — *Hinkle* answers: "You have an INDEPENDENT reinvestigation duty. You cannot outsource it entirely to the furnisher."

5. **When opposing summary judgment for the CRA** — *Hinkle* (combined with *Losch*) establishes that reasonableness of reinvestigation is a jury question in the Eleventh Circuit.

### Ready-Made Pleading Language

**For § 1681i(a) Counts Against CRAs:**

> "Upon receiving Plaintiff's dispute, Defendant [TransUnion/Equifax/Experian] was obligated under 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a) to conduct a reasonable reinvestigation. Instead, Defendant merely transmitted an Automated Consumer Dispute Verification (ACDV) to the furnisher via the e-OSCAR system, accepted the furnisher's coded verification response, and closed Plaintiff's dispute without further inquiry. This is precisely the type of automated, perfunctory process the Eleventh Circuit found potentially unreasonable in *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016). The CRA has an independent reinvestigation duty — it cannot delegate that duty entirely to the furnisher through an automated system that does not facilitate meaningful investigation."

**For Cases Where Furnisher Is a Debt Buyer:**

> "Defendant [CRA] accepted verification from [Midland/Portfolio Recovery/LVNV/debt buyer] — an entity that, as a debt buyer, possesses only a purchased electronic data file without original account documentation. Defendant knew or should have known that a debt buyer's verification, based solely on purchased summary data, is inherently unreliable. Under *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016), 'the reasonableness of a CRA's reinvestigation may depend on the reliability of the source it consults.' Defendant's uncritical acceptance of a debt buyer's verification — without requesting underlying documentation, without independent investigation, and without considering the known limitations of debt buyer records — rendered Defendant's reinvestigation unreasonable."

**For Cases Where Consumer Documentation Was Ignored:**

> "Plaintiff submitted [describe documentation — payment receipts, identity theft report, account statement, etc.] with [his/her] dispute. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(4), Defendant was required to 'consider all relevant information submitted by the consumer.' Defendant failed to do so — its reinvestigation consisted solely of transmitting an ACDV to the furnisher and accepting the verification response, without meaningfully reviewing or acting upon the documentation Plaintiff provided. Under *Hinkle*, 827 F.3d at 1295, and *Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.*, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997), this failure to consider consumer-submitted information renders the reinvestigation unreasonable."

**For Opposition to CRA MSJ:**

> "Whether Defendant's reinvestigation satisfied § 1681i(a) is a question of fact for the jury. *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016); *Losch v. Nationstar Mortgage LLC*, 995 F.3d 937 (11th Cir. 2021). The evidence shows that Defendant's reinvestigation consisted of transmitting an ACDV and accepting the furnisher's response — without human review of Plaintiff's dispute, without consideration of consumer-submitted documentation, and without any steps beyond the automated e-OSCAR process. A reasonable jury could find this reinvestigation inadequate. Summary judgment must be denied."

**For § 1681e(b) Reasonable Procedures Claims:**

> "Defendant [CRA] failed to maintain reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy under 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b). Specifically, Defendant's procedures rely on the automated e-OSCAR system as the sole mechanism for reinvestigating disputes, without protocols for: (a) human review of disputes involving debt buyer furnishers; (b) requesting underlying documentation from furnishers who verify based on limited records; (c) meaningful consideration of consumer-submitted evidence; or (d) follow-up when the furnisher's response does not address the consumer's specific dispute. Under *Hinkle*, 827 F.3d 1295, such procedures are not reasonable where they result in the perpetuation of inaccurate information."

### Discovery Requests Supported by Hinkle (CRA Focus)

**Interrogatories:**

1. "Describe in detail every step Defendant took during its reinvestigation of Plaintiff's dispute(s), including: (a) whether and when an ACDV was transmitted to the furnisher; (b) the content of the ACDV (attach a copy); (c) the furnisher's response (attach a copy); (d) whether any person at Defendant reviewed the dispute beyond the automated system; (e) whether Plaintiff's submitted documentation was reviewed and by whom; and (f) what determination was made and why."

2. "Identify whether Defendant has any policy, procedure, or protocol that distinguishes between verifications received from original creditors versus verifications received from debt buyers, collection agencies, or other entities with potentially limited documentation. If so, produce those policies. If not, explain why not."

3. "State whether Defendant forwarded to the furnisher any documentation submitted by Plaintiff in connection with the dispute. If so, identify what was forwarded and in what format. If not, explain why not."

4. "Describe Defendant's policies and procedures for determining when a reinvestigation requires human review beyond the automated ACDV process, including any criteria, triggers, or escalation protocols."

5. "Identify the total time spent on Defendant's reinvestigation of Plaintiff's dispute(s), and state whether any human being reviewed the substance of Plaintiff's dispute at any point during the process."

**Requests for Production:**

1. "The complete dispute file for Plaintiff's dispute(s), including: the consumer's dispute submission, all internal notes, ACDV forms transmitted, furnisher responses received, system logs showing each step of the reinvestigation, and the final result notification sent to Plaintiff."

2. "All policies and procedures governing Defendant's reinvestigation process under 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a), including procedures for handling disputes where the furnisher is a debt buyer or collection agency."

3. "All training materials provided to reinvestigation personnel regarding: (a) when to go beyond the ACDV process; (b) how to evaluate the reliability of furnisher verifications; (c) how to handle consumer-submitted documentation; and (d) procedures for disputes involving debt buyer furnishers."

4. "Documents sufficient to show Defendant's reinvestigation metrics, including: average handling time per dispute, percentage of disputes resolved through ACDV alone versus additional steps, and escalation rates."

5. "Any communications between Defendant and [furnisher name] concerning Plaintiff's dispute(s) beyond the ACDV transmission and response."

**Requests for Admission:**

1. "Admit that Defendant's reinvestigation of Plaintiff's dispute consisted solely of transmitting an ACDV to [furnisher] and accepting [furnisher]'s verification response."

2. "Admit that no person employed by or acting on behalf of Defendant conducted any investigation of Plaintiff's dispute beyond the automated e-OSCAR ACDV process."

3. "Admit that Defendant did not request underlying account documentation from [furnisher] during its reinvestigation of Plaintiff's dispute."

4. "Admit that [furnisher] is a debt buyer that purchased Plaintiff's alleged account from [original creditor]."

5. "Admit that Defendant does not have a policy distinguishing between verifications received from original creditors and verifications received from debt buyers."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Dual Defendants — Dual Theories:** Always sue BOTH the CRA and the furnisher, as Hinkle did. The CRA's § 1681i(a) claim and the furnisher's § 1681s-2(b) claim reinforce each other. The CRA's failure to go beyond the ACDV is made worse by the furnisher's rubber-stamping — and vice versa. *Hinkle* gives you binding authority against both.

2. **Equifax Is Headquartered in NDGA:** TransUnion and Experian are the two non-Georgia CRAs, but Equifax is headquartered in Atlanta. NDGA is Equifax's home court. Judges are very familiar with FCRA cases against Equifax. *Hinkle*'s CRA holdings apply with full force to Equifax — and Equifax's reinvestigation procedures are substantially similar to TransUnion's (ACDV-based, largely automated).

3. **The "Debt Buyer Verification" Argument:** This is your most powerful argument against CRAs in NDGA debt buyer cases. When the furnisher is a debt buyer, the CRA's acceptance of that verification is particularly suspect because debt buyers notoriously lack original documentation. *Hinkle* gives you the hook: the CRA must consider the RELIABILITY of the source. A debt buyer's verification — based on a purchased data file — is inherently less reliable than an original creditor's verification based on complete records.

4. **e-OSCAR Discovery Is Critical:** The ACDV form and the furnisher's response are KEY documents. They show: (a) what information the CRA actually conveyed to the furnisher (often less than what the consumer provided); (b) how quickly the furnisher responded (often suspiciously fast); (c) whether the furnisher's response addressed the consumer's specific dispute or was a generic verification. Subpoena these from both the CRA and the furnisher — compare them for inconsistencies.

5. **30(b)(6) on Reinvestigation Process:** Depose the CRA's 30(b)(6) witness on:
   - "Walk me through exactly what you did when you received my client's dispute"
   - "Did any human being read my client's dispute letter?"
   - "What did you do with the documents my client submitted?"
   - "When the debt buyer verified, what did you do to assess whether that verification was reliable?"
   - "Do you have different procedures for debt buyer verifications vs. original creditor verifications?"
   The answers to these questions will almost always reveal a purely automated process — which is your *Hinkle* violation.

6. **Settlement Leverage:** CRA defendants know *Hinkle* well — it is binding in their home circuit (for Equifax in NDGA). They also know *Losch* makes summary judgment nearly impossible. The combination of *Hinkle* (substantive inadequacy) and *Losch* (jury question) means the case is going to trial unless settled. This creates strong settlement pressure, particularly when actual damages are well-documented.

7. **Willfulness Against CRAs Post-Hinkle:** *Hinkle* was decided in 2016. After 2016, any CRA in the Eleventh Circuit that continues to conduct ACDV-only reinvestigations — without human review, without document consideration, without reliability assessment of furnisher verifications — does so in knowing or reckless disregard of binding circuit authority. This supports statutory and punitive damages under § 1681n.

## Key Takeaway for the AI Drafter

When drafting claims against CRAs (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) in the Northern District of Georgia, *Hinkle v. TransUnion* provides binding Eleventh Circuit authority that a CRA's reinvestigation under § 1681i(a) cannot consist solely of sending an ACDV to the furnisher and accepting the verification response. The CRA has an INDEPENDENT duty to reinvestigate — and that duty is heightened when the furnisher is a debt buyer or other entity with limited documentation. Combined with the furnisher-side holding (against Midland), *Hinkle* condemns the entire system of automated, uninvestigated dispute processing at BOTH levels. This is the single most important case for NDGA FCRA practice — it provides binding authority against both CRAs and furnishers in one opinion.
