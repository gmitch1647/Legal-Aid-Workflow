# Losch v. Nationstar Mortgage LLC, 995 F.3d 937 (11th Cir. 2021)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Wayne Losch, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. Nationstar Mortgage LLC, d/b/a Mr. Cooper, Defendant-Appellee, 995 F.3d 937 (11th Cir. 2021)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit
**Date:** April 27, 2021
**Docket:** No. 19-14870
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the Southern District of Florida
**Panel:** Judges William Pryor (Chief Judge), Newsom, and Branch
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy), § 1681i(a) (reinvestigation), § 1681s-2(b) (furnisher duties upon notice of dispute), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance), § 1681n (willful noncompliance)

**BINDING AUTHORITY:** This is Eleventh Circuit precedent, directly binding on all district courts within the circuit, including the Northern District of Georgia.

---

## Facts

Wayne Losch had a mortgage serviced by Nationstar Mortgage LLC (doing business as "Mr. Cooper"). A dispute arose regarding the accuracy of information Nationstar was reporting to the consumer reporting agencies about Losch's mortgage account.

**The Reporting Dispute:** Losch contended that Nationstar was inaccurately reporting the status and/or payment history of his mortgage account to the CRAs (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion). The specific inaccuracies involved Nationstar's characterization of the account — whether payments were being made timely, whether the account was delinquent, and/or the correct balance and payment status following a servicing transfer or modification.

**Losch's Disputes:** Losch disputed the inaccurate reporting both directly with Nationstar and through the CRAs. He provided information supporting his position that the reporting was inaccurate.

**Nationstar's Response:** Nationstar conducted what it characterized as a reinvestigation but continued to report the same disputed information. The CRAs similarly conducted reinvestigations upon receiving Losch's disputes, but their investigations consisted primarily of sending ACDVs to Nationstar and accepting Nationstar's verification responses without independent analysis.

**The Reasonableness Question:** The central issue became whether the procedures employed by both the CRAs (under § 1681e(b)) and Nationstar (under § 1681s-2(b)) were "reasonable" — a determination that the district court resolved against Losch at summary judgment.

**Key Factual Disputes:** The record contained disputed facts regarding:
- What information Losch provided in his disputes
- What steps the CRAs and Nationstar took during their respective investigations
- Whether those steps were adequate given the nature and specificity of Losch's dispute
- Whether alternative, readily available procedures would have revealed the inaccuracy
- Whether the ultimate reporting was, in fact, accurate or inaccurate

---

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (S.D. Fla.):** Granted summary judgment to Nationstar on all FCRA claims, finding as a matter of law that Nationstar's procedures and investigation were reasonable
2. **Eleventh Circuit:** REVERSED and REMANDED — holding that the reasonableness of CRA and furnisher procedures under § 1681e(b) and § 1681s-2(b) is normally a question for the jury that cannot be resolved on summary judgment where material facts are disputed

---

## Holdings

### Holding 1: Reasonableness Under § 1681e(b) Is Normally a Jury Question

> "Whether a credit reporting agency maintained reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy is normally a question for trial, not summary judgment."

The Eleventh Circuit unequivocally held that the reasonableness of a CRA's procedures under § 1681e(b) is a factual question that should go to the jury in all but the most clear-cut cases. Courts may not grant summary judgment on § 1681e(b) claims merely because the defendant presents evidence of some procedures — the QUALITY and ADEQUACY of those procedures, in context, is for the factfinder.

### Holding 2: Disputed Facts Preclude Summary Judgment on Reasonableness

> "Where the facts surrounding the investigation are disputed, the question of whether that investigation was reasonable cannot be decided as a matter of law."

When there is any genuine dispute about what steps were taken, what information was available, or what the defendant knew or should have known, summary judgment is improper. The court emphasized that credibility determinations and inferences must be drawn in the plaintiff's favor at the summary judgment stage.

### Holding 3: Furnisher Investigation Reasonableness Is Similarly a Jury Question

The court extended the jury-question principle to furnisher investigations under § 1681s-2(b), consistent with *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Management, Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016). Whether a furnisher's investigation was reasonable is a factual determination the jury must make after hearing evidence about what the furnisher did and what it should have done.

### Holding 4: Context-Specific Analysis Required

The reasonableness inquiry cannot be assessed in a vacuum. Courts must consider:
- The nature and specificity of the consumer's dispute
- The information available to the defendant
- The cost and difficulty of further investigation
- The potential harm to the consumer from inaccurate reporting
- Whether the consumer provided information that should have prompted further inquiry

> "The reasonableness of a furnisher's or CRA's investigation depends on the totality of the circumstances, including the nature of the dispute and the information available."

### Holding 5: Summary Judgment Standard Must Be Rigorously Applied

The court emphasized that the non-moving party (the consumer) is entitled to all reasonable inferences at the summary judgment stage. District courts that credit the defendant's characterization of its investigation over the plaintiff's contrary evidence have applied the wrong standard.

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### The Statutory Standard

Section 1681e(b) requires CRAs to "follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy of the information" in consumer reports. Section 1681s-2(b) requires furnishers to "conduct an investigation" of disputed information upon notice from a CRA. Neither statute defines "reasonable" — that determination is inherently fact-bound.

### Why Reasonableness Cannot Be Decided on Summary Judgment

The Eleventh Circuit identified several reasons why reasonableness determinations are ill-suited for summary judgment:

**1. Reasonableness Is Inherently Factual.** What constitutes "reasonable" procedures or a "reasonable" investigation depends on the specific circumstances — the nature of the error, the information available, the cost of investigation, the harm to the consumer. These are quintessential jury questions, analogous to the reasonableness inquiry in negligence law.

**2. Credibility Issues Are Pervasive.** FCRA cases almost always involve factual disputes about what the defendant actually did during its investigation. The defendant's witness says the investigation was thorough; the plaintiff points to evidence suggesting it was perfunctory. This credibility determination belongs to the jury.

**3. The Industry Standard Problem.** Defendants frequently argue that their procedures are "reasonable" because they follow "industry standards." But industry standards may themselves be inadequate — an entire industry can be negligent. Whether industry-standard procedures satisfy the statutory requirement of "maximum possible accuracy" is a jury question.

**4. Inferences Must Favor the Non-Movant.** At summary judgment, all inferences go to the plaintiff. If a reasonable juror could look at the evidence of the defendant's investigation and conclude it was inadequate, summary judgment must be denied — even if a reasonable juror could also conclude it was adequate.

### The Eleventh Circuit's Framework

After *Losch*, the analytical framework in the Eleventh Circuit for reasonableness is:

1. **The plaintiff must show** an inaccuracy in the credit report (for § 1681e(b)) or in the furnished information (for § 1681s-2(b))
2. **The burden then shifts** to the defendant to show its procedures/investigation were reasonable
3. **Whether the defendant has met this burden** is a question for the jury unless the evidence is so one-sided that no reasonable juror could find for the plaintiff (which is rare)

### Relationship to Hinkle

*Losch* builds on *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Management*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016). While *Hinkle* established that a furnisher's rubber-stamp investigation is insufficient, *Losch* addresses the procedural question of HOW courts should evaluate these claims. Together, they establish:

- **Hinkle**: Rubber-stamping is not reasonable (substantive standard)
- **Losch**: Whether the investigation was reasonable is for the jury (procedural standard)

This one-two combination makes it extraordinarily difficult for defendants to obtain summary judgment on FCRA investigation claims in the Eleventh Circuit.

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Losch

**THIS IS YOUR PRIMARY MSJ-DEFEAT CASE IN NDGA.** Cite *Losch* every single time a CRA or furnisher moves for summary judgment in the Northern District of Georgia. It is binding circuit authority for the proposition that reasonableness goes to the jury.

1. **Opposing ANY motion for summary judgment on § 1681e(b) claims** — Whenever Equifax, Experian, or TransUnion moves for summary judgment arguing their procedures were reasonable, *Losch* is your lead case.

2. **Opposing ANY motion for summary judgment on § 1681s-2(b) claims** — Whenever a furnisher (ED Financial, Midland, Portfolio Recovery, any loan servicer) argues its investigation was reasonable as a matter of law, cite *Losch*.

3. **Opposing ANY motion for summary judgment on § 1681i(a) claims** — The same principle applies to reinvestigation claims: whether the CRA's reinvestigation was reasonable is for the jury.

4. **When the defendant argues "industry standard" procedures** — If the defense argues it followed industry standard procedures (e.g., "we used the e-OSCAR system, which is industry standard"), cite *Losch* for the principle that whether industry-standard procedures satisfy the FCRA's reasonableness requirement is a jury question.

5. **At summary judgment where ANY facts are disputed** — If there is any dispute about what the defendant actually did during its investigation — what records it reviewed, how long it spent, what information it considered — *Losch* mandates denial of summary judgment.

### Ready-Made Pleading Language

**For Response in Opposition to Motion for Summary Judgment (§ 1681e(b)):**

> "Whether Defendant maintained reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy under 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) is a question of fact for the jury. The Eleventh Circuit has squarely held that '[w]hether a credit reporting agency maintained reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy is normally a question for trial, not summary judgment.' *Losch v. Nationstar Mortgage LLC*, 995 F.3d 937, [942] (11th Cir. 2021). Here, the facts surrounding Defendant's procedures are disputed. Plaintiff has presented evidence that [describe deficiencies]. Viewing this evidence in the light most favorable to Plaintiff, a reasonable jury could find that Defendant's procedures were unreasonable. Summary judgment must be denied."

**For Response in Opposition to MSJ (§ 1681s-2(b)):**

> "Whether Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute was reasonable under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b) is a factual question for the jury. *Losch v. Nationstar Mortgage LLC*, 995 F.3d 937 (11th Cir. 2021); *Hinkle v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 827 F.3d 1295 (11th Cir. 2016). The evidence shows that Defendant's investigation consisted of [describe limited steps]. Plaintiff has presented evidence that readily available procedures — including [describe what defendant should have done] — would have revealed the inaccuracy. On this record, a reasonable jury could conclude that Defendant's investigation fell short of the statutory standard. The Court should deny Defendant's motion."

**For Response in Opposition to MSJ (General Framework):**

> "Defendant asks this Court to find, as a matter of law, that its investigation was reasonable. But the Eleventh Circuit has made clear that this determination belongs to the jury, not the Court. *Losch v. Nationstar Mortgage LLC*, 995 F.3d 937 (11th Cir. 2021). 'Where the facts surrounding the investigation are disputed, the question of whether that investigation was reasonable cannot be decided as a matter of law.' *Id.* at [943]. Here, the parties dispute [identify factual disputes]. These disputes preclude summary judgment."

**For Pretrial Motions in Limine:**

> "Defendant should not be permitted to characterize its procedures as 'industry standard' and thereby imply they are per se reasonable. Under *Losch v. Nationstar Mortgage LLC*, 995 F.3d 937 (11th Cir. 2021), whether procedures — even industry-standard ones — satisfy the FCRA's reasonableness requirement is a jury determination. The jury should be instructed that compliance with industry standards is relevant but not dispositive."

### Discovery Requests Supported by Losch

**Interrogatories:**

1. "For each consumer dispute received by Defendant relating to Plaintiff's account, describe in detail: (a) the date the dispute was received; (b) the manner in which it was received (e-OSCAR ACDV, direct dispute, or other); (c) each step taken in the investigation; (d) the identity and title of each person who participated in the investigation; (e) the amount of time spent on the investigation; (f) each document or record reviewed; and (g) the result of the investigation."

2. "Identify every written policy, procedure, or guideline that governs Defendant's investigation of consumer disputes, including any updates or revisions to those policies during the relevant time period."

3. "State whether Defendant's investigation of Plaintiff's dispute included any steps beyond (a) reviewing Defendant's internal electronic records and (b) responding to the CRA via e-OSCAR. If so, identify each additional step taken."

4. "Describe any metrics, quotas, or performance targets applied to Defendant's dispute investigation personnel, including but not limited to: average handling time, disputes resolved per day, percentage of disputes verified, and any incentives tied to investigation outcomes."

5. "Identify what information Plaintiff provided in connection with each dispute, and describe specifically how that information was used or addressed during Defendant's investigation."

**Requests for Production:**

1. "All documents comprising, reflecting, or relating to Defendant's investigation of each dispute submitted by or on behalf of Plaintiff, including internal notes, ACDV forms, communications, records reviewed, and supervisory review."

2. "Defendant's complete written policies, procedures, training materials, and guidelines governing the investigation of consumer disputes under 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2(b) or § 1681i(a), including all versions in effect during the relevant time period."

3. "Documents sufficient to show Defendant's dispute investigation metrics, including average investigation time, volume handled per analyst, verification rates, and quality assurance records for the unit that handled Plaintiff's dispute."

4. "All communications between Defendant and the consumer reporting agencies concerning Plaintiff's account, including all ACDV transmissions, responses, and any supplemental correspondence."

5. "All internal communications (including emails, instant messages, and case notes) relating to Plaintiff's account and/or disputes."

**30(b)(6) Deposition Topics (designed to prove inadequacy):**

1. "The policies and procedures governing investigation of consumer disputes during the relevant time period."
2. "The specific steps taken in investigating Plaintiff's dispute(s)."
3. "The training provided to dispute investigation personnel."
4. "The metrics, quotas, or performance targets applied to dispute investigation personnel."
5. "The quality assurance and supervisory review processes for dispute investigations."
6. "The technology systems used in dispute investigations, including the e-OSCAR system."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Losch Is Your MSJ Shield:** In every FCRA case in NDGA, you will face a motion for summary judgment from the defendant(s). *Losch* is your primary weapon for defeating that motion. Brief it prominently in every opposition.

2. **Front-Load the Citation:** Put *Losch* in the first paragraph of your opposition to MSJ. Do not bury it. The NDGA judges know this case — a prominent early citation signals that your opposition is well-founded and that the court cannot simply grant the motion.

3. **The "Industry Standard" Trap:** Defendants love arguing their procedures are reasonable because they follow "industry standards." *Losch* forecloses this argument at summary judgment. Even if the defendant proves it followed industry standards, whether those standards satisfy the FCRA is for the jury. Make this point explicitly.

4. **Create Factual Disputes in Discovery:** *Losch* tells you that if facts are disputed, summary judgment is denied. Therefore, your discovery strategy should be designed to create material factual disputes. Depose the 30(b)(6) witness extensively on what was done during the investigation. Obtain metrics showing the investigation was cursory. Get admissions that no original documents were reviewed. Each of these facts, if disputed, precludes summary judgment.

5. **Combine With Hinkle:** Use *Hinkle* for the substantive standard (what the furnisher must do) and *Losch* for the procedural protection (why the court cannot decide reasonableness at MSJ). Together, these cases give you both a sword (Hinkle's substantive standard) and a shield (Losch's jury-question rule).

6. **Pre-Motion Conference Strategy:** In NDGA, many judges require pre-motion conferences before dispositive motions. At the conference, cite *Losch* immediately. Some judges will discourage filing the MSJ once they understand that binding circuit authority makes summary judgment on reasonableness extremely difficult to obtain.

7. **Useful Even When You Might Lose on the Merits:** Even if the facts of your particular case are somewhat weak, *Losch* gives you the procedural argument that summary judgment is still improper. The question is for the jury — and the threat of a jury trial often motivates settlement.

8. **Post-Discovery Timing:** The best time to cite *Losch* is after discovery has closed and you have deposition testimony and documents showing the defendant's actual investigation procedures. The more detailed your factual record, the more material disputes you can identify, and the stronger your *Losch* argument becomes.
