# FCRA Employment and Tenant Screening  
**Topic:** Consumer reports used for employment and tenant screening — disclosure, authorization, pre-adverse action, investigative reports, public records, state-law overlay  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b); 15 U.S.C. § 1681k; 15 U.S.C. § 1681d  
**Cross-references:** Topical analysis file 08 (adverse action); Reg V (12 C.F.R. Part 1022); California ICRAA (Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1786 et seq.) and CCRAA (§§ 1785.1 et seq.); FACTA disposal rule  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
## Introduction  
  
The FCRA reaches well beyond credit underwriting: it governs the use of "consumer reports" for *employment* and *tenant screening*. Both contexts share a procedural backbone — disclosure, authorization, and pre-adverse action notice — but each has unique litigation patterns. Employment screening has been a magnet for class-action litigation under § 1681b(b)(2)'s "consists solely of" disclosure requirement, while tenant screening has driven recent CFPB and FTC enforcement focused on accuracy and reasonable procedures.  
  
## § 1681b(b) — Employment Consumer Reports  
  
### § 1681b(b)(1) — User certification to the CRA  
  
Before a CRA may furnish a consumer report for employment purposes, the CRA must obtain from the user a certification that (i) the user has complied with § 1681b(b)(2), i.e., obtained the required stand-alone disclosure and written authorization, and (ii) information from the report will not be used in violation of any applicable federal or state equal-employment-opportunity law. The certification is the CRA's first-line compliance protection; defective certifications expose the CRA itself to liability.  
  
### § 1681b(b)(2) — Stand-alone disclosure and written authorization  
  
This is the most heavily litigated FCRA provision in the employment space. Section 1681b(b)(2)(A) provides that a person may not procure a consumer report for employment purposes unless:  
  
- A clear and conspicuous disclosure has been made in writing to the consumer at any time before the report is procured, **in a document that consists solely of the disclosure**, that a consumer report may be obtained for employment purposes; and  
- The consumer has authorized in writing (which may be on the same document as the disclosure) the procurement of the report.  
  
The statutory authorization-on-the-same-document exception is the *only* permitted addition to the stand-alone disclosure.  
  
### § 1681b(b)(3) — Pre-adverse action notice  
  
Before taking adverse action based in whole or in part on a consumer report for employment purposes, the user must provide the consumer with (i) a copy of the report and (ii) a description in writing of the consumer's rights under the FCRA (the "Summary of Your Rights" prescribed by the CFPB). The interval between the pre-adverse action notice and the final adverse action must be long enough for the consumer to dispute the report — typically interpreted as five to eight business days. See *Reardon v. ClosetMaid Corp.*, No. 2:08-CV-01730 (W.D. Pa. Dec. 2, 2013) (eight days reasonable).  
  
Most courts treat § 1681b(b)(3) as carrying its own private right of action, distinct from § 1681m. *Long v. Tommy Hilfiger U.S.A., Inc.*, 671 F.3d 371 (3d Cir. 2012).  
  
## § 1681k — Public Record Information for Employment Purposes  
  
A CRA furnishing a consumer report for employment purposes that contains public-record information that is *likely to have an adverse effect* upon the consumer's ability to obtain employment must either:  
  
1. At the time the public-record information is reported to the user, also notify the consumer of the fact that the information is being reported, with the name and address of the user; **or**  
2. Maintain strict procedures to ensure that any public-record information reported is complete and up to date — meaning, for adverse items, items that are a matter of public record as of the date of the report.  
  
Most national background-screening CRAs choose option (1) — concurrent notice — to avoid the operational burden of option (2)'s "strict procedures" standard, which courts have read strictly.  
  
## § 1681d — Investigative Consumer Reports  
  
Section 1681d governs "investigative consumer reports" (defined at § 1681a(e)) — reports that contain information on a consumer's character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living, obtained through *personal interviews* with neighbors, friends, associates, or others. The user must:  
  
- Not later than three days after the date on which the report was first requested, disclose to the consumer in writing that an investigative consumer report may be made, and inform the consumer of the right to request additional disclosures and the written summary of rights;  
- On the consumer's written request within a reasonable period of time, disclose the nature and scope of the investigation.  
  
The investigative-report provisions are far less commonly invoked today (most background-check products are not "investigative" under the statutory definition) but become important when reference checks include neighbor or community interviews.  
  
## Tenant Screening as a Consumer Report  
  
Tenant screening companies (e.g., RealPage, SafeRent/CoreLogic, TransUnion Rental Screening Solutions, AppFolio) qualify as CRAs because their reports are "consumer reports" used for a permissible purpose under § 1681b(a)(3)(F)(ii) — i.e., a legitimate business need in connection with a business transaction initiated by the consumer (the rental application).  
  
Tenant screening is treated as a category of *nationwide specialty CRA* under § 1681a(w), which means consumers are entitled to a free annual disclosure under § 1681j(a) from each such CRA on request. The CFPB has identified tenant screening as a focus of supervision and enforcement under the FCRA, with particular attention to accuracy of criminal-record reporting (often the only data point) and the absence of dispute responsiveness.  
  
## Major Cases on Employment Disclosures  
  
- **Gilberg v. California Check Cashing Stores, LLC**, 913 F.3d 1169 (9th Cir. 2019). The Ninth Circuit held that including state-law-required disclosures *and* extraneous language (e.g., a statement about California Civil Code rights) in the same document as the federal FCRA disclosure violates the "consists solely of the disclosure" requirement of § 1681b(b)(2)(A). The court rejected an implied exception for state-law disclosures and held the disclosure was not clearly written.  
- **Syed v. M-I, LLC**, 853 F.3d 492 (9th Cir. 2017). Holding that inclusion of a *liability waiver* in the FCRA disclosure-and-authorization form is a willful violation under *Safeco*'s "objectively unreasonable" standard. The court reasoned that the statute permits authorization *and* nothing else; waiver is the antithesis of authorization. Syed remains the cornerstone authority for willfulness in disclosure-content cases.  
- **Walker v. Fred Meyer, Inc.**, 953 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 2020). Refining *Gilberg*: certain limited additional content (e.g., a brief explanation of what a consumer report is and how the report will be used) may survive scrutiny, but extensive content beyond the federal disclosure does not.  
- **Reardon v. ClosetMaid Corp.**, No. 2:08-CV-01730, 2013 WL 6231606 (W.D. Pa. Dec. 2, 2013). Cited for both stand-alone disclosure violations and pre-adverse action timing. [VERIFY: ClosetMaid has multiple opinions in the docket; the operative ruling on the stand-alone-disclosure claim is the principal Western District of Pennsylvania decision.]  
- **Long v. Tommy Hilfiger U.S.A., Inc.**, 671 F.3d 371 (3d Cir. 2012). Recognizing § 1681b(b)(3) as a freestanding cause of action requiring a copy of the report and Summary of Rights *before* adverse action.  
  
## State-Law Overlay (California Example)  
  
California presents an especially active overlay through two statutes:  
  
- **Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (ICRAA)**, Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1786 et seq. — governs investigative consumer reports prepared in California or about California residents; the California Supreme Court in *Connor v. First Student, Inc.*, 5 Cal. 5th 1026 (2018), held ICRAA constitutional and applicable to employment background checks notwithstanding the parallel CCRAA.  
- **Consumer Credit Reporting Agencies Act (CCRAA)**, Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1785.1 et seq. — governs consumer credit reports.  
  
Both impose disclosure-and-authorization requirements that *cannot* be merged with the FCRA disclosure after *Gilberg*. Standard practice in California is to provide three separate documents: the federal FCRA disclosure (alone), the state ICRAA/CCRAA disclosure(s), and a separate authorization (which can lawfully be on the federal disclosure form).  
  
"Ban-the-box" laws (in California, the Fair Chance Act, Cal. Gov't Code § 12952; many other states and localities) further restrict the *timing* and *content* of criminal-record questioning. They overlay the FCRA's procedural rules but do not displace them: an employer that complies with FCRA pre-adverse action timing can still violate state ban-the-box requirements if the inquiry occurs too early in the process.  
  
## Common Employment-Screening Violations  
  
The class-action bar identifies these recurring violation patterns:  
  
1. **Combined disclosure-and-authorization with extra content** — the most common violation post-*Syed* and *Gilberg*. Typical additions that violate the stand-alone rule: state-law disclosures, liability waivers, at-will-employment statements, EEO statements, "I certify the information above is true" clauses, references to drug testing.  
2. **Failure to provide pre-adverse action notice at all** — particularly common in high-volume hourly hiring.  
3. **Insufficient interval between pre-adverse action notice and final adverse action** — sending both letters within one or two days, or sending them simultaneously.  
4. **Missing Summary of Rights** — providing the report but not the CFPB-prescribed Summary.  
5. **Use of "blanket" disclosures across an enterprise** that include subsidiary names or generic "and its agents" language — courts have split on whether this satisfies "clear and conspicuous."  
6. **Failure to obtain investigative-report disclosure** under § 1681d when reference checks involve personal interviews.  
  
## Tenant-Screening Enforcement  
  
CFPB and FTC supervisory and enforcement work has spotlighted tenant screening:  
  
- **FTC v. RealPage, Inc.** (2018) — $3 million civil penalty settlement for FCRA violations: RealPage's automated matching of criminal records to applicants used loose match criteria (last-name match alone in some cases), resulting in inaccurate adverse information. RealPage was required to implement enhanced procedures under § 1681e(b)'s reasonable-procedures standard.  
- **Connecticut Fair Housing Center v. CoreLogic Rental Property Solutions, LLC** — fair-housing case alleging the CrimeSAFE tenant-screening tool produced racially disparate outcomes. Demonstrates the overlay of the FCRA with the Fair Housing Act.  
- **CFPB Tenant Background Check Consumer Snapshot (November 2022)** — identifying RealPage, AppFolio, TransUnion, CoreLogic/SafeRent as among the most-complained-about screeners, with consumers reporting that the screening companies do not respond meaningfully to disputes.  
- **CFPB Advisory Opinions (January 2024)** — clarifying FCRA accuracy obligations on background-screening CRAs, including criminal-record matching, and consumer file-disclosure obligations.  
  
## Practical Litigation and Compliance Notes  
  
### For employers  
  
1. **Use a one-page, federal-only disclosure.** No state language. No waivers. No EEO statements. No at-will clauses.  
2. **Use a separate authorization** (may be on the same document as the disclosure, but most counsel still recommend separate forms post-*Gilberg* to avoid argument).  
3. **Provide ICRAA/CCRAA disclosures on separate documents** for California applicants.  
4. **Two-letter pre-adverse-action workflow** with a documented waiting period of at least five business days.  
5. **Train recruiters** on ban-the-box timing — don't even *ask* about criminal history at the wrong stage.  
6. **Audit the disclosure form annually**; case law continues to evolve and many forms in circulation include obsolete language.  
  
### For tenant-screening defendants (CRAs)  
  
1. **Reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy** under § 1681e(b) — the central battleground in *RealPage*-style cases. Use middle-name and date-of-birth matching, not last-name alone.  
2. **Robust dispute response** under § 1681i — automation that pings furnishers without independent investigation is a vulnerability.  
3. **§ 1681k notice or strict procedures** for public records used adversely.  
  
### For plaintiffs' counsel  
  
1. Class certification of stand-alone-disclosure claims has been routine post-*Gilberg* and *Syed*, particularly in the Ninth Circuit.  
2. Standing post-*TransUnion v. Ramirez*: plead concrete harm flowing from the procedural defect (lost job, lost housing, emotional harm, time spent disputing).  
3. Investigate the disclosure form via FOIA from state employment agencies or by employer subpoena.
