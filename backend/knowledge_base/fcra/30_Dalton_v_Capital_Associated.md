# Dalton v. Capital Associated Industries, Inc., 257 F.3d 409 (4th Cir. 2001)

## Case Information

**Full Citation:** Brenda Dalton, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. Capital Associated Industries, Inc., Defendant-Appellee, 257 F.3d 409 (4th Cir. 2001)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit
**Date:** July 17, 2001
**Case No:** 00-1708
**Appeal From:** United States District Court for the Middle District of North Carolina
**Panel:** Judges Wilkinson, Michael, and King
**Statutes Interpreted:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) (reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy), § 1681n (willful noncompliance), § 1681o (negligent noncompliance)

---

## Facts

Brenda Dalton applied for employment with a company that used Capital Associated Industries, Inc. ("CAI") as its employment screening service. CAI was a consumer reporting agency under the FCRA that provided background check reports to employers.

**The Inaccuracy:**

CAI's background check report on Dalton contained a significant inaccuracy — it reported incorrect criminal record information that did not belong to Dalton. The erroneous information was attributable to a failure in CAI's procedures for matching criminal records to the correct consumer. Specifically, CAI's system matched records based on insufficient identifying criteria, leading to a "mixed file" or misattribution of another person's criminal record to Dalton.

**Consumer's Notice to CAI:**

After learning of the inaccuracy (through the adverse action process), Dalton notified CAI that the criminal record information was not hers. She disputed the accuracy of the report and provided information to CAI sufficient to identify the error.

**CAI's Response:**

Despite receiving clear notice from Dalton that its report contained inaccurate information belonging to a different person, CAI failed to adequately correct the error and/or failed to implement procedures that would prevent the same type of misattribution from occurring. The evidence showed that CAI's matching procedures were known to be imprecise and that the company had been put on notice (either by Dalton specifically or through prior similar errors) that its system generated false matches.

**Impact:**

The inaccurate criminal record information in Dalton's employment background check had devastating consequences — it resulted in loss of employment opportunity. Criminal record misattribution in employment screening is among the most harmful types of CRA errors because it directly affects a person's ability to earn a living and carries social stigma.

---

## Procedural History

1. **District Court (M.D.N.C.):** Granted summary judgment to Capital Associated Industries on the willfulness claim under § 1681n. The district court concluded that a single inaccuracy, without more, could not support a finding of willful noncompliance as a matter of law. The court may have allowed the negligence claim (§ 1681o) to proceed but eliminated the possibility of punitive damages.

2. **Fourth Circuit:** REVERSED — holding that a single inaccuracy CAN support a finding of willful noncompliance under § 1681n when the CRA had clear notice of the deficiency in its procedures or of the specific inaccuracy, and failed to correct it. The court held this was a question for the jury.

---

## Holdings

### Holding 1: A Single Inaccuracy Can Support a Willfulness Finding

> "A consumer need not demonstrate a pattern of inaccuracies to establish willfulness under § 1681n. A single instance of reporting inaccurate information may support a finding of willful noncompliance if the consumer reporting agency had clear notice that the information was inaccurate or that its procedures for ensuring accuracy were inadequate."

**Translation:** Defense counsel's favorite argument — "it was just one mistake, that can't be willful" — is wrong as a matter of law. One mistake IS enough for willfulness if the CRA knew (or should have known) the information was wrong. The question is not how many errors occurred but whether the CRA acted with knowledge or reckless disregard.

### Holding 2: Clear Notice + Failure to Correct = Willfulness

> "When a consumer reporting agency has been put on clear notice that information in a consumer's file is inaccurate — whether through the consumer's own dispute, through prior similar errors, or through knowledge that its matching procedures are unreliable — and the agency fails to take corrective action, a jury may find that the agency acted willfully."

The court established that "clear notice" can come from multiple sources:
- The consumer's direct dispute
- Prior complaints about the same type of error
- Knowledge that the CRA's underlying system/procedure is prone to generating inaccuracies
- Industry standards that the CRA's procedures fail to meet

### Holding 3: Willfulness Is a Jury Question

> "Whether a consumer reporting agency's conduct rises to the level of willful noncompliance is a question of fact for the jury. Summary judgment for the defendant on the willfulness issue is inappropriate when the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, would permit a reasonable jury to find that the agency knew or recklessly disregarded its obligations under the statute."

**Translation:** The CRA cannot get willfulness dismissed at summary judgment merely by arguing "we didn't mean to." If the plaintiff presents evidence of notice + failure to correct, the jury decides willfulness.

### Holding 4: Procedures Known to Be Deficient Support Willfulness

> "Evidence that a consumer reporting agency maintained procedures that it knew or should have known were inadequate to assure maximum possible accuracy — and continued to use those procedures without correction — supports an inference of willful noncompliance."

This holding is significant because it allows willfulness to be established not just through evidence about the specific plaintiff's file, but through systemic evidence that the CRA's matching procedures were known to be unreliable. The CRA's conscious decision to continue using a flawed system, despite knowledge of its deficiencies, constitutes reckless disregard.

### Holding 5: Lower Willfulness Bar Than Pattern Requirement

The court explicitly rejected any requirement that a plaintiff demonstrate a "pattern or practice" of violations to establish willfulness:

> "Section 1681n does not require a pattern of violations. It provides that '[a]ny person who willfully fails to comply with any requirement imposed under this subchapter with respect to any consumer is liable to that consumer.' The focus is on the defendant's state of mind regarding the specific violation at issue, not on the number of times the violation has occurred."

## Complete Legal Reasoning

### Textual Analysis of § 1681n

The Fourth Circuit began with the text of § 1681n(a), which provides liability for "any person who willfully fails to comply with any requirement imposed under this subchapter with respect to any consumer." The court emphasized:

- "Any" requirement — no exclusion for § 1681e(b) accuracy requirements
- "With respect to any consumer" — singular; the statute contemplates liability for a violation affecting one consumer
- No textual requirement of multiple violations or a pattern

The court concluded that the plain text of § 1681n makes willful noncompliance possible based on a single violation, as long as the "willful" mental state is satisfied.

### The Willfulness Standard

Applying the willfulness framework (later confirmed by the Supreme Court in *Safeco*), the Fourth Circuit identified the relevant inquiry: Did the CRA know that its conduct violated the FCRA, or did it act in reckless disregard of whether its conduct complied?

Evidence supporting willfulness in the single-inaccuracy context includes:

1. **Consumer's dispute as notice:** When a consumer disputes information and provides evidence of inaccuracy, the CRA is on notice. Continuing to report the information after this notice — without meaningful corrective action — demonstrates at minimum reckless disregard.

2. **Knowledge of system deficiencies:** If the CRA knows its matching algorithms or data collection methods are imprecise (through prior complaints, internal audits, or employee knowledge), continuing to use those methods without improvement shows reckless disregard for accuracy.

3. **Industry standards:** If the CRA's procedures fall below what other CRAs in the industry employ for the same type of data, this supports an inference that the CRA knew its procedures were inadequate.

4. **Cost of correction vs. potential harm:** If correcting the deficiency would be inexpensive relative to the potential harm to consumers, the CRA's failure to correct suggests it knowingly chose to accept inaccurate results.

### Distinguishing Negligence from Willfulness

The court drew the line between negligent and willful noncompliance:

- **Negligence (§ 1681o):** The CRA failed to exercise reasonable care — it should have caught the error but didn't. This might be a one-time procedural breakdown without notice.
- **Willfulness (§ 1681n):** The CRA had notice that its procedures were flawed or that specific information was inaccurate, and it proceeded anyway. The key differentiator is KNOWLEDGE (actual or constructive) plus FAILURE TO ACT.

The consumer's dispute provides the critical pivot point: before the dispute, the CRA may have merely been negligent. AFTER the dispute (which puts the CRA on clear notice), continued reporting transforms negligence into willfulness.

### Policy Considerations

The court recognized that requiring a "pattern" for willfulness would create perverse incentives:

- CRAs could knowingly report inaccurate information about individual consumers with impunity, so long as they corrected it before the same consumer filed multiple complaints
- The punitive damages provision of § 1681n would be rendered largely meaningless if limited to situations involving repeated violations
- Congress intended § 1681n to deter CRAs from maintaining known-deficient procedures, and that deterrence function requires liability even for individual instances of willful disregard

## Application to LegalFlow Cases

### When to Cite Dalton v. Capital Associated Industries

Cite *Dalton* in any case where:

1. **Defense argues "one mistake isn't willful"** — This is the #1 use of *Dalton*. Defense counsel in CRA and furnisher cases invariably argue that a single error cannot constitute willfulness. *Dalton* squarely rejects this argument.

2. **Client's dispute put the CRA on notice** — If your client disputed the inaccuracy and the CRA failed to correct after being put on notice, cite *Dalton* for the proposition that clear notice + failure to correct can establish willfulness even for one error.

3. **Opposing summary judgment on willfulness** — When the defense moves for summary judgment arguing insufficient evidence of willfulness because there was only one inaccuracy, *Dalton* is your primary authority for keeping the willfulness claim alive for the jury.

4. **Supporting punitive damages claims** — *Dalton* establishes that punitive damages (available only for willful violations under § 1681n) do not require showing a pattern — they are available for a single willful violation.

5. **Mixed file / misattribution cases** — *Dalton* involved criminal record misattribution. If your client has a mixed file (information from another consumer erroneously merged with their file), *Dalton* is directly factually analogous.

6. **Employment screening errors** — *Dalton* involved an employment background check CRA. For cases involving inaccurate background checks, cite *Dalton* for both the accuracy duty and the willfulness standard.

### Pleading Language

**For willfulness based on single inaccuracy with notice:**

> "Defendant's violation of 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(b) was willful within the meaning of § 1681n. A single instance of reporting inaccurate information supports willful noncompliance when the CRA has clear notice of the inaccuracy. See Dalton v. Capital Associated Indus., Inc., 257 F.3d 409, 415 (4th Cir. 2001) ('A consumer need not demonstrate a pattern of inaccuracies to establish willfulness under § 1681n.'). Here, Plaintiff provided clear notice to Defendant that the [TRADELINE/INFORMATION] was inaccurate by submitting a written dispute on [DATE] that specifically identified the error and provided [DOCUMENTATION/EXPLANATION]. Despite this clear notice, Defendant failed to correct the inaccuracy and continued to report false information, demonstrating willful noncompliance or, at minimum, reckless disregard for Plaintiff's rights under the FCRA."

**For opposing summary judgment on willfulness:**

> "Defendant's motion for summary judgment on Plaintiff's § 1681n willfulness claim must be denied. The Fourth Circuit has expressly held that 'a single instance of reporting inaccurate information may support a finding of willful noncompliance if the consumer reporting agency had clear notice that the information was inaccurate.' Dalton v. Capital Associated Indus., Inc., 257 F.3d 409, 415 (4th Cir. 2001). Here, Plaintiff's [NUMBER] dispute letter(s) provided clear notice that [SPECIFIC INFORMATION] was inaccurate. Defendant's continued reporting of this information after receiving such notice creates a genuine dispute of material fact as to willfulness that must be resolved by the jury."

**For rebutting the "pattern" argument:**

> "Defendant incorrectly argues that Plaintiff must demonstrate a 'pattern' of violations to establish willfulness. This argument has been squarely rejected. Section 1681n 'does not require a pattern of violations.' Dalton v. Capital Associated Indus., Inc., 257 F.3d 409, 415 (4th Cir. 2001). 'The focus is on the defendant's state of mind regarding the specific violation at issue, not on the number of times the violation has occurred.' Id. The evidence here establishes that Defendant had clear notice of the inaccuracy and failed to correct it — that is sufficient for a jury to find willfulness regardless of whether Defendant made similar errors with respect to other consumers."

**For system-wide deficiency arguments:**

> "In addition to the notice provided by Plaintiff's individual dispute, the evidence demonstrates that Defendant maintained [matching/reporting/investigation] procedures that it knew or should have known were inadequate to assure maximum possible accuracy. See Dalton, 257 F.3d at 416 ('Evidence that a consumer reporting agency maintained procedures that it knew or should have known were inadequate... supports an inference of willful noncompliance.'). [DESCRIBE SPECIFIC PROCEDURAL DEFICIENCY]. Defendant's conscious decision to continue employing these inadequate procedures despite knowledge of their deficiencies constitutes reckless disregard for the accuracy requirements of § 1681e(b)."

For each, state the date received, the nature of the complaint, and the resolution."

2. "Describe all quality assurance or audit procedures Defendant employs to verify the accuracy of information before including it in consumer reports, and identify any audits, reviews, or analyses that identified deficiencies in those procedures during the period [DATE] through [DATE]."

3. "State whether Defendant has received any regulatory complaints, examination findings, or enforcement actions regarding the accuracy of its consumer reporting during the period [DATE] through [DATE], and if so, describe each."

4. "Identify all instances in which Defendant corrected or deleted the same type of inaccuracy at issue in this case after receiving consumer disputes during the period [DATE] through [DATE]. State the total number of such corrections/deletions and the total number of disputes received regarding this type of issue."

**Requests for Production:**

5. "Produce all internal audits, quality assurance reviews, accuracy studies, or error rate analyses conducted by Defendant regarding its reporting accuracy during the period [DATE] through [DATE]."

6. "Produce all employee complaints, suggestions, or internal communications identifying deficiencies in Defendant's procedures for ensuring accuracy, including any 'lessons learned' documents, post-incident reviews, or process improvement recommendations."

7. "Produce training materials provided to employees responsible for handling consumer disputes, investigating accuracy issues, or maintaining reporting systems."

8. "Produce all documents reflecting prior disputes from other consumers regarding the same type of error at issue in this case, sufficient to show that Defendant was on notice that its procedures generated this type of inaccuracy."

9. "Produce any correspondence with regulators (CFPB, FTC, state attorneys general) regarding Defendant's accuracy rates, dispute handling procedures, or systemic reporting errors."

10. "Produce the complete dispute file for Plaintiff, including all internal notes, communications, records reviewed, and decisions made during Defendant's handling of Plaintiff's dispute(s)."

**30(b)(6) Deposition Topics:**

11. "Defendant's knowledge, prior to receiving Plaintiff's dispute, that its [matching/reporting/data processing] procedures could result in the type of inaccuracy at issue in this case."

12. "Prior complaints, disputes, or lawsuits involving the same type of inaccuracy as alleged by Plaintiff."

13. "Defendant's error rates for the type of reporting at issue, and any benchmarks or goals established for accuracy."

14. "Cost estimates for implementing procedures that would prevent or reduce the type of inaccuracy at issue versus the harm such inaccuracies cause to consumers."

### Strategic Notes for NDGA Practice

1. **Timing of the dispute is critical:** *Dalton*'s power comes from the "clear notice + failure to correct" framework. The consumer's dispute IS the clear notice. This means: (a) always have the client dispute BEFORE filing suit, (b) make the dispute specific and clear, (c) document everything, and (d) allow the CRA/furnisher time to respond. If they fail to correct after receiving clear notice, you have your willfulness evidence.

2. **The "one mistake" defense is dead:** In briefing, when defense counsel argues "this was a one-time error, not willful," quote *Dalton* at 415 directly. This argument fails as a matter of law. The question is not "how many mistakes" but "did the defendant have notice and fail to act?"

3. **Build the notice record:** Send multiple dispute letters with increasing specificity. Each letter after the first that goes unanswered strengthens the "clear notice" evidence. While *Dalton* says even one dispute suffices for notice, multiple disputes make the case even stronger (and overlap with *Cortez*'s pattern theory).

4. **System-wide discovery is key:** *Dalton* permits willfulness based on knowledge of systemic procedural deficiency. This opens the door to broad discovery about the CRA's error rates, prior complaints about the same type of error, and internal knowledge of system limitations. Request error rate data and prior similar complaints — this evidence is admissible on willfulness.

5. **Combine with Safeco:** After the Supreme Court's 2007 *Safeco* decision defining willfulness to include "reckless disregard," *Dalton*'s holding is even stronger. Reckless disregard exists when the CRA's interpretation of its obligations is "objectively unreasonable." Receiving clear notice that information is wrong and continuing to report it is objectively unreasonable — no reasonable CRA could conclude that ignoring a meritorious dispute complies with § 1681e(b) or § 1681i(a).

6. **NDGA application — Equifax headquarters:** Equifax is headquartered in Atlanta (NDGA). Cases against Equifax in NDGA are common. *Dalton* (4th Circuit) is persuasive authority (not binding), but its reasoning is highly applicable and has been cited favorably by courts nationwide. Pair with 11th Circuit authorities on willfulness.

7. **Employment screening cases in NDGA:** Many NDGA clients face employment denials due to inaccurate background checks. *Dalton* is directly on point for these cases because it involved an employment screening CRA. The same framework applies to companies like HireRight, Sterling, Checkr, and First Advantage.

8. **Damages implications:** By preserving the willfulness claim for the jury, *Dalton* keeps punitive damages alive. This transforms the case from a "low-value negligence case" (capped at actual damages, which may be modest) into a potentially significant punitive damages case. Use this leverage in settlement negotiations.

9. **Jury instruction on willfulness:** At trial, request a jury instruction that specifically states: "You may find willfulness based on a single violation if you find that the defendant had clear notice of the inaccuracy and failed to correct it. A pattern of violations is not required." Cite *Dalton* as authority.

10. **Defense expert challenges:** Defense may try to offer expert testimony that "industry standard" is to resolve disputes quickly and that minor errors are inevitable. Counter with *Dalton*'s holding that knowledge of procedural inadequacy — regardless of industry practice — supports willfulness. Industry practice that falls below statutory requirements does not insulate the CRA.

## Related Cases

- **Safeco Ins. Co. of Am. v. Burr**, 551 U.S. 47 (2007) — Supreme Court willfulness standard: includes reckless disregard (objectively unreasonable interpretation of statute)
- **Cortez v. Trans Union, LLC**, 617 F.3d 688 (3d Cir. 2010) — Pattern of ignoring disputes supports willfulness; $1.8M punitive damages upheld
- **Cushman v. Trans Union Corp.**, 115 F.3d 220 (3d Cir. 1997) — Perfunctory reinvestigation may be willful when CRA prioritizes cost savings
- **Philbin v. Trans Union Corp.**, 101 F.3d 957 (3d Cir. 1996) — Willfulness and CRA procedures
- **Smith v. HireRight Solutions, Inc.**, No. 1:09-cv-625 (N.D. Ga. 2010) — NDGA employment screening FCRA case

- **Cahlin v. General Motors Acceptance Corp.**, 936 F.2d 1151 (11th Cir. 1991) — 11th Circuit FCRA willfulness analysis

## Key Takeaway for Practice

*Dalton v. Capital Associated Industries* destroys the most common defense argument in FCRA litigation: "it was only one mistake — that can't be willful." The Fourth Circuit's clear holding that a single inaccuracy CAN support willfulness (when the CRA had notice and failed to correct) should be quoted in every brief opposing summary judgment on willfulness. For NDGA practice, *Dalton* is persuasive authority that aligns with the *Safeco* willfulness framework and pairs powerfully with *Cortez* (multiple disputes) and 11th Circuit precedent. Use *Dalton* to keep punitive damages alive, leverage the case in settlement negotiations, and remind defense counsel that the "one mistake" argument will not carry the day.
