# FCRA Identity Theft Protections  
**Topic:** Identity theft prevention, fraud alerts, security freezes, blocking, furnisher and CRA duties, Red Flags Rule, disposal  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1681a(q), 1681c-1, 1681c-2, 1681j(d), 1681s-2(a)(6)–(7), 1681w  
**Cross-references:** Reg V Subpart J (Red Flags Rule, 16 C.F.R. Part 681 / 12 C.F.R. Part 1022 Subpart J); Reg V Subpart M (12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.120–.124); FACTA Disposal Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 682); EGRRCPA (2018, Pub. L. 115-174)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
## Introduction  
  
The FCRA's identity-theft regime was largely added by the Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act (FACTA) of 2003 and substantially expanded by the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act (EGRRCPA) of 2018. The system has four interlocking layers: (1) alert mechanisms on the consumer's CRA file (fraud alerts, security freezes), (2) blocking of fraudulent tradelines once an identity-theft report exists, (3) duties on furnishers and CRAs to prevent and respond to identity theft, and (4) prophylactic risk-management programs at financial institutions and creditors (the Red Flags Rule).  
  
## Definition: § 1681a(q) — "Identity theft" and "identity theft report"  
  
Section 1681a(q) defines "identity theft" by reference to rules of the FTC (now CFPB for many institutions); Reg V defines it as "a fraud committed using the identifying information of another person, subject to such further definition as the Bureau may prescribe." An "identity theft report" is a report alleging an identity theft that (i) is a copy of an official, valid report filed by a consumer with a federal, state, or local law-enforcement agency, the filing of which subjects the person filing the report to criminal penalties relating to filing false information; and (ii) may include additional information or documentation that an information furnisher or CRA reasonably requests for the purpose of determining the validity of the report. The FTC's IdentityTheft.gov platform produces an FTC Identity Theft Report that, combined with a police-report filing, satisfies the requirement.  
  
## § 1681c-1 — Fraud Alerts and Active Duty Alerts  
  
### Initial fraud alert (§ 1681c-1(a))  
  
A consumer who asserts in good faith a suspicion that he or she has been or is about to become a victim of fraud or identity theft may request a CRA to include an initial fraud alert in the consumer's file. Under EGRRCPA, the duration was extended from 90 days to **one year**. The alert requires anyone who later receives the file to take reasonable steps to verify the consumer's identity before extending new credit. CRAs must refer the alert to the other nationwide CRAs (one-call rule) and provide the consumer with a free credit-report disclosure on request triggered by the alert.  
  
### Extended fraud alert (§ 1681c-1(b))  
  
If the consumer submits an *identity theft report* to the CRA, the consumer is entitled to an **extended fraud alert** of **seven years** plus additional protections: (i) the consumer is automatically opted out of prescreened credit and insurance offers for **five years** under § 1681c-1(e), and (ii) the consumer is entitled to **two free file disclosures** within twelve months. As with the initial alert, anyone using the file must take reasonable steps to verify identity before extending credit.  
  
### Active duty alert (§ 1681c-1(c))  
  
A member of the U.S. military on active duty stationed away from a usual duty station may request an active duty alert of **one year**. The alert requires identity verification like a fraud alert and excludes the consumer from prescreened lists for two years.  
  
### Security freeze (§ 1681c-1(i), added by EGRRCPA 2018)  
  
EGRRCPA created a uniform federal security-freeze regime. Key features:  
  
- **Free of charge** to place, temporarily lift, or remove — for the consumer, the consumer's protected child, and incapacitated persons.  
- A CRA must place the freeze within **one business day** of receiving an electronic or telephone request (within three business days for a written request).  
- A CRA must lift the freeze within **one hour** of an electronic or telephone request; within three business days for a written request.  
- Effect: while a freeze is in place, the CRA may not release the consumer's report to a third party for new credit purposes (with statutory exceptions, e.g., for existing creditors, child-support agencies, government use).  
- Preempts inconsistent state freeze regimes only to the extent of the inconsistency — § 1681t(b) controls.  
  
EGRRCPA also added a separate regime for protected consumers (minors and incapacitated adults) at § 1681c-1(j).  
  
## § 1681c-2 — Block of Information Resulting from Identity Theft  
  
Section 1681c-2 is the FCRA's core remedial tool for identity-theft victims. A CRA *shall block* the reporting of information that the consumer identifies as resulting from an alleged identity theft, **not later than four business days** after receipt of (i) appropriate proof of the consumer's identity, (ii) a copy of an identity theft report, (iii) the consumer's identification of the information, and (iv) a statement by the consumer that the information is not information relating to any transaction by the consumer.  
  
The CRA must promptly notify the furnisher that the information may be a result of identity theft, that an identity theft report has been filed, that a block has been requested, and of the effective date of the block. The CRA may decline or rescind the block on limited grounds in § 1681c-2(c): material misrepresentation of fact, error by the CRA, or the consumer's having obtained possession of goods/services as a result of the blocked transaction.  
  
The block obligation is distinct from the dispute regime of § 1681i and is a more powerful remedy for victims: once the documentation requirements are met, the CRA must block within four business days regardless of the furnisher's response.  
  
## § 1681s-2(a)(6) and (a)(7) — Furnisher Duties on Identity Theft  
  
Furnishers have parallel duties:  
  
- **§ 1681s-2(a)(6)** — Once a furnisher receives notice from a CRA under § 1681c-2 that information may be the result of identity theft, the furnisher must have reasonable procedures to respond and may not refurnish the blocked information.  
- **§ 1681s-2(a)(7)** — Negative information notice (operates with § 1681m(g)) — financial institutions that furnish negative information to a nationwide CRA must provide the consumer with a clear and conspicuous one-time notice in writing.  
  
Reg V's furnisher rule (12 C.F.R. § 1022.40 et seq.) supplements these statutory duties.  
  
Note on private enforcement: § 1681s-2(c) and (d) preserve private enforcement of § 1681s-2(b) (post-dispute duties) but not of § 1681s-2(a) (initial accuracy duties). However, the identity-theft furnisher duties at § 1681s-2(a)(6)–(7) are functionally enforced through CRA-side blocking under § 1681c-2 and through CFPB/FTC enforcement.  
  
## § 1681j(d) — Free Disclosures for Identity Theft Victims  
  
Section 1681j(d) entitles a consumer who has placed a fraud alert under § 1681c-1 to a free file disclosure: one in connection with an initial or active-duty alert, two within twelve months in connection with an extended fraud alert. This sits alongside the general free-annual disclosure of § 1681j(a) (one per year per nationwide CRA, requested through AnnualCreditReport.com), and the 60-day post-adverse-action free disclosure at § 1681j(b).  
  
## § 1681w — Disposal of Records (FACTA Disposal Rule)  
  
Section 1681w requires any person who maintains or otherwise possesses consumer information for a business purpose to properly dispose of it. The implementing rule is the FTC's Disposal Rule, 16 C.F.R. Part 682, which requires "reasonable measures" — typically burning, pulverizing, or shredding paper, and securely destroying or erasing electronic media. Failure to dispose properly is enforceable by the FTC, state AGs, and other agencies, and supports state-law negligence claims when breached personal information leads to identity theft.  
  
## Reg V Subpart J — The Red Flags Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 681; 12 C.F.R. Part 1022 Subpart J)  
  
The Red Flags Rule, mandated by FACTA at § 1681m(e) and jointly issued by the FTC, the federal banking agencies, and the NCUA (with CFPB now responsible for many entities), requires every "financial institution" and "creditor" that offers or maintains a "covered account" to develop and implement a written **Identity Theft Prevention Program** designed to detect, prevent, and mitigate identity theft in connection with the opening or maintenance of covered accounts.  
  
The Program must include reasonable policies and procedures to:  
  
1. Identify relevant "red flags" — patterns, practices, or specific activities indicating possible identity theft;  
2. Detect those red flags in operations;  
3. Respond appropriately to red flags detected, to prevent and mitigate identity theft; and  
4. Update the program periodically to reflect changes in risk.  
  
Implementation must involve board approval and reporting, staff training, and oversight of service-provider arrangements. Appendix A to 16 C.F.R. Part 681 provides interagency guidelines and an illustrative list of red flags (e.g., alerts from a CRA, suspicious documents, suspicious personally identifying information).  
  
Coverage of "creditors" was narrowed by the Red Flag Program Clarification Act of 2010 (Pub. L. 111-319), excluding many trade-credit and professional-service contexts; the rule now reaches creditors that regularly and in the ordinary course of business obtain or use consumer reports in connection with credit transactions, furnish information to CRAs, or advance funds based on an obligation to repay.  
  
## Reg V Subpart M (12 C.F.R. §§ 1022.120–.124) — CRA Duties Regarding Identity Theft  
  
Subpart M implements CRA-side duties:  
  
- **§ 1022.121** — Procedures for handling and content of fraud and active-duty alerts (one-call rule, sharing among nationwide CRAs, content standards).  
- **§ 1022.122** — Information available to victims (account information for fraudulent transactions, on identity-theft-report request).  
- **§ 1022.123** — Notice of negative information (model B-1, B-2 forms).  
- **§ 1022.124** — Summary of consumer rights, including the **Summary of Rights for Identity Theft Victims** (Appendix I to Part 1022), required to be provided to consumers who self-identify as identity theft victims.  
  
## Address Discrepancy and Truncation Rules  
  
- **§ 1681c(g)** — Truncation of credit and debit card numbers on electronically generated receipts (no more than the last five digits, no expiration date).  
- **§ 1681c(h)** and Reg V § 1022.82 — Address discrepancy: a user that receives a notice of address discrepancy from a nationwide CRA must employ reasonable policies and procedures to confirm the consumer's address and reconcile the discrepancy.  
  
## Major Enforcement and Litigation Touchpoints  
  
- **TransUnion (CFPB consent order, 2017)** — TransUnion was sanctioned for deceptive marketing of credit-related products including identity-theft monitoring. The order required restitution and operational changes. [VERIFY exact year and citation; consent orders have been multiple over time.]  
- **Equifax 2017 breach** — The 2017 disclosure of a breach affecting ~147 million consumers led to a multi-agency settlement (FTC, CFPB, 50 states) of up to $700 million in 2019, free credit monitoring, and was the political catalyst for the EGRRCPA security-freeze amendments in 2018.  
- **FTC IdentityTheft.gov** — Designated by the FTC as the official ID-theft reporting platform; the FTC's report-generation flow produces a document accepted by CRAs as an "identity theft report" under § 1681a(q) when combined with (or in lieu of, depending on CRA practice) a police report.  
  
## Practical Litigation and Compliance Notes  
  
1. **Order of operations for a victim**: (i) file an FTC report at IdentityTheft.gov and a police report, (ii) place an initial fraud alert with one nationwide CRA (one-call), (iii) request free disclosures, (iv) request a § 1681c-2 block of fraudulent tradelines, (v) consider extended fraud alert and security freeze, (vi) send written disputes to furnishers and CRAs as additional belt-and-suspenders.  
2. **Plead § 1681c-2 violations precisely** — courts will examine whether the consumer provided all four statutory items (ID proof, ID theft report, identification of information, statement of non-relation). Missing items are the most common defense.  
3. **Furnisher pushback after block**: § 1681c-2(c)'s rescission grounds are limited. CRA capitulation to a furnisher's bare denial that the transaction is fraudulent is a litigation opportunity.  
4. **For employers and creditors**: maintain a written Red Flags Program with board-approval documentation, even at small institutions. Examiners commonly cite documentation gaps.  
5. **State law overlay**: state security-freeze laws are largely superseded for the nationwide CRAs but state notification-of-breach laws and state UDAP statutes remain active. Plead them in the alternative.  
6. **Disposal rule liability**: a dumpster-diving identity-theft case can sustain both § 1681w/Disposal-Rule claims (via state AG / FTC) and state-law negligence / UDAP claims.
