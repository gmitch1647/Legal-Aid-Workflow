# FCRA Permissible Purposes (15 U.S.C. § 1681b)  
  
**Topic:** Comprehensive analytical explainer of the closed list of permissible purposes for furnishing, using, and obtaining consumer reports  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b (FCRA § 604)  
**Cross-references:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(d), (h), (k); 15 U.S.C. § 1681e(a) (certification); 15 U.S.C. § 1681m (user notice on adverse action); 15 U.S.C. § 1681n / § 1681o (civil liability); 12 C.F.R. Part 1022 Subpart F (Reg V, duties of users); FTC, *40 Years of Experience with the Fair Credit Reporting Act* (July 2011), § 604; CFPB Advisory Opinion, *Fair Credit Reporting; Permissible Purposes for Furnishing, Using, and Obtaining Consumer Reports*, 87 Fed. Reg. 41243 (July 12, 2022)  
**Last updated:** May 16, 2026  
  
The permissible-purpose architecture in § 1681b is the FCRA's gating mechanism: a CRA may not lawfully furnish, and a user may not lawfully obtain or use, a consumer report unless the report fits within one of the enumerated purposes in subsection (a). The list is closed. There is no general "good business reason" catch-all, and there is no "the consumer doesn't object" workaround.  
  
This file walks the structure of § 1681b — what each permissible purpose covers, where the bright lines run, and where the common litigation flashpoints are.  
  
---  
  
## 1. The closed list — § 1681b(a)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(a); source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1681b; https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=(title:15+section:1681b+edition:prelim)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "[A]ny consumer reporting agency may furnish a consumer report under the following circumstances and no other ..."  
  
The phrase **"and no other"** is the textual hook for the closed-list reading. The FTC's *40 Years of Experience with the Fair Credit Reporting Act* (July 2011) staff report adopts that reading in its discussion of § 604, and the CFPB's July 2022 Advisory Opinion (87 Fed. Reg. 41243) confirms that "[t]he permissible purposes listed in FCRA section 604(a) are exhaustive." The Supreme Court's *Safeco Ins. Co. of America v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007), assumes the same closed-list framework when it analyzes whether an action is "in connection with the underwriting of insurance" under § 1681b(a)(3)(C).  
  
Two consequences:  
  
1. A CRA that furnishes a report outside the list violates the FCRA, even if the user lied about its purpose — though "reason to believe" gives the CRA a partial defense.  
2. A user that obtains or uses a report outside the list violates § 1681b(f), regardless of whether the user thought it had a permissible purpose.  
  
---  
  
## 2. Court order or federal grand jury subpoena — § 1681b(a)(1)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(a)(1)  
  
**Operative language:** A CRA may furnish a report "[i]n response to the order of a court having jurisdiction to issue such an order, or a subpoena issued in connection with proceedings before a Federal grand jury."  
  
**Plain English:** A court order (any court with jurisdiction) or a federal grand jury subpoena. Note the asymmetry — *federal* grand jury subpoenas qualify; state grand jury subpoenas and ordinary civil subpoenas do not.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Civil discovery subpoenas served on CRAs without a court order are not, by themselves, a permissible purpose. CRAs typically require a court order or written instructions of the consumer (§ 1681b(a)(2)) before responding.  
- Administrative subpoenas from federal regulators are addressed by § 1681f and related provisions, not § 1681b(a)(1).  
  
---  
  
## 3. Written instructions of the consumer — § 1681b(a)(2)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(a)(2)  
  
**Operative language:** A CRA may furnish a report "[i]n accordance with the written instructions of the consumer to whom it relates."  
  
**Plain English:** With the consumer's express written authorization, a CRA may release the report to anyone the consumer specifies.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- The basis for consumer-facing services like Credit Karma, mortgage broker pulls, and most landlord screening pulls where the applicant runs their own report and forwards it.  
- The CFPB has cautioned that "written instructions" should be specific to the recipient and purpose; vague blanket authorizations buried in click-through terms are vulnerable to challenge.  
- "Written" includes electronic under E-SIGN.  
  
---  
  
## 4. Specific permissible business needs — § 1681b(a)(3)  
  
The largest and most-litigated bucket. A CRA may furnish a report to a person it has "reason to believe" intends to use it for one of the following purposes.  
  
### 4.1 § 1681b(a)(3)(A) — Credit transactions  
  
**Operative language:** "[I]ntends to use the information in connection with a credit transaction involving the consumer on whom the information is to be furnished and involving the extension of credit to, or review or collection of an account of, the consumer."  
  
**Plain English:** Three sub-uses: (i) extension of credit, (ii) review of an existing account, (iii) collection of an existing account. The most-used permissible purpose in practice. Note: "collection" here is collection of the user's own account; third-party debt collectors typically rely on (a)(3)(F)(ii) account-review or written-instructions purposes (see § 4.6 below).  
  
### 4.2 § 1681b(a)(3)(B) — Employment purposes  
  
**Operative language:** "[I]ntends to use the information for employment purposes."  
  
Cross-reference: § 1681a(h) defines "employment purposes"; § 1681b(b) imposes additional procedural rules (see § 5 below).  
  
### 4.3 § 1681b(a)(3)(C) — Underwriting of insurance  
  
**Operative language:** "[I]ntends to use the information in connection with the underwriting of insurance involving the consumer."  
  
**Plain English:** Insurance carriers writing or rating a policy. *Safeco v. Burr* clarified that an initial rate quote based on a credit-based insurance score is "in connection with the underwriting of insurance" — and that an unfavorable initial rate can be "adverse action" under § 1681a(k).  
  
### 4.4 § 1681b(a)(3)(D) — License or government benefit  
  
**Operative language:** "[I]ntends to use the information in connection with a determination of the consumer's eligibility for a license or other benefit granted by a governmental instrumentality required by law to consider an applicant's financial responsibility or status."  
  
**Plain English:** Government licensing/benefit decisions where the governing statute or regulation requires consideration of financial responsibility. Not a general government "we want to know" purpose — there must be a legal mandate that financial status be considered.  
  
### 4.5 § 1681b(a)(3)(E) — Potential investor, servicer, or current insurer  
  
**Operative language:** "[I]ntends to use the information, as a potential investor or servicer, or current insurer, in connection with a valuation of, or an assessment of the credit or prepayment risks associated with, an existing credit obligation."  
  
**Plain English:** The secondary-market purpose. Mortgage-backed-securities investors, loan servicers, and existing insurers of credit obligations may pull reports to value or risk-assess a specific existing obligation. Note: limited to *existing* obligations.  
  
### 4.6 § 1681b(a)(3)(F) — Legitimate business need  
  
**Operative language:** "[O]therwise has a legitimate business need for the information — (i) in connection with a business transaction that is initiated by the consumer; or (ii) to review an account to determine whether the consumer continues to meet the terms of the account."  
  
**Plain English:** A residual purpose with two narrow tails: (i) consumer-initiated transactions; (ii) periodic account review.  
  
**Litigation flashpoints:**  
- **"Initiated by the consumer."** The Eleventh Circuit has held that a satellite TV provider had a legitimate business need to pull a credit report to verify the identity of someone applying for service, even though the applicant turned out to be an impostor. The user-side good-faith inquiry is real, but courts have not extended "initiated by the consumer" to debt collectors pursuing alleged debtors who have not engaged with the collector.  
- **"Review of an account."** Limited to accounts the user actually has with the consumer. The Seventh Circuit has held that pulling a credit report to facilitate collection of a charged-off or sold debt is not § 1681b(a)(3)(F)(ii) "account review" if the original creditor relationship has been terminated.  
- **Debt collectors and § 1681b(a)(3)(F).** This is the high-volume problem area. Many courts hold that a debt collector working a delinquent account on behalf of the original creditor has the same permissible purpose as the creditor under (a)(3)(A) collection or (a)(3)(F)(ii) review. But a buyer of charged-off receivables pulling reports to skip-trace or pressure consumers is on far shakier ground; private FCRA suits under § 1681b(f) regularly succeed against such purchasers.  
  
### 4.7 § 1681b(a)(3)(G) — Child support / travel charge cards  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(a)(3)(G)  
  
**Operative language:** [VERIFY exact phrasing] — covers executive branch use of consumer reports in connection with the issuance of government-sponsored individually-billed travel charge cards.  
  
**Note on child support:** Child-support enforcement uses are governed not by (a)(3)(G) but by **§ 1681b(a)(4)** (state and local child support enforcement agencies for purposes of establishing parentage, capacity to pay, or enforcement) and **§ 1681b(a)(5)** (federal CSE agencies). The 10-day pre-pull notice to the non-custodial parent runs from § 1681b(a)(4)(C). Drafters should check the current subsection lettering before citing — the post-CARD-Act and post-FACTA renumberings have caused widespread citation errors.  
  
### 4.8 § 1681b(a)(3)(H) and other banking/agency uses  
  
Subsection (a)(3) has additional subparagraphs covering specific banking and regulatory uses. § 1681b(a)(3)(H) and the surrounding subparagraphs cover bank agency examinations and FDIC-insured deposit determinations [VERIFY exact scope of (H) versus (I); subparagraph lettering has changed]. § 1681b(a)(6) covers Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation use in connection with the resolution of failed institutions.

## 5. Special employment rules — § 1681b(b)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)  
  
The procedural overlay for employment-purpose reports. Three pillars:  
  
### 5.1 Certification by user — § 1681b(b)(1)  
  
The user must certify to the CRA (in advance, in a general or specific certification) that the user will comply with paragraph (2)'s disclosure-and-authorization rules and with paragraph (3)'s pre-adverse-action rules.  
  
### 5.2 Stand-alone disclosure and written authorization — § 1681b(b)(2)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "[A] person may not procure a consumer report, or cause a consumer report to be procured, for employment purposes with respect to any consumer, unless — (i) a clear and conspicuous disclosure has been made in writing to the consumer at any time before the report is procured or caused to be procured, in a document that consists solely of the disclosure, that a consumer report may be obtained for employment purposes; and (ii) the consumer has authorized in writing ... the procurement of the report by that person."  
  
**Plain English:** Two requirements: (a) a clear and conspicuous, stand-alone written disclosure; (b) written authorization. The "stand-alone document" requirement is the source of the largest wave of FCRA class actions in the last decade. Courts have held that extraneous waivers, indemnities, or state-law disclosures in the same document violate § 1681b(b)(2)(A) (see *Gilberg v. California Check Cashing Stores, LLC*, 913 F.3d 1169 (9th Cir. 2019)).  
  
### 5.3 Pre-adverse-action notice — § 1681b(b)(3)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "[I]n using a consumer report for employment purposes, before taking any adverse action based in whole or in part on the report, the person intending to take such adverse action shall provide to the consumer to whom the report relates — (i) a copy of the report; and (ii) a description in writing of the rights of the consumer under this subchapter, as prescribed by the Bureau."  
  
**Plain English:** Before pulling the trigger on an adverse employment decision based on the report, give the consumer (a) a copy of the report and (b) the CFPB's Summary of Rights. After the decision, the user still owes a § 1681m post-adverse-action notice.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- Courts have not settled on a bright-line waiting period between pre-adverse-action notice and final decision, but five business days is widely treated as a safe floor.  
- *Long v. Southeastern Pa. Transp. Auth.*, 903 F.3d 312 (3d Cir. 2018), held that consumers have Article III standing for naked pre-adverse-action violations only when they can show informational injury and a realistic opportunity to contest.

## 6. Prescreening — § 1681b(c)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(c)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** A CRA may furnish a consumer report "in connection with any credit or insurance transaction that is not initiated by the consumer" only if (1) it is a firm offer of credit or insurance, (2) the CRA has complied with § 1681b(e) opt-out procedures, (3) there is no in-effect opt-out election by the consumer, and (4) the disclosure includes specific consumer notices and opt-out instructions.  
  
**Plain English:** The pre-approved-offer ("prescreened") credit card and insurance solicitation regime. The CRA may sell a "list" derived from consumer files only if the offer is firm (will be honored if the consumer meets the published criteria), the consumer has not opted out via OptOutPrescreen.com, and the solicitation contains the statutory short and long notices.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- "Firm offer" is defined at § 1681a(l). *Cole v. U.S. Capital, Inc.*, 389 F.3d 719 (7th Cir. 2004), held that a "firm offer" must carry real value; sham low-credit-limit offers do not qualify.  
- The opt-out can be a five-year opt-out or a permanent opt-out (electronic or by mail).

## 7. User-side prohibition — § 1681b(f)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(f)  
  
**Operative language:** "A person shall not use or obtain a consumer report for any purpose unless — (1) the consumer report is obtained for a purpose for which the consumer report is authorized to be furnished under this section; and (2) the purpose is certified in accordance with section 1681e of this title by a prospective user of the report through a general or specific certification."  
  
**Plain English:** Two affirmative obligations on the user side: (a) actually have a § 1681b(a) purpose; (b) certify that purpose to the CRA. Violation is independently actionable under §§ 1681n / 1681o.  
  
**Litigation flashpoints:**  
- **Impermissible pulls by debt collectors.** Most common fact pattern in private litigation. Collectors pulling reports for sold/charged-off debts, or to skip-trace on contested debts, generate § 1681b(f) claims.  
- **Pulls by people who are not creditors at all.** Ex-spouses, opposing parties in litigation, private investigators — repeat-offender fact patterns.  
- **Pulls based on stale or revoked authorization.** Consumer-initiated pulls (under (a)(2) or (a)(3)(F)(i)) require some current consumer connection; pulls weeks or months after the transaction closed are vulnerable.

## 8. Medical information limitations — § 1681b(g)  
  
**Citation:** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(g)  
  
**Operative language (excerpt):** "A consumer reporting agency shall not furnish for employment purposes, or in connection with a credit or insurance transaction, a consumer report that contains medical information (other than medical contact information treated in the manner required under section 1681c(a)(6)) about a consumer, unless — (A) if furnished in connection with an insurance transaction, the consumer affirmatively consents to the furnishing of the report; (B) if furnished for employment purposes or in connection with a credit transaction — (i) the information to be furnished is relevant to process or effect the employment or credit transaction; and (ii) the consumer provides specific written consent for the furnishing of the report that describes in clear and conspicuous language the use for which the information will be furnished ..."  
  
**Plain English:** Medical information is locked down. Insurance: needs affirmative consumer consent. Employment/credit: needs relevance plus a specific written consent that describes the use. The provision interacts with § 1681c(a)(6) (medical-contact-information masking on credit reports) and the Reg V medical-information rules.  
  
**Practical notes:**  
- The provision is the structural basis for keeping diagnosis codes, provider names that disclose specialty, and similar identifying medical detail off credit reports.  
- The 2022 NCAP medical-debt changes (no medical debt under $500; one-year delay; paid medical debts removed) are CRA voluntary practices layered over the § 1681b(g) floor.

## 9. Key litigation issues  
  
- **Pulling reports without a permissible purpose.** § 1681b(f) violations are the bread-and-butter of consumer FCRA practice. *Safeco* applies: willfulness requires recklessness, and "an objectively reasonable interpretation of the statute" is a defense.  
- **What "review of an account" means.** Account review under § 1681b(a)(3)(F)(ii) requires an existing account relationship. Once the account is charged off, sold, or extinguished, review-purpose pulls are vulnerable.  
- **"Initiated by the consumer."** Consumer-initiated under § 1681b(a)(3)(F)(i) is read fairly broadly when the user is in good faith — see the Eleventh Circuit's identity-verification line of cases — but does not extend to communications the consumer did not actually initiate.  
- **Debt collectors and FCRA.** A debt collector working a delinquent original-creditor account stands in the original creditor's permissible-purpose shoes. A debt buyer pulling reports on its acquired portfolio is on different statutory footing and faces serious § 1681b(f) exposure.  
- **Employment class actions.** § 1681b(b)(2) "stand-alone document" failures continue to drive seven- and eight-figure settlements. Post-*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), standing remains a battleground.  
- **Government uses.** The lettering of § 1681b(a)(3)(D), (G), (H), and § 1681b(a)(4)–(6) has shifted across amendments. Before citing for child-support, banking-agency, or FDIC purposes, [VERIFY] the current subsection lettering against the prelim edition at uscode.house.gov.

## Practical compliance notes  
  
- **Certification is not optional.** § 1681b(f)(2) cross-references § 1681e(a). CRA contracts (subscriber agreements with Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) operationalize this with general certifications plus per-pull purpose codes. Plead the certification chain when challenging the pull.  
- **Reason to believe.** § 1681b(a)(3) says the CRA furnishes "to a person which it has reason to believe" intends a permissible use. The CRA's good-faith reliance on subscriber certifications usually shields it from liability when a subscriber lies — but pattern-of-lying knowledge defeats the defense.  
- **State law overlays.** California's CCRAA, Vermont's opt-in regime, New York's Article 25, and several other state laws impose additional permissible-purpose restrictions and consumer-consent requirements that supplement (and are not preempted by — see § 1681t) the FCRA in many respects.
