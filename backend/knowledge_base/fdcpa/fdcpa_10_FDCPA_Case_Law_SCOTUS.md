\# FDCPA Case Law — Supreme Court of the United States  
  
\*\*Topic:\*\* Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) — Supreme Court decisions  
\*\*Primary statute:\*\* 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692–1692p  
\*\*Last updated:\*\* May 16, 2026  
\*\*Cross-references:\*\* See \`fdcpa\_02\_Definitions.md\` (§ 1692a defined terms), \`fdcpa\_03\_Prohibited\_Conduct.md\` (§§ 1692b–f), \`fdcpa\_04\_Validation\_Notice.md\` (§ 1692g), \`fdcpa\_05\_Remedies.md\` (§ 1692k), \`fdcpa\_11\_FDCPA\_Case\_Law\_Circuits.md\` (circuit-level applications)  
  
\---  
  
\#\# Overview  
  
The Supreme Court has decided a small but consequential set of FDCPA cases. Together they have:  
  
1\. Established that \*\*attorneys\*\* who regularly collect consumer debts are "debt collectors" (\*Heintz\*);  
2\. Narrowed the \*\*bona fide error defense\*\* so it cannot excuse mistakes of law (\*Jerman\*);  
3\. Allowed prevailing defendants to recover \*\*routine costs\*\* under FRCP 54(d) without a bad-faith finding (\*Marx\*);  
4\. Approved use of a state \*\*Attorney General's letterhead\*\* by special counsel collecting state debts (\*Sheriff v. Gillie\*);  
5\. Held that filing an obviously \*\*time-barred proof of claim\*\* in Chapter 13 is not an FDCPA violation (\*Midland Funding v. Johnson\*);  
6\. Held that a buyer of defaulted debt \*\*collecting for itself\*\* is not a "debt collector" under § 1692a(6)'s "regularly collects" branch (\*Henson\*);  
7\. Held that a law firm pursuing only \*\*non-judicial foreclosure\*\* is not a "debt collector" except for § 1692f(6) (\*Obduskey\*); and  
8\. Rejected a \*\*discovery rule\*\* for the FDCPA's one-year statute of limitations (\*Rotkiske\*).  
  
The cases are presented chronologically.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291 (1995)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* Supreme Court of the United States  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1995  
\*\*Vote:\*\* 9-0  
\*\*Author:\*\* Justice Breyer  
  
\#\#\# Holding  
The FDCPA's term "debt collector" includes a lawyer who "regularly," through litigation, attempts to collect consumer debts. Attorneys engaged in consumer debt-collection litigation are subject to the Act.  
  
\#\#\# Facts  
Petitioner George Heintz, a lawyer, represented a bank that sued respondent Darlene Jenkins to recover the balance due on her defaulted automobile loan. A letter from Heintz's firm itemized the amount owed, including the cost of insurance the bank had purchased after Jenkins allegedly failed to maintain coverage. Jenkins sued Heintz and his firm under the FDCPA, alleging that the inclusion of the insurance figure was a false representation of the debt's character and amount. The district court dismissed, holding the FDCPA does not reach lawyers engaged in litigation. The Seventh Circuit reversed.  
  
\#\#\# Key reasoning  
The Court rested on two textual points. First, the FDCPA's definition of "debt collector" — "any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another," § 1692a(6) — contains no carve-out for lawyers. Second, Congress had originally exempted attorneys but repealed that exemption in 1986 (Pub. L. 99-361). Reading the statute to nonetheless exempt attorneys engaged in litigation would re-import the repealed exemption.  
  
Heintz argued that several provisions — including § 1692e(5) (threatening to take action that "cannot legally be taken") and § 1692e(11) (mini-Miranda warnings in initial communications) — would be unworkable if applied to litigation activity. The Court rejected these arguments, reasoning that a lawyer who files a non-frivolous lawsuit cannot have threatened an action that "cannot legally be taken," and that § 1692e(11)'s "initial communication" language could fairly be construed to accommodate formal pleadings (an issue Congress later clarified in the 2006 amendment exempting "formal pleadings" from § 1692e(11) and § 1692g(a)).  
  
\#\#\# Impact / what the bot should know  
\- Law firms and individual attorneys who regularly file consumer collection suits are debt collectors and must comply with the FDCPA.  
\- A demand letter on attorney letterhead is itself a "communication" subject to §§ 1692e and 1692g.  
\- 2006 amendments (15 U.S.C. § 1692e(11), § 1692g(d)) exempt formal pleadings from validation notice requirements but otherwise leave \*Heintz\* intact.  
\- The opinion did not draw a litigation/non-litigation line — both pre-suit demands and litigation conduct may give rise to FDCPA liability.  
  
\#\#\# Related statute sections  
§ 1692a(6) (definition of debt collector); § 1692e (false or misleading representations); § 1692e(5) (threats); § 1692e(11) (mini-Miranda); § 1692g (validation notice).

\#\# Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA, 559 U.S. 573 (2010)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* Supreme Court of the United States  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2010  
\*\*Vote:\*\* 7-2 (Sotomayor, J., for the Court; Scalia & Alito concurring; Kennedy, J., dissenting, joined by Roberts, C.J.)  
\*\*Author:\*\* Justice Sotomayor  
  
\#\#\# Holding  
The FDCPA's bona fide error defense, 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(c), does not shield a debt collector from liability for violations resulting from a mistaken interpretation of the FDCPA itself. The defense is limited to clerical or factual mistakes.  
  
\#\#\# Facts  
Carlisle, a law firm representing a mortgage company, filed a foreclosure complaint in Ohio state court against Karen Jerman. The complaint contained a notice that the debt would be assumed valid unless Jerman disputed it \*in writing\*. Jerman's counsel notified Carlisle that the debt had been paid; Countrywide confirmed payment, and Carlisle dismissed the foreclosure. Jerman then brought a putative class action alleging that the requirement that disputes be in writing violated § 1692g(a)(3), which (unlike § 1692g(a)(4) and (5)) does not contain a "writing" requirement. The Sixth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Carlisle on the ground that the bona fide error defense excused the firm's misreading of the statute.  
  
\#\#\# Key reasoning  
Section 1692k(c) provides that a debt collector is not liable "if \[it\] shows by a preponderance of evidence that the violation was not intentional and resulted from a bona fide error notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error." The Court read "bona fide error" against the well-established maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse. By contrast, the Truth in Lending Act's bona fide error provision had been amended in 1980 specifically to exclude "an error of legal judgment" — language Congress did not put in the FDCPA, but that the Court read as confirming that "bona fide error" in unmodified form covers only clerical/factual mistakes.  
  
The Court emphasized structural reasons too. The FDCPA already accommodates legal uncertainty in other ways — for example, through the "least sophisticated consumer" standard's "quotient of reasonableness," the statute's safe-harbor advisory opinion provisions in § 1692k(e), and discretion in awarding statutory damages under § 1692k(b). Allowing a "mistake of law" defense would render those mechanisms redundant and would dramatically reduce the deterrent effect of the Act.  
  
Justice Kennedy's dissent argued the majority's rule put attorneys in an impossible position when the law was genuinely unsettled. The majority responded that hard cases of statutory interpretation are best handled through formal advisory opinions and good-faith reliance on circuit precedent — not through a defense that swallows the rule.  
  
\#\#\# Impact / what the bot should know  
\- A debt collector cannot avoid FDCPA liability by showing it sincerely (but wrongly) believed its conduct complied with the statute.  
\- The bona fide error defense remains available for \*\*clerical and factual\*\* errors (mistyped balance, wrong consumer addressed, data-entry error), provided the collector maintained "procedures reasonably adapted" to avoid such errors.  
\- \*Jerman\* sharply increases compliance risk for law firms doing high-volume collections work.  
\- Circuit courts have split on whether the defense covers mistakes of \*state\* law (e.g., misjudging when a state's limitations period runs) — most circuits read \*Jerman\* to bar that defense too.  
  
\#\#\# Related statute sections  
§ 1692k(c) (bona fide error defense); § 1692g (validation notice); § 1692k(e) (safe-harbor advisory opinions); § 1692k(b) (factors in damages award).

\#\# Marx v. General Revenue Corp., 568 U.S. 371 (2013)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* Supreme Court of the United States  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2013  
\*\*Vote:\*\* 7-2 (Sotomayor & Kagan, JJ., dissenting)  
\*\*Author:\*\* Justice Thomas  
  
\#\#\# Holding  
A district court may award costs to a prevailing defendant in an FDCPA case under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(d)(1) without first finding that the plaintiff brought the action in bad faith and for the purpose of harassment. FDCPA § 1692k(a)(3) does not "provide otherwise" within the meaning of Rule 54(d).  
  
\#\#\# Facts  
Olivea Marx sued General Revenue Corporation (GRC) alleging FDCPA violations arising out of debt-collection calls. The district court found for GRC on the merits and, applying Rule 54(d)(1), taxed $4,543.03 in costs against Marx — primarily deposition transcripts and witness fees. Marx argued that 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(3) — which authorizes the court to award the defendant attorney's fees \*and costs\* upon a finding of bad faith and harassment — was the exclusive avenue for cost-shifting in FDCPA cases.  
  
\#\#\# Key reasoning  
Rule 54(d)(1) provides that "unless a federal statute, these rules, or a court order provides otherwise, costs — other than attorney's fees — should be allowed to the prevailing party." The Court read "provides otherwise" narrowly: a statute "provides otherwise" only when it is contrary to Rule 54(d). Section 1692k(a)(3) does not say costs are \*only\* available in cases of bad faith; it simply identifies bad faith as a condition for an \*additional\* fee-and-cost award against a plaintiff. Read in light of the background rule that prevailing parties get costs, § 1692k(a)(3) is best understood as an addition to Rule 54(d), not a substitute for it.  
  
Justice Sotomayor's dissent argued that the statute's structure — listing both fees and costs together — implied a legislative judgment that ordinary cost-shifting against consumers in FDCPA cases would chill legitimate suits, and that the majority's reading made the express "costs" reference in § 1692k(a)(3) superfluous.  
  
\#\#\# Impact / what the bot should know  
\- Losing FDCPA plaintiffs can be ordered to pay routine taxable costs (deposition transcripts, copying, witness fees) under Rule 54(d) regardless of good faith.  
\- Costs awards remain modest in scope (the Rule 54(d) catalog at 28 U.S.C. § 1920 is limited); attorney's fees against the plaintiff still require the § 1692k(a)(3) showing.  
\- For consumer counsel, the case is a reminder to assess case strength carefully — even meritorious-looking claims can produce thousands of dollars in adverse costs if lost.  
\- Some district courts use their Rule 54(d) discretion to deny or reduce cost awards against indigent FDCPA plaintiffs.  
  
\#\#\# Related statute sections  
§ 1692k(a)(3) (attorney's fees and costs for prevailing defendant on bad-faith showing); Fed. R. Civ. P. 54(d)(1); 28 U.S.C. § 1920 (taxable costs).

\#\# Sheriff v. Gillie, 578 U.S. 317 (2016)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* Supreme Court of the United States  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2016  
\*\*Vote:\*\* 8-0  
\*\*Author:\*\* Justice Ginsburg  
  
\#\#\# Holding  
Special counsel appointed by the Ohio Attorney General to collect debts owed to the State are not "false, deceptive, or misleading" under § 1692e when they use the Attorney General's letterhead, because their use of that letterhead accurately conveys that they act on the AG's behalf. The Court declined to decide whether such special counsel are "state officers" excluded from the FDCPA definition of "debt collector" under § 1692a(6)(C).  
  
\#\#\# Facts  
Ohio law authorizes the Attorney General to retain outside attorneys as "special counsel" to collect debts owed to the State or its instrumentalities. The AG directed special counsel — petitioners Mark Sheriff and Eric Jones, and their firms — to use the official Ohio Attorney General letterhead when corresponding with debtors. Hazel Meadows and Pamela Gillie, who received such letters relating to debts owed to a state hospital and a state university, brought a putative class action under the FDCPA, alleging the use of the AG's letterhead was false or misleading because the letters were really from outside law firms. The Sixth Circuit found genuine issues of material fact requiring trial; the Supreme Court reversed.  
  
\#\#\# Key reasoning  
The Court analyzed the letters under § 1692e's "false, deceptive, or misleading" standard from the perspective of the "least sophisticated consumer." Each letter explicitly identified the writer (the law firm) and stated that the firm was "Outside Counsel" or "Special Counsel" for the Attorney General. The use of the AG's letterhead, far from being misleading, accurately reflected the legal reality: the special counsel were authorized agents of the State. The letterhead "conveys on whose authority the special counsel has issued the letters demanding payment of the debt owed the State."  
  
Although the Sixth Circuit had also addressed whether special counsel might fall within § 1692a(6)(C)'s exclusion for "officer\[s\] or employee\[s\] of ... any State," the Supreme Court did not need to reach that question because the letters did not violate § 1692e in any event.  
  
\#\#\# Impact / what the bot should know  
\- Lawyers acting as authorized agents for state debt collection may use the state's official letterhead without violating § 1692e, provided the letters identify the writer.  
\- The case does not generally license private debt collectors to use third-party letterhead — the holding turns on the special counsel's actual authority to speak on behalf of the State.  
\- \*Sheriff\* is the leading example of the Court accepting a context-and-authority justification for a representation that might otherwise look misleading to the "least sophisticated consumer."  
  
\#\#\# Related statute sections  
§ 1692a(6) (definition of debt collector, including § 1692a(6)(C) "officer or employee of ... any State" exclusion); § 1692e (false or misleading representations).

\#\# Midland Funding, LLC v. Johnson, 581 U.S. 224 (2017)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* Supreme Court of the United States  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2017  
\*\*Vote:\*\* 5-3 (Sotomayor, J., dissenting, joined by Ginsburg & Kagan, JJ.; Gorsuch, J., did not participate)  
\*\*Author:\*\* Justice Breyer  
  
\#\#\# Holding  
A debt collector does not engage in conduct prohibited by the FDCPA — specifically, false, deceptive, misleading, unfair, or unconscionable conduct — when it files a proof of claim in a Chapter 13 bankruptcy proceeding on a debt that is obviously barred by the applicable state statute of limitations.  
  
\#\#\# Facts  
Aleida Johnson filed for Chapter 13 bankruptcy in the Southern District of Alabama. Midland Funding, a debt buyer, filed a proof of claim asserting a credit-card debt on which the last transaction had occurred more than ten years earlier — well beyond Alabama's six-year limitations period. Johnson objected, the bankruptcy court disallowed the claim, and Johnson then sued Midland under the FDCPA. The Eleventh Circuit (following its earlier decision in \*Crawford v. LVNV Funding\*) held that filing a proof of claim on an obviously time-barred debt violated the FDCPA. The Supreme Court reversed.  
  
\#\#\# Key reasoning  
The Court reasoned that a "claim" in bankruptcy is a "right to payment," and under Alabama law (and the law of most states), a stale debt remains a "right to payment" even though it cannot be enforced through a defensive statute-of-limitations bar. Therefore, Midland's proof of claim was not "false" in the ordinary sense. Nor was it "unfair" or "unconscionable": the Bankruptcy Code itself sets up a process — claim objections by the trustee or debtor — for sorting out stale or invalid claims. Unlike a consumer in a one-on-one debt-collection action, a Chapter 13 debtor has the protections of a trustee, court supervision, and counsel.  
  
The Court emphasized the structural mismatch between FDCPA private litigation and the bankruptcy process: importing the "least sophisticated consumer" standard into bankruptcy claim filing would conflict with the Bankruptcy Code's design and could displace its remedies.  
  
Justice Sotomayor's dissent argued that Midland's business model — buying portfolios of stale debt cheaply and filing thousands of claims in the hope that some go unobjected to — was paradigmatically unfair conduct that the FDCPA was designed to deter.  
  
\#\#\# Impact / what the bot should know  
\- Filing a proof of claim on a stale debt in Chapter 13 generally is not an FDCPA violation when the debt remains a "right to payment" under state law.  
\- The holding turns on the state-law treatment of time-barred debts. In states where the limitations period \*extinguishes\* the right to payment (e.g., Wisconsin, Mississippi), \*Midland Funding\* is distinguishable and the FDCPA may still apply.  
\- \*Midland Funding\* does \*\*not\*\* disturb circuit law (e.g., \*McMahon\*, \*Daugherty\*) holding that out-of-court collection on time-barred debts can violate § 1692e.  
\- The 2021 Regulation F rule, 12 C.F.R. § 1006.26, prohibits suing or threatening to sue on time-barred debts but applies outside bankruptcy.  
  
\#\#\# Related statute sections  
§ 1692e (false or misleading representations); § 1692f (unfair or unconscionable conduct); 11 U.S.C. § 101(5) (definition of "claim").

\#\# Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 582 U.S. 79 (2017)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* Supreme Court of the United States  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2017  
\*\*Vote:\*\* 9-0  
\*\*Author:\*\* Justice Gorsuch (his first opinion)  
  
\#\#\# Holding  
A company that purchases defaulted consumer debt and seeks to collect that debt for its own account is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA's "regularly collects ... debts owed or due another" branch of § 1692a(6). The Court did not decide whether such a debt buyer might qualify as a debt collector under § 1692a(6)'s separate "principal purpose" branch.  
  
\#\#\# Facts  
CitiFinancial Auto extended loans to four borrowers, who defaulted. CitiFinancial sold the defaulted loans to Santander Consumer USA. Santander then attempted to collect the deficiencies for itself. The borrowers sued under the FDCPA, alleging various violations. The Fourth Circuit held that Santander was not a "debt collector" because the defaulted debts were now owed to \*Santander\* — not to "another." The Supreme Court affirmed.  
  
\#\#\# Key reasoning  
Justice Gorsuch's textualist opinion focused tightly on the statutory phrase "owed ... another." When Santander collects on debts it now owns, those debts are owed to \*Santander\*, not "another." The fact that the debts were in default at the time Santander purchased them did not matter to the analysis: the statute speaks to ownership, not to default status.  
  
The plaintiffs argued that Congress's reference to the "originator" of the debt in nearby provisions implied a default-status test, and that the act's evident purpose was to police third-party collection abuses regardless of who held the paper. The Court rejected those policy arguments as overriding the statutory text: "\[I\]t is never our job to rewrite a constitutionally valid statutory text under the banner of speculation about what Congress might have done."  
  
Critically, the Court expressly noted that it was deciding only the "regularly collects ... for another" branch of § 1692a(6). It left open whether a debt buyer might fall within the alternative "principal purpose" definition — i.e., a "person ... in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts." After \*Henson\*, the Third Circuit's \*Tepper v. Amos Financial, LLC\*, 898 F.3d 364 (3d Cir. 2018), and most district courts have held that debt buyers whose entire business is buying and collecting defaulted debt do qualify under the "principal purpose" prong.  
  
\#\#\# Impact / what the bot should know  
\- A debt buyer collecting for its own account is \*\*not\*\* a debt collector under the "regularly collects for another" prong, regardless of whether the debts were in default at purchase.  
\- The same debt buyer is very likely still a debt collector under § 1692a(6)'s "principal purpose" prong — see \*Tepper v. Amos Financial\* (3d Cir. 2018).  
\- Bank servicers collecting performing debts they have purchased remain outside the FDCPA's "debt collector" definition (consistent with § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) for non-defaulted debt acquired by a servicer).  
\- \*Henson\* did not change the rule that traditional third-party collection agencies collecting for others remain debt collectors.  
  
\#\#\# Related statute sections  
§ 1692a(6) (definition of debt collector — both the "regularly collects for another" branch and the "principal purpose" branch); § 1692a(4) (definition of creditor); § 1692a(6)(F) (servicer/originator exclusions).

\#\# Obduskey v. McCarthy & Holthus LLP, 586 U.S. 466 (2019)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* Supreme Court of the United States  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2019  
\*\*Vote:\*\* 9-0 (Sotomayor, J., filed a concurrence)  
\*\*Author:\*\* Justice Breyer  
  
\#\#\# Holding  
A business engaged in no more than non-judicial foreclosure of a security interest is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA, except for the limited purpose of § 1692f(6) (which prohibits non-judicial action without a present right to possession). Such an entity is therefore not subject to most FDCPA provisions, including § 1692g's validation requirements.  
  
\#\#\# Facts  
Dennis Obduskey bought a Colorado home in 2007 with a $329,940 loan secured by the property. He defaulted in 2009. In 2014, Wells Fargo retained McCarthy & Holthus LLP to conduct a non-judicial foreclosure under Colorado law. McCarthy sent Obduskey a letter stating the firm had been "instructed to commence foreclosure" and identifying Wells Fargo as the creditor. Obduskey sent McCarthy a written dispute and demanded verification of the debt under § 1692g(b). McCarthy proceeded with the foreclosure without responding. Obduskey sued, alleging the firm violated the FDCPA's verification provisions.  
  
\#\#\# Key reasoning  
The Court found the statute genuinely ambiguous: the primary "debt collector" definition in § 1692a(6) plausibly includes a foreclosure firm because foreclosure is an "indirect" attempt to collect the underlying debt; but § 1692a(6)'s "limited-purpose" definition treats anyone whose business is the enforcement of security interests as a "debt collector" only "\[f\]or the purpose of section 1692f(6)." The Court read the latter provision as expressing Congress's specific judgment about how foreclosure agents fit into the Act. If foreclosure agents were already "debt collectors" under § 1692a(6)'s main definition for all purposes, the limited-purpose clause would be superfluous.  
  
The Court emphasized that this reading also respects the federalism interest in not federalizing detailed regulation of state foreclosure procedures, which already provide their own due process protections. Justice Sotomayor's concurrence stressed that the holding is narrow — the moment a law firm steps outside what the state foreclosure process strictly requires (e.g., by demanding payment or making extraneous threats), it may again become a "debt collector" for all FDCPA purposes.  
  
\#\#\# Impact / what the bot should know  
\- A law firm whose work is limited to the steps required by a state's non-judicial foreclosure process is not a debt collector for most FDCPA purposes — it cannot be sued under §§ 1692c, 1692d, 1692e, 1692g, etc.  
\- Such a firm \*\*is\*\* subject to § 1692f(6) (prohibiting taking nonjudicial action without a present right to possession of the property).  
\- The holding is limited to non-judicial foreclosure. Judicial foreclosure (where the firm files a lawsuit) is generally treated as ordinary debt-collection litigation governed by \*Heintz\*.  
\- If the firm sends letters demanding payment, offers to negotiate, makes representations beyond what state foreclosure law strictly requires, it leaves the \*Obduskey\* safe harbor and can be liable for those communications.  
  
\#\#\# Related statute sections  
§ 1692a(6) (definition of debt collector, including the limited-purpose clause for enforcers of security interests); § 1692f(6) (prohibited non-judicial action); § 1692g (validation notice).

\#\# Rotkiske v. Klemm, 589 U.S. 8 (2019)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* Supreme Court of the United States  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2019  
\*\*Vote:\*\* 8-1 (Ginsburg, J., dissenting in part; Sotomayor, J., filed a concurrence)  
\*\*Author:\*\* Justice Thomas  
  
\#\#\# Holding  
The FDCPA's one-year statute of limitations, 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(d), begins to run on the date the FDCPA violation occurs — not on the date the violation is discovered. The Court did not decide whether equitable doctrines such as equitable tolling or a fraud-specific discovery rule might apply in appropriate cases.  
  
\#\#\# Facts  
Klemm & Associates sued Kevin Rotkiske in Pennsylvania state court in 2009 to collect a credit-card debt. Service was attempted at an address where Rotkiske no longer lived; another individual accepted service. Rotkiske did not appear; Klemm obtained a default judgment. Rotkiske claims he first learned of the judgment in 2014, when his mortgage application was denied. He sued Klemm under the FDCPA in 2015 — more than one year after the violation but less than one year after discovery. The district court dismissed and the Third Circuit affirmed.  
  
\#\#\# Key reasoning  
Section 1692k(d) says an action must be brought "within one year from the date on which the violation occurs." The Court read that text literally: "the violation occurs" is when the wrongful act takes place, and the statute does not contain (and the courts should not engraft) a "discovery rule" tolling the period until the plaintiff knew or should have known of the violation. The Court contrasted the FDCPA with statutes — e.g., several SEC and tax provisions — where Congress expressly wrote in a discovery rule. The absence of such language in the FDCPA is dispositive.  
  
Importantly, the Court expressly left for another day the availability of two related doctrines: (1) the \*equitable\* discovery rule applied in fraud cases (the "fraud-specific" doctrine); and (2) ordinary equitable tolling, which can stop the clock when a defendant's misconduct has prevented the plaintiff from filing in time. Rotkiske had forfeited the latter argument below.  
  
Justice Ginsburg's dissent would have read the statute to permit a discovery rule in cases of fraudulent concealment, arguing that the strict-occurrence rule rewards a debt collector that successfully hides its violation for a year and a day.  
  
\#\#\# Impact / what the bot should know  
\- The default rule is strict: the FDCPA's one-year clock runs from the date of the alleged violation, not from when the consumer learned of it.  
\- After a default state-court judgment obtained by suspect service, a consumer typically cannot bring a federal FDCPA claim more than one year out — even if she didn't know about the judgment.  
\- Equitable tolling and a "fraud-specific" exception remain open after \*Rotkiske\*. Several circuits permit equitable tolling for FDCPA claims when defendant's misconduct concealed the violation (see \*Mangum v. Action Collection Serv.\*, 575 F.3d 935 (9th Cir. 2009)).  
\- State-law analogues (e.g., state consumer protection acts, state UDAP statutes) may have discovery rules and can be a backstop when the federal claim is time-barred.  
  
\#\#\# Related statute sections  
§ 1692k(d) (one-year statute of limitations); § 1692k(a) (private right of action and statutory damages).
