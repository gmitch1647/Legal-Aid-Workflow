\# FDCPA Harassment and Abuse — 15 U.S.C. § 1692d  
  
\*\*Topic:\*\* Conduct the natural consequence of which is to harass, oppress, or abuse — general prohibition and enumerated examples  
\*\*Primary statute:\*\* 15 U.S.C. § 1692d  
\*\*Regulation F cross-reference:\*\* 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14 (harassing, oppressive, or abusive conduct, including the 7-in-7 telephone call frequency presumption)  
\*\*Related federal law:\*\* Telephone Consumer Protection Act, 47 U.S.C. § 227 (overlap on autodialed and repeated-call claims)  
\*\*Last updated:\*\* May 16, 2026  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 1. Overview  
  
Section 1692d prohibits any "conduct the natural consequence of which is to harass, oppress, or abuse any person in connection with the collection of a debt." The general standard is followed by a \*non-exclusive\* list of six examples — subsections (1)–(6) — that are \*per se\* violations when proven, but conduct outside the list can still violate the general prohibition.  
  
Two doctrinal features dominate § 1692d litigation:  
  
1\. \*\*The "natural consequence" standard is objective.\*\* Most circuits hold that intent to harass is not required to establish the general prohibition — though intent \*is\* an element of § 1692d(5) (repeated phone calls "with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass"). The question is whether a reasonable person in the consumer's position would experience the conduct as harassing.  
  
2\. \*\*Totality-of-circumstances analysis controls call-frequency cases.\*\* Courts assess volume, frequency, pattern, time of day, the consumer's stated objections, and the substance of conversations. Reg F's 7-in-7 presumption now provides a regulatory anchor but does not displace the underlying § 1692d(5) analysis for conduct outside its scope.  
  
Section 1692d protects "any person" — not only the consumer. A neighbor, family member, or coworker subjected to harassing conduct in the course of debt collection may bring a § 1692d claim. \*Contrast\* with § 1692a(3)'s narrower "consumer" definition that limits standing under several other FDCPA provisions.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 2. Statutory Text — § 1692d  
  
"A debt collector may not engage in any conduct the natural consequence of which is to harass, oppress, or abuse any person in connection with the collection of a debt. Without limiting the general application of the foregoing, the following conduct is a violation of this section:  
  
(1) The use or threat of use of violence or other criminal means to harm the physical person, reputation, or property of any person.  
  
(2) The use of obscene or profane language or language the natural consequence of which is to abuse the hearer or reader.  
  
(3) The publication of a list of consumers who allegedly refuse to pay debts, except to a consumer reporting agency or to persons meeting the requirements of section 1681a(f) or 1681b(3) of this title.  
  
(4) The advertisement for sale of any debt to coerce payment of the debt.  
  
(5) Causing a telephone to ring or engaging any person in telephone conversation repeatedly or continuously with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass any person at the called number.  
  
(6) Except as provided in section 1692b of this title, the placement of telephone calls without meaningful disclosure of the caller's identity." 15 U.S.C. § 1692d.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 3. The General Prohibition — "Natural Consequence" Standard  
  
The opening clause of § 1692d operates as a \*separate\* basis for liability beyond the six enumerated examples. Conduct not specifically listed — abusive social-media contact, threatening references to immigration status, exploiting a known consumer vulnerability — can violate the general prohibition.  
  
\*\*Doctrinal points.\*\*  
  
\- "Natural consequence" is objective. Courts ask what a reasonable person in the consumer's position would experience, not what the collector intended. \*See\* leading circuit authority interpreting the standard as a "least sophisticated consumer" inquiry consistent with general FDCPA objective-standard doctrine.  
\- "Any person" — not only the consumer — has standing under § 1692d. A neighbor harassed by repeated location-seeking calls may sue.  
\- The standard does not require an actual psychological injury; conduct whose "natural consequence" \*would be\* to harass suffices.  
  
\*\*Examples of conduct courts have held actionable under the general prohibition:\*\*  
  
\- Contacting a consumer at a hospital after notice of admission.  
\- Repeated contact with family members about a deceased debtor's debt after the estate was clearly closed.  
\- Persistent contact via social media private messages after a request to cease.  
\- Threats of contacting the consumer's employer, church, or community in disclosure-style fashion.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 4. § 1692d(1) — Threats of Violence or Criminal Harm  
  
"The use or threat of use of violence or other criminal means to harm the physical person, reputation, or property of any person." § 1692d(1).  
  
\*\*Scope.\*\*  
  
\- Threats of physical violence against the consumer, family members, or any other person.  
\- Threats of criminal action against reputation (defamation, doxxing of a criminal-prosecution nature) or property (vandalism, damage).  
\- "Criminal means" includes threats to file false criminal charges; courts treat fabricated threats of criminal prosecution as a § 1692d(1) violation independent of § 1692e(4) (threat of action that cannot legally be taken).  
  
\*\*Litigation note.\*\* § 1692d(1) violations are rare in modern collection practice but, when proven, frequently produce significant statutory and actual damage awards. Punitive components are unavailable under the FDCPA (statutory damages cap at $1,000 per § 1692k(a)(2)(A)), but emotional-distress actual damages often dominate the recovery.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 5. § 1692d(2) — Obscene or Profane Language  
  
"The use of obscene or profane language or language the natural consequence of which is to abuse the hearer or reader." § 1692d(2).  
  
\*\*Scope.\*\*  
  
\- Profanity directed at the consumer in the course of collection.  
\- Crude, insulting, or demeaning language (slurs, name-calling, sexually explicit comments).  
\- Language whose "natural consequence" is to abuse — even if technically not profane (e.g., racial epithets that may not contain a profanity word).  
  
\*\*Practical points.\*\*  
  
\- A single instance of strong profanity in a heated call has been held actionable in some circuits; others require a pattern.  
\- The "natural consequence" tail clause expands the prohibition beyond a strict "obscene" vocabulary list — culturally specific demeaning language can qualify.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 6. § 1692d(3) — Publication of "Deadbeat" Lists  
  
"The publication of a list of consumers who allegedly refuse to pay debts, except to a consumer reporting agency or to persons meeting the requirements of section 1681a(f) or 1681b(3) of this title." § 1692d(3).  
  
\*\*Scope.\*\*  
  
\- \*Per se\* prohibition on publishing or distributing lists of consumers who have not paid debts, with one narrow exception: furnishing to consumer reporting agencies under the FCRA framework.  
\- The exception channels collector reporting into FCRA's regulated permissible-purpose architecture (15 U.S.C. § 1681a(f), § 1681b(3)).  
  
\*\*Modern application — doxxing and social media.\*\*  
  
\- Publishing the consumer's name and debt status on social media, on a public website, or in a press release violates § 1692d(3) and frequently triggers § 1692c(b) (third-party communication) and Reg F § 1006.22(f)(4) (public social-media posting in connection with collection).  
\- "Shame lists" historically distributed to local merchants are paradigmatic violations.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 7. § 1692d(4) — Advertising Debt for Sale to Coerce Payment  
  
"The advertisement for sale of any debt to coerce payment of the debt." § 1692d(4).  
  
\*\*Scope.\*\* Threatening or actually advertising the sale of a consumer's specific debt to pressure payment — for example, listing a named consumer's account in a public sale notice. The narrow target is the \*coercion\* function; legitimate non-coercive portfolio sales (commonly bulk and anonymized) do not violate this provision.  
  
\*\*Modern relevance.\*\* Bulk debt-buyer marketplaces operate behind confidential portfolio descriptions; § 1692d(4) is rarely litigated standalone in modern practice but pairs with § 1692d(3) and § 1692c(b) when individual consumer identities are disclosed in marketing.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 8. § 1692d(5) — Repeated or Continuous Telephone Calls  
  
"Causing a telephone to ring or engaging any person in telephone conversation repeatedly or continuously with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass any person at the called number." § 1692d(5).  
  
This is by volume the most-litigated § 1692d subsection.  
  
\#\#\# 8.1 Elements  
  
1\. The defendant caused a telephone to ring or engaged the person in conversation;  
2\. Repeatedly or continuously;  
3\. With intent to annoy, abuse, or harass.  
  
Unlike the general prohibition, subsection (5) contains an \*intent\* element. Intent is typically inferred from circumstances — pattern, frequency, prior cease requests, and tone.  
  
\#\#\# 8.2 Totality-of-Circumstances Analysis  
  
No bright-line call count automatically violates § 1692d(5). Courts evaluate:  
  
\- Number of calls per day, week, and month;  
\- Time-of-day pattern;  
\- Whether the consumer answered and asked the collector to stop;  
\- Whether the calls continued after a cease request;  
\- Whether voicemails were left and what they said;  
\- Whether the consumer was the called party or a third party;  
\- Pattern of immediate redial after a hang-up.  
  
District-court rulings vary widely. Some courts have found large volumes (100+ calls in a few weeks) noncompliant with the standard absent additional circumstances such as outbursts or post-cease redials; others have found small numbers sufficient where the conduct was specifically targeted to annoy (e.g., back-to-back redials at unusual hours).  
  
\#\#\# 8.3 Reg F § 1006.14(b) 7-in-7 Presumption  
  
Effective November 30, 2021, Regulation F creates a presumptive safe harbor and a presumptive violation tied to telephone-call frequency:  
  
\- \*\*Presumed compliant with § 1692d(5)\*\*: no more than seven calls within seven consecutive days to a particular person in connection with a particular debt, \*and\* no call within seven consecutive days after a telephone conversation with that person about the debt. 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2)(i).  
\- \*\*Presumed \*not\* compliant\*\*: exceeding either limit. 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(3).  
  
Key points:  
  
\- The presumption is \*\*per debt\*\*, not per consumer. Multiple-debt portfolios can yield more than seven total contacts within a week without triggering the presumption-of-violation.  
\- \*\*Voicemails count\*\* as call attempts, including limited-content messages. CFPB Official Comment 1006.14(b)(2)(i)-1.  
\- \*\*Inbound calls do not count.\*\*  
\- The presumption is \*\*rebuttable in both directions\*\* — a collector may be liable inside the safe harbor based on totality of circumstances, and may defeat liability outside the safe harbor with rebuttal evidence (although outside-the-safe-harbor conduct is exposed to a strong inference of violation).  
\- 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(h) supplies a parallel framework for \*text and email\* communications, with separate consent and frequency analytics; the 7-in-7 telephone presumption does not extend to electronic channels.  
  
\#\#\# 8.4 Common Plaintiff and Defense Postures  
  
\*\*Plaintiff.\*\* Plead specific call counts by day; pair with § 1692c(c) post-cease-and-desist claims; develop call-detail records and dialer audit logs in discovery.  
  
\*\*Defense.\*\* Move for early summary judgment within the safe harbor; for outside-safe-harbor totals, develop rebuttal facts (mistaken-number outreach, consumer-initiated callbacks, legitimate operational reasons).

\#\# 9. § 1692d(6) — Meaningful Disclosure of Caller's Identity  
  
"Except as provided in section 1692b of this title, the placement of telephone calls without meaningful disclosure of the caller's identity." § 1692d(6).  
  
\#\#\# 9.1 What Counts as "Meaningful Disclosure"  
  
Circuits split on the precise content required:  
  
\- \*\*Majority view\*\* (including the Eleventh Circuit in \*Hart v. Credit Control, LLC\*, 871 F.3d 1305 (11th Cir. 2017)): meaningful disclosure requires disclosure of the \*collection company's name\* and the \*nature of the business\* (i.e., that the caller is a debt collector calling about a debt), but does \*not\* require the individual employee to give his or her personal name.  
\- \*\*Other circuits\*\* have required the collector's true name (or a registered alias) plus the company name.  
  
\#\#\# 9.2 Section 1692b Exception  
  
The cross-reference to § 1692b means that location-information calls are \*not\* subject to § 1692d(6) — because § 1692b specifically requires the caller to identify himself but to disclose the \*employer\* only if expressly requested. The § 1692b regime governs those calls.  
  
\#\#\# 9.3 Voicemail and Limited-Content Messages  
  
The intersection with voicemail is critical: a voicemail left without identifying the caller as a debt collector (or, in some circuits, without identifying the collection company) may violate § 1692d(6). But disclosing too much in a voicemail — e.g., stating "this is John from ABC Collections calling about your past-due debt" where a household member could overhear — risks § 1692c(b) third-party-communication liability. Reg F's limited-content-message safe harbor at 12 C.F.R. § 1006.2(j) attempts to thread this needle, but the message format must follow Reg F's prescription exactly.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 10. § 1692d and § 1006.14 — Together, Not Apart  
  
12 C.F.R. § 1006.14 implements § 1692d. The regulation:  
  
\- \*\*Restates the general prohibition\*\* at § 1006.14(a).  
\- \*\*Operationalizes § 1692d(5)\*\* with the 7-in-7 presumption at § 1006.14(b).  
\- \*\*Adds prohibitions on email and text communications\*\* under § 1006.14(h) where the consumer has indicated those channels are inconvenient or where the collector has been prohibited from contacting through those channels.  
\- \*\*Mirrors the substantive § 1692d(1)–(6) prohibitions\*\* at § 1006.14(c)–(g).  
\- The regulation does not supplant the statute; conduct can violate § 1692d without separately violating § 1006.14 (and vice versa, at least conceptually).

\#\# 11. Interaction with TCPA — 47 U.S.C. § 227  
  
Call-frequency, autodialer, and pre-recorded-message claims under § 1692d(5) routinely co-plead with claims under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, 47 U.S.C. § 227. Key points:  
  
\- \*\*Distinct statutes, distinct remedies.\*\* TCPA provides $500 per call statutory damages (treblable to $1,500 for willful violations); FDCPA provides up to $1,000 per \*action\* in statutory damages plus actual damages and fees.  
\- \*\*Different consent regimes.\*\* TCPA's prior-express-consent requirement applies to autodialed and prerecorded calls/texts to cellular numbers; FDCPA's § 1692c(b) consent requirement is for collector contact with anyone other than the consumer.  
\- \*\*Overlap value.\*\* Repeated autodialed calls without consent often violate both statutes, particularly post-cease-and-desist.  
\- \*\*Robocall/prerecord rules.\*\* Robocalls and prerecorded messages to cell phones without consent violate TCPA regardless of FDCPA status; if the calls are also harassing in pattern, they additionally violate § 1692d(5).  
  
\*\*Litigation note for the chatbot.\*\* When a user describes repeated debt-collection calls to a cell phone, screen for \*both\* FDCPA § 1692d(5) and TCPA § 227 claims, plus state mini-FDCPA and state telemarketing laws. \*See\* file \`fdcpa\_15\_TCPA\_Interaction.md\`.

\#\# 12. Selected Case-Law Patterns  
  
\- \*\*High-volume calls, no further aggravating facts.\*\* Several circuits have declined to find violations on call counts alone where the collector immediately heeded oral or written cease requests and made no abusive statements. \*See, e.g.\*, \*Saltzman v. I.C. System, Inc.\*, 2009 WL 3means cases. Volume must be combined with pattern or post-cease persistence to meet the intent element.  
\- \*\*Post-cease persistence.\*\* When the consumer has asked the collector to stop and calls continue, courts more readily infer intent to harass under § 1692d(5).  
\- \*\*Voicemail and "meaningful disclosure."\*\* \*Hart v. Credit Control, LLC\* (11th Cir. 2017) — voicemail is a "communication" but identification of the company satisfies meaningful disclosure even without individual name. Other circuits diverge on the individual-name requirement.  
\- \*\*Social-media harassment.\*\* Several district courts have applied § 1692d to repeated direct-message contact on Facebook and Instagram; Reg F § 1006.22(f)(4) layers a public-posting prohibition.  
\- \*\*Calls to third parties.\*\* Repeated location-seeking calls to family members, friends, or employers violate both § 1692b(3) (once-only) and § 1692d (harassment) and may be the basis for the third party's own § 1692d claim.

\#\# 13. Practical Litigation and Compliance Notes  
  
\*\*For plaintiff counsel:\*\*  
  
\- Pull cellular carrier call-detail records to establish exact times of inbound calls.  
\- Document any prior oral or written cease requests with date/time precision.  
\- Plead § 1692d general prohibition plus the specific subsection violated.  
\- For multi-channel patterns (calls + texts + social), pair § 1692d with § 1692c violations and Reg F § 1006.14(h) and § 1006.22(f)(4).  
\- For TCPA-eligible facts (autodial or prerecord to a cell phone), plead both statutes.  
  
\*\*For defense counsel:\*\*  
  
\- Implement and document the 7-in-7 safe-harbor compliance system; produce data demonstrating compliance.  
\- Maintain robust cease-and-desist suppression queues.  
\- Build call scripts that meet \*Hart\*-style meaningful-disclosure standards in voicemail contexts.  
\- Avoid public-channel social-media communication; treat private DM as inbound only.  
\- For high-volume callers, prepare rebuttal facts to overcome the 7-in-7 presumption-of-violation.  
  
\*\*Recurring CFPB Supervisory Highlights themes:\*\*  
  
\- Exceeding 7-in-7 thresholds for high-priority accounts where local management overrode automated suppression.  
\- Failure to suppress after consumer's oral request (which is not a § 1692c(c) trigger but is relevant to § 1692d(5) intent analysis).  
\- Inadequate identification on voicemail messages, producing simultaneous § 1692d(6) and § 1692e(11) exposure.

\#\# 14. Damages Architecture for § 1692d Claims  
  
Section 1692k governs damages. Section 1692d claims often combine:  
  
\- \*\*Statutory damages\*\* up to $1,000 per \*action\* (not per violation) — § 1692k(a)(2)(A).  
\- \*\*Actual damages\*\* including emotional-distress and medical-bill components — § 1692k(a)(1). § 1692d claims often have the strongest actual-damages narrative of any FDCPA section.  
\- \*\*Fees and costs\*\* — § 1692k(a)(3) (mandatory for prevailing plaintiff).  
\- \*\*Class statutory damages\*\* capped at the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of the collector's net worth — § 1692k(a)(2)(B).  
  
\*Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA\*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010), held that the § 1692k(c) bona-fide-error defense does not apply to mistakes of law — relevant when the collector defends a § 1692d(5) or § 1692d(6) claim on the theory it misunderstood the statutory requirements.
