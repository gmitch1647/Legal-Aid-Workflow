# FDCPA Unfair Practices (15 U.S.C. § 1692f)

**Topic:** Prohibition against unfair or unconscionable means of debt collection; payment allocation; venue restrictions  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692f (FDCPA § 808); 15 U.S.C. § 1692h (FDCPA § 810 — multiple debts); 15 U.S.C. § 1692i (FDCPA § 811 — legal actions by debt collectors)  
**Cross-references:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692e (false representations); 12 C.F.R. § 1006.22 (Regulation F — unfair or unconscionable means); 15 U.S.C. § 1692k (civil liability)  
**Last updated:** May 17, 2026  

---

## Introduction

Section 1692f prohibits debt collectors from using "unfair or unconscionable means to collect or attempt to collect any debt." Like § 1692e, it contains both a general catch-all prohibition and a list of specifically enumerated practices (eight subsections). The enumerated practices are per se violations; conduct not fitting within subsections (1) through (8) may still violate the general prohibition if it is unfair or unconscionable under the circumstances.

Section 1692f frequently overlaps with § 1692e — the same conduct (e.g., demanding unauthorized fees) can simultaneously constitute a false representation and an unfair practice. Practitioners should routinely plead both sections when the underlying facts support dual theories.

This file also covers two related sections often litigated alongside § 1692f: § 1692h (payment allocation when a consumer owes multiple debts to the same collector) and § 1692i (venue restrictions on legal actions brought by debt collectors).

---

## The General Prohibition — § 1692f (Opening Clause)

**Statutory text:** "A debt collector may not use unfair or unconscionable means to collect or attempt to collect any debt. Without limiting the general application of the foregoing, the following conduct is a violation of this section..."

**Key interpretive points:**
- "Unfair" and "unconscionable" are distinct concepts — a practice may be unfair (i.e., substantially injurious to consumers without countervailing benefit) without reaching the level of unconscionability, and either is independently sufficient
- The catch-all applies to conduct not enumerated in (1)-(8)
- Courts look to the totality of the circumstances, including the vulnerability of the consumer and the egregiousness of the collector's conduct
- Unlike § 1692e, which focuses on deception, § 1692f focuses on exploitation and overreaching — even truthful practices can be "unfair"

## Specifically Enumerated Violations — § 1692f(1) through § 1692f(8)

### § 1692f(1) — Collecting Unauthorized Amounts

"The collection of any amount (including any interest, fee, charge, or expense incidental to the principal obligation) unless such amount is expressly authorized by the debt instrument creating the debt or permitted by law."

**This is the most commonly litigated subsection of § 1692f.**

**Two-part authorization test:** The collector may only demand amounts that are EITHER:
1. Expressly authorized by the debt instrument (contract, promissory note, credit agreement), OR
2. Permitted by applicable law (statute, court order, or rule)

**Common violations:**
- Adding collection fees or "collection costs" not authorized by the underlying contract
- Charging interest at a rate exceeding the contractual rate or applicable statutory rate
- Demanding attorney fees when the contract contains no attorney-fee provision
- Adding "convenience fees" for electronic payment or pay-by-phone transactions not authorized by the original agreement
- Compounding interest when the agreement provides for simple interest
- Demanding post-judgment interest at an incorrect rate
- Including NSF/bounced check fees exceeding the contractually or statutorily authorized amount
- Charging "account maintenance fees" or "administration fees" not in the original agreement

**Proving the violation:**
- Obtain the original credit agreement (often available through CFPB credit card agreement database for credit cards)
- Compare the amount demanded to the contractual terms
- Identify each component of the demanded amount (principal, interest, fees, costs)
- Check applicable state law on allowable fees and interest rates
- Review any assignment or purchase agreement to determine what the debt buyer acquired

**Case law:**
- *Jeter v. Credit Bureau, Inc.*, 760 F.2d 1168 (11th Cir. 1985) — Demand for collection fees not authorized by the underlying instrument violated § 1692f(1)
- *Tuttle v. Equifax Check Services, Inc.*, 190 F.3d 9 (2d Cir. 1999) — Service charges imposed on dishonored checks that exceeded the amount authorized by state law violated § 1692f(1)

**Practice tip:** Many debt buyers acquire portfolios at pennies on the dollar and add fees or interest that the original creditor never charged. Request the complete payment history and the bill of sale/assignment documents in discovery. The assignment often specifies what the buyer purchased — if it acquired only the principal balance, subsequent interest and fee charges may be unauthorized.

### § 1692f(2) — Accepting Post-Dated Checks by More Than Five Days Without Notice

"The acceptance from any person of a check or other payment instrument postdated by more than five days unless such person is notified in writing of the debt collector's intent to deposit such check or instrument not more than ten nor less than three business days prior to such deposit."

**Requirements:**
- If the collector accepts a post-dated check dated more than 5 days in the future, the collector MUST provide written notice of intent to deposit between 3 and 10 business days before depositing it
- The notice must be in writing — oral notice is insufficient
- The window is "business days" — weekends and federal holidays do not count

**Purpose:** Prevents collectors from obtaining post-dated checks and depositing them early, triggering overdraft fees or bounced-check charges before the consumer has arranged funds.

**Practice tip:** If the consumer gave a post-dated check and the collector deposited it without the required notice period, the consumer may have both a § 1692f(2) claim and actual damages (overdraft fees, returned check fees, cascading bounced payments).

---

### § 1692f(3) — Soliciting Post-Dated Checks for Threat Purposes

"The solicitation by a debt collector of any postdated check or other postdated payment instrument for the purpose of threatening or instituting criminal prosecution."

**This subsection prohibits two-step schemes:**
1. The collector solicits a post-dated check from the consumer
2. The collector then uses the bounced check as the basis for a criminal prosecution threat or referral

**Practice tip:** This is relatively rare in modern practice but may arise with check-cashing operations or payday lenders who use criminal "hot check" statutes as collection leverage.

---

### § 1692f(4) — Depositing or Threatening to Deposit Post-Dated Checks Prematurely

"Depositing or threatening to deposit any postdated check or other postdated payment instrument prior to the date on such check or instrument."

**This is a strict prohibition** — no notice can cure premature deposit. If the check is dated June 15 and the collector deposits it on June 10, this subsection is violated per se.

---

### § 1692f(5) — Causing Communication Charges

"Causing charges to be made to any person for communications by concealment of the true purpose of the communication. Such charges include, but are not limited to, collect telephone calls and telegram fees."

**Examples:**
- Making collect calls to the consumer without disclosing the purpose
- Causing the consumer to incur toll charges by calling back a number without knowing it is a long-distance or premium-rate line
- Sending communications via methods that incur charges to the recipient (e.g., COD deliveries, certified mail requiring payment)
- Using premium-rate callback numbers

**Modern application:** This subsection may apply to digital communications that trigger data charges or to practices designed to cause the consumer to call back premium-rate numbers.

---

### § 1692f(6) — Taking or Threatening Nonjudicial Action on Property Without Right or Intent

"Taking or threatening to take any nonjudicial action to effect dispossession or disablement of property if — (A) there is no present right to possession of the property claimed as collateral through an enforceable security interest; (B) there is no present intention to take possession of the property; or (C) the property is exempt by law from such dispossession or disablement."

**Three independent grounds for violation:**
1. No enforceable security interest — the collector claims to have a lien or security interest but does not, or the interest has been extinguished (paid off, released, time-barred)
2. No present intention — the collector threatens repossession but has no actual plan to repossess (used as a pressure tactic)
3. Exempt property — the property is exempt from seizure under applicable state or federal law

**Examples:**
- Threatening to repossess a vehicle when the debt is unsecured
- Threatening to take household goods claimed under a blanket lien that is unenforceable under FTC rules
- Threatening to seize Social Security funds (exempt under 42 U.S.C. § 407)
- Threatening to take tools of the trade or other exempt personal property
- Threatening to disable a vehicle (e.g., GPS kill switch) without a valid security interest

**Practice tip:** Many debt buyers acquire debts without the accompanying security interests — the UCC Article 9 requirements for perfection and assignment of security interests are often not met in bulk debt sales. Request the chain of title for any claimed security interest.

---

### § 1692f(7) — Postcards

"Communicating with a consumer regarding a debt by post card."

**This is a flat prohibition** — no debt collection communication may be sent on a postcard. The purpose is privacy: postcards can be read by anyone who handles the mail (letter carriers, roommates, family members).

**Practice tip:** This includes any communication viewable without opening an envelope. Some collectors have violated this by using windowed envelopes that display collection language visible through the window.

---

### § 1692f(8) — Deceptive Envelope Markings

"Using any language or symbol, other than the debt collector's address, on any envelope when communicating with a consumer by use of the mails or by telegram, except that a debt collector may use his business name if such name does not indicate that he is in the debt collection business."

**What is prohibited:**
- Any language on the envelope (other than the return address) that reveals the nature of the communication
- Using a business name that reveals the debt collection purpose (e.g., "ABC Collections" or "National Debt Recovery Services")
- Markings like "PAST DUE," "URGENT — COLLECTION NOTICE," or "FINAL WARNING"
- Symbols associated with debt collection

**What is permitted:**
- The collector's address (without a revealing business name)
- A business name that does not indicate debt collection (e.g., "Midland Credit Management" — courts split on whether such names are revealing)
- Standard postal markings

**Practice tip:** Examine envelopes carefully. Some collectors use "teaser" language ("TIME SENSITIVE MATERIAL," "IMPORTANT LEGAL DOCUMENT") that, while not explicitly saying "debt collection," creates a risk of disclosure to third parties or may violate the spirit of the provision.

## The Catch-All — Unfair Practices Not Specifically Enumerated

Courts have found the following practices violate § 1692f's general prohibition even though they do not fit neatly within subsections (1)-(8):

### Convenience Fees and Payment Processing Fees
- Charging a "convenience fee" for paying by credit card, debit card, or electronic payment when the original agreement does not authorize such fees
- Charging for payment processing that benefits the collector (faster collection) rather than the consumer
- Adding "technology fees" or "portal access fees" for online payment options
- The CFPB has taken enforcement actions against collectors imposing undisclosed convenience fees

### Credit Card Surcharges
- Requiring consumers to pay by credit card and adding a surcharge violates § 1692f(1) if not authorized by the underlying agreement
- Some states independently prohibit credit card surcharges, providing an additional basis for claiming the charge is not "permitted by law"

### Improper Application of Payments
- Applying payments to fees or interest before principal when the agreement specifies a different application order
- Applying payments to disputed amounts rather than undisputed amounts
- Misallocating payments across multiple accounts (see § 1692h below)

### Unfair Litigation Practices
- Filing suit in an improper venue (see § 1692i below)
- Obtaining default judgments through inadequate service of process
- Filing affidavits based on robo-signed documents without personal knowledge
- Seeking excessive post-judgment discovery designed to harass

### Miscellaneous Unfair Practices
- Contacting consumers known to be represented by counsel
- Refusing to provide receipts for payments made
- Cashing settlement checks and then continuing to collect the remaining balance
- Reporting to CRAs without providing required dispute notices

## § 1692h — Multiple Debts: Payment Allocation

**Statutory text:** "If any consumer owes multiple debts and makes any single payment to any debt collector with respect to such debts, such debt collector may not apply such payment to any debt which is disputed by the consumer and, where applicable, shall apply such payment in accordance with the consumer's directions."

**Key rules:**
1. **Disputed debts:** If the consumer has disputed one of multiple debts owed to the same collector, the collector CANNOT apply a single payment to the disputed debt
2. **Consumer direction:** Where the consumer directs how a payment should be applied (e.g., "apply this to Account A"), the collector must follow those directions
3. **No direction + no dispute:** If the consumer provides no direction and no debt is disputed, the collector has discretion in allocation

**Practical importance:**
- Consumers who owe multiple debts to a single collector should always specify in writing which account their payment should apply to
- If a consumer disputes one debt and the collector applies an undesignated payment to the disputed debt, that is a per se § 1692h violation
- This prevents collectors from strategically applying payments to debts where they earn higher fees or commissions while leaving lower-fee accounts unpaid

**Practice tip:** In discovery, request the collector's payment application records. If the consumer disputed one account and the collector allocated payments to that account, you have a clear § 1692h violation. Also look for situations where the collector applied payments contrary to written consumer instructions.

## § 1692i — Venue Restrictions for Legal Actions

**Statutory text (paraphrased):** A debt collector who brings any legal action on a debt against any consumer shall bring such action only:
- **(a)(1)** In the case of an action to enforce an interest in real property securing the consumer's obligation — in the judicial district or similar legal entity where the property is located; or
- **(a)(2)** In the case of any other action — in the judicial district or similar legal entity (i) in which the consumer signed the contract sued upon, or (ii) in which the consumer resides at the commencement of the action.

**Key limitations:**
- The venue restriction applies only to "debt collectors" (not original creditors collecting their own debts)
- For real property actions: venue is where the property is located (straightforward)
- For all other actions: venue must be where the contract was signed OR where the consumer currently lives
- Forum selection clauses in the original contract do NOT override § 1692i — the FDCPA preempts contractual venue provisions that would require the consumer to litigate in an inconvenient forum

**Common violations:**
- Filing suit in the county where the creditor's headquarters is located rather than where the consumer resides
- Filing in a distant courthouse even within the same state, imposing travel burden on the consumer
- Relying on a forum selection clause in the credit agreement to file in a distant venue
- Filing in a court near the debt collector's attorney rather than near the consumer

**Case law:**
- *Suesz v. Med-1 Solutions, LLC*, 757 F.3d 636 (7th Cir. 2014) (en banc) — "Judicial district or similar legal entity" means the smallest geographic unit with court jurisdiction (typically a county or borough), not a larger judicial circuit or federal district. Filing in the wrong county within a state violates § 1692i.

**Practice tip:** If a client has had a default judgment entered in a distant county, investigate whether the filing venue violated § 1692i. This can support both a motion to vacate the default judgment AND an affirmative FDCPA counterclaim. Check: (1) where the consumer signed the contract, (2) where the consumer resided when suit was filed, and (3) whether the filing location matches either permitted venue.

## Relationship Between § 1692f and § 1692e

The same conduct frequently violates both sections:

| Conduct | § 1692e Theory | § 1692f Theory |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| Demanding unauthorized fees | § 1692e(2)(A) — false amount | § 1692f(1) — collecting unauthorized amounts |
| Threatening repossession without right | § 1692e(4)/(5) — threat of action cannot take | § 1692f(6) — nonjudicial action without right |
| Deceptive envelope markings | § 1692e(9)/(10) — false/deceptive means | § 1692f(8) — impermissible envelope language |
| Filing suit in wrong venue | § 1692e(10) — deceptive means to collect | § 1692i — venue violation |
| Adding undisclosed convenience fees | § 1692e(2)(A) — false amount | § 1692f(1) — unauthorized amount |

**Practice tip:** Always plead both sections when the facts support it. Courts routinely permit dual recovery theories, and some jurisdictions treat each violated section as supporting independent statutory damage findings.

## Proving § 1692f in Litigation

### Evidence to Gather
- The original debt instrument (contract, agreement, promissory note)
- The collector's demand letters showing the total amount demanded and itemization
- Payment receipts, cancelled checks, and bank statements showing amounts paid
- The collector's internal account records showing how amounts were calculated
- State law on permitted fees and interest for the type of debt at issue
- The assignment/purchase agreement showing what the debt buyer acquired
- Phone records showing any collect calls (for § 1692f(5))
- Envelopes from collection correspondence (for § 1692f(7)/(8))
- Docket sheets showing where suits were filed (for § 1692i)

### Discovery Targets
- Collector's fee schedules and policies for adding charges to accounts
- Training materials on what amounts may be demanded
- Agreements between the collector and the original creditor regarding fees
- Payment processing vendor contracts (for convenience fee claims)
- Records of all amounts charged to the consumer's account beyond the original balance
- Collector's litigation policies regarding venue selection

### Damages
- **Actual damages:** Overdraft fees from premature check deposits; excess amounts paid by consumer; credit damage from inflated reported balances; costs of defending improperly-venued lawsuits
- **Statutory damages:** Up to $1,000 per action under § 1692k(a)(2)(A)
- **Attorney fees:** Mandatory for prevailing plaintiffs under § 1692k(a)(3)
- **Restitution:** Some courts permit recovery of unauthorized amounts collected

## Practice Tips for Common Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Collector Demands Amount Exceeding Contract
1. Obtain the original credit agreement (CFPB database, client records, or subpoena to original creditor)
2. Calculate the correct balance using contractual terms (principal + authorized interest + authorized fees)
3. Compare to the collector's demand
4. Any excess = violation of both § 1692f(1) and § 1692e(2)(A)

### Scenario 2: Collector Charges Convenience Fee
1. Check the original agreement for any fee authorization
2. Check applicable state law on permissible collection fees
3. If the fee is not expressly authorized by either source = § 1692f(1) violation
4. If the collector failed to disclose the fee before processing payment = potential § 1692e(10) violation

### Scenario 3: Threat to Repossess
1. Verify whether the collector holds an enforceable security interest
2. Check UCC filings for perfection
3. Verify the security interest was properly assigned in the debt sale
4. Check whether the property is exempt under state law
5. Any deficiency in the security interest chain = § 1692f(6) violation

### Scenario 4: Suit Filed in Distant Court
1. Identify where the consumer signed the contract
2. Identify where the consumer resided when suit was filed
3. If the suit was filed somewhere else = § 1692i violation
4. Consider motion to dismiss/transfer plus affirmative FDCPA counterclaim

## Regulation F Overlay — 12 C.F.R. § 1006.22

Regulation F supplements § 1692f by:
- Clarifying that certain fees are not "expressly authorized" merely because a contract has a general "costs of collection" clause — the authorization must be specific
- Addressing electronic payment processing fees and the conditions under which they may be imposed
- Providing guidance on when post-dated check practices trigger notification requirements
- Confirming that § 1692f's prohibitions apply to modern payment methods (ACH, digital wallets, mobile payments) in the same manner as traditional checks

## Checklist: Evaluating a § 1692f Claim

- [ ] Is the defendant a "debt collector" under § 1692a(6)?
- [ ] Was the conduct "in connection with the collection of a debt"?
- [ ] Identify the specific unfair or unconscionable practice
- [ ] Does it fit within § 1692f(1)-(8)? (If yes, per se violation)
- [ ] If not enumerated, would the conduct shock the conscience or substantially injure the consumer?
- [ ] For § 1692f(1): Is there a debt instrument? Does it authorize the amounts demanded? Does state law permit them?
- [ ] For § 1692f(6): Is there an enforceable security interest? Was there intent? Is the property exempt?
- [ ] For § 1692f(8): Examine the actual envelope — any impermissible markings?
- [ ] Check for § 1692h (payment allocation) and § 1692i (venue) violations in the same case
- [ ] Has the one-year statute of limitations under § 1692k(d) run?
- [ ] Calculate actual damages (overdraft fees, excess payments, travel costs for improper venue)
