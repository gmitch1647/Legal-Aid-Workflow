\# FDCPA Communications with Consumers — 15 U.S.C. § 1692c  
  
\*\*Topic:\*\* Communications in connection with debt collection — time, place, manner, and third-party restrictions  
\*\*Primary statute:\*\* 15 U.S.C. § 1692c  
\*\*Regulation F cross-references:\*\* 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6 (communications in connection with debt collection); 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14 (harassing, oppressive, or abusive conduct, including the 7-in-7 call frequency presumption); 12 C.F.R. § 1006.22 (unfair or unconscionable means)  
\*\*Related case law:\*\* \*Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Mgmt. Servs., Inc.\*, 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc); \*Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA\*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010)  
\*\*Last updated:\*\* May 16, 2026  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 1. Overview  
  
Section 1692c is — together with § 1692e and § 1692g — one of the three most-litigated provisions of the FDCPA. It governs \*when, where, and with whom\* a debt collector may communicate in connection with collecting a debt. It is structured in four operational subsections:  
  
\- § 1692c(a) — restrictions on direct communications with the consumer (time/place; attorney representation; workplace)  
\- § 1692c(b) — restrictions on communications with third parties  
\- § 1692c(c) — consumer's right to demand cessation of communications  
\- § 1692c(d) — expanded definition of "consumer" for purposes of § 1692c  
  
Regulation F, in effect since November 30, 2021, builds on § 1692c at 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6 (communications generally), § 1006.14 (the 7-in-7 telephone-call frequency presumption, opt-out requirements for email and text, and the meaningful-disclosure rule), and § 1006.22 (unfair or unconscionable means, including unsolicited use of a consumer's social-media profile in connection with debt collection).  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 2. § 1692c(a)(1) — Time and Place Restrictions  
  
"Without the prior consent of the consumer given directly to the debt collector or the express permission of a court of competent jurisdiction, a debt collector may not communicate with a consumer in connection with the collection of any debt —  
  
(1) at any unusual time or place or a time or place known or which should be known to be inconvenient to the consumer. In the absence of knowledge of circumstances to the contrary, a debt collector shall assume that the convenient time for communicating with a consumer is after 8 o'clock antemeridian and before 9 o'clock postmeridian, local time at the consumer's location." 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(a)(1).  
  
\*\*Plain-English explanation.\*\* Default convenient window is 8:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m. \*local\* to the consumer. Communications outside that window — and communications at any other "unusual" or known-inconvenient time or place — are prohibited unless the consumer consents or a court orders otherwise.  
  
\*\*Key issues:\*\*  
  
\- \*\*Local time.\*\* The "local time at the consumer's location" anchor means a New York–based collector calling a California consumer must respect Pacific time. Auto-dialer systems must time-zone the destination, not the dialer's location.  
\- \*\*Place restrictions.\*\* "Place" includes hospitals (a consumer admitted as an inpatient), houses of worship, and other contexts where collection contact would be plainly inappropriate. If the consumer has stated that contact at a specific place (e.g., a relative's home) is inconvenient, that place becomes off-limits.  
\- \*\*"Should be known."\*\* Constructive knowledge applies. If the consumer's account file reflects a night shift or unusual hours, calling at noon may be a violation even though noon is in the default safe window.  
\- \*\*Reg F parallel.\*\* 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6(b)(1)(i) restates the rule. Comment 1006.6(b)(1)-1 makes clear the default 8 a.m.–9 p.m. window is rebuttable by knowledge of inconvenience.  
  
\*\*Reg F overlay — electronic communications time-of-day.\*\* Reg F applies the 8 a.m.–9 p.m. convenient-time restriction equally to email and text messages. \*See\* 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6(b)(1). Delivery time, not composition time, controls.

\#\# 3. § 1692c(a)(2) — Represented-by-Attorney Rule  
  
"(2) if the debt collector knows the consumer is represented by an attorney with respect to such debt and has knowledge of, or can readily ascertain, such attorney's name and address, unless the attorney fails to respond within a reasonable period of time to a communication from the debt collector or unless the attorney consents to direct communication with the consumer." 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(a)(2).  
  
\*\*Plain-English explanation.\*\* Once the collector knows (or could readily learn) that an attorney represents the consumer on the \*specific\* debt, the collector must communicate exclusively with the attorney about that debt — unless the attorney does not respond in a reasonable time or affirmatively consents to direct contact.  
  
\*\*Litigation issues:\*\*  
  
\- The representation must be "with respect to such debt." General representation in an unrelated matter does not trigger the rule.  
\- "Readily ascertain" imports a duty of reasonable diligence once representation is signaled.  
\- Mass-tort consumer attorneys frequently send "blanket" representation letters covering all of a consumer's debt. Whether such letters trigger § 1692c(a)(2) for every account turns on whether the letter identifies the specific debts and creditors.  
\- "Reasonable period" to respond is fact-specific; courts have found two-week silence sufficient in some contexts but not others.  
\- Statutory court-filed pleadings served on the consumer's lawyer are not communications "with the consumer."  
  
\*\*Practical compliance.\*\* Maintain a representation-flag field in the collection system. Block all direct-consumer dialer activity on accounts with active representation. Suppress validation-notice re-sends to the consumer once an attorney appears.  
  
\*\*Reg F parallel.\*\* 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6(b)(2).

\#\# 4. § 1692c(a)(3) — Workplace Rule  
  
"(3) at the consumer's place of employment if the debt collector knows or has reason to know that the consumer's employer prohibits the consumer from receiving such communication." 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(a)(3).  
  
\*\*Plain-English explanation.\*\* Workplace contact is permitted only if the collector lacks knowledge — actual or constructive — that the employer prohibits such communications.  
  
\*\*Litigation issues:\*\*  
  
\- The trigger is the \*employer's\* prohibition, not the consumer's preference. A consumer who tells the collector "please don't call me at work" creates a § 1692c(a)(1) "known-inconvenient place" basis for cessation independent of (a)(3).  
\- "Reason to know" includes industry-wide prohibitions on personal calls (security-cleared facilities; certain regulated workplaces).  
\- A single workplace call is permissible unless prior notice of the employer's prohibition exists.  
\- Once the consumer tells the collector at the workplace number that the employer disallows such calls, continued calls violate § 1692c(a)(3).  
  
\*\*Reg F parallel.\*\* 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6(b)(1)(iii).

\#\# 5. § 1692c(b) — Third-Party Communications  
  
"Except as provided in section 1692b of this title, without the prior consent of the consumer given directly to the debt collector, or the express permission of a court of competent jurisdiction, or as reasonably necessary to effectuate a postjudgment judicial remedy, a debt collector may not communicate, in connection with the collection of any debt, with any person other than the consumer, his attorney, a consumer reporting agency if otherwise permitted by law, the creditor, the attorney of the creditor, or the attorney of the debt collector." 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(b).  
  
\*\*Plain-English explanation.\*\* The collector may communicate about a debt only with:  
  
1\. The consumer (and the § 1692c(d) extended consumer list);  
2\. The consumer's attorney;  
3\. A consumer reporting agency, if otherwise permitted by law (FCRA);  
4\. The creditor;  
5\. The creditor's attorney;  
6\. The collector's attorney.  
  
All other third-party communications are prohibited, with three narrow exceptions:  
  
\- Consumer's prior consent given \*directly\* to the collector (not to the creditor — although Reg F has refined this);  
\- Court permission;  
\- Postjudgment-remedy effectuation (e.g., contacting an employer for wage-garnishment service).  
  
Section 1692b provides a separate carve-out \*only\* for location-information acquisition. \*See\* file \`fdcpa\_03\_Acquisition\_of\_Location\_Information.md\`.  
  
\#\#\# 5.1 The \*Hunstein\* Letter-Vendor Controversy  
  
In \*Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Management Services, Inc.\*, 994 F.3d 1341 (11th Cir. 2021), an Eleventh Circuit panel held that a debt collector violated § 1692c(b) by transmitting consumer account information to a third-party mail-house vendor that printed and mailed dunning letters. The decision triggered immediate operational alarm across the industry because every collection-letter operation uses letter vendors.  
  
The Eleventh Circuit re-heard the case and ultimately, sitting en banc, vacated on Article III standing grounds. The en banc court held that "the disclosure of \[the plaintiff's\] debt-related information to \[the letter vendor\] did not result in any cognizable harm" — leaning heavily on \*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez\*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), and concluding that bare disclosure to a vendor, without dissemination to the public, was insufficient to confer standing. \*Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Mgmt. Servs., Inc.\*, 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc).  
  
\*\*Current state.\*\* The en banc \*Hunstein\* decision rests on standing, not merits. Other circuits and district courts have split: some have followed the standing rationale, while a minority have found Article III standing on letter-vendor facts. \*See, e.g.\*, post-\*Hunstein\* district-court decisions in the Ninth Circuit finding standing in some letter-vendor matters. The merits question — whether vendor disclosure is a § 1692c(b) "communication" with a "person other than" the listed parties — remains open in most circuits.  
  
\*\*Practical compliance.\*\* Most letter vendors now operate under written confidentiality agreements that arguably bring the relationship within the "creditor's attorney" or agency-of-collector framework. Many also operate behind end-to-end encryption to limit any individual human's access. Defense practitioners should pursue early standing dismissals where letter-vendor disclosure is the only alleged third-party communication.  
  
\#\#\# 5.2 The Six Authorized Recipients  
  
\- \*\*Consumer's attorney.\*\* Once representation is established, the attorney is the proper contact for substantive collection communications.  
\- \*\*Consumer reporting agency.\*\* Furnishing under FCRA § 1681s-2 is a permissible communication under § 1692c(b). Reporting must comply with separate FDCPA rules (e.g., reporting a disputed debt without disclosing the dispute violates § 1692e(8)).  
\- \*\*Creditor and creditor's attorney.\*\* Routine in account assignment and litigation contexts.  
\- \*\*Collector's attorney.\*\* Routine.  
  
\#\#\# 5.3 Consumer Consent  
  
Consent must be given \*directly\* to the debt collector. Pre-default consent buried in original credit-card terms-of-service does not satisfy § 1692c(b) — although Reg F has carved out a separate path for electronic-communications consent (see § 8 below).

\#\# 6. § 1692c(c) — Cease-and-Desist  
  
"If a consumer notifies a debt collector in writing that the consumer refuses to pay a debt or that the consumer wishes the debt collector to cease further communication with the consumer, the debt collector shall not communicate further with the consumer with respect to such debt, except —  
  
(1) to advise the consumer that the debt collector's further efforts are being terminated;  
  
(2) to notify the consumer that the debt collector or creditor may invoke specified remedies which are ordinarily invoked by such debt collector or creditor; or  
  
(3) where applicable, to notify the consumer that the debt collector or creditor intends to invoke a specified remedy.  
  
If such notice from the consumer is made by mail, notification shall be complete upon receipt." 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c).  
  
\*\*Plain-English explanation.\*\* A \*written\* "refusal to pay" or "stop contacting me" notice from the consumer triggers a hard stop on collection communications. After receipt, only three narrow categories of follow-up are permitted: (1) confirming cessation, (2) notifying of remedies the collector or creditor \*may\* invoke, or (3) notifying of remedies the collector or creditor \*intends\* to invoke.  
  
\*\*Litigation issues:\*\*  
  
\- The notice must be \*in writing\*. Oral "stop calling me" is not a § 1692c(c) notice — but it may be a § 1692c(a)(1) "known-inconvenient" trigger and (if calls continue) a § 1692d(5) harassment basis.  
\- Email may qualify as "writing" depending on facts and any Reg F-relevant consent context.  
\- The "specified remedies" exception is narrow. Courts have held it does not authorize a generic dunning letter framed as "remedy notice." It authorizes one notice that the collector will, e.g., file suit, repossess, or refer the matter.  
\- Filing a state-court collection complaint \*is\* the invocation of a remedy; service of process is not a § 1692c(c) violation. \*See Heintz v. Jenkins\*, 514 U.S. 291 (1995).  
\- "Notification complete upon receipt" — collectors cannot rely on a fictitious mailbox rule to deny knowledge. Receipt is the trigger.  
  
\*\*Compliance:\*\* Hard-block accounts on cease-and-desist receipt. Pre-write three narrow allowed follow-up letters and confirm in the file which (if any) is being used.

\#\# 7. § 1692c(d) — Expanded "Consumer" Definition for § 1692c  
  
"For the purpose of this section, the term 'consumer' includes the consumer's spouse, parent (if the consumer is a minor), guardian, executor, or administrator." 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(d).  
  
\*\*Plain-English explanation.\*\* Section 1692c's \*protections\* (time/place rules, attorney rule, workplace rule, cease-and-desist rule) and its third-party-contact \*categorization\* extend to a defined family circle:  
  
\- Spouse;  
\- Parent of a minor consumer;  
\- Guardian;  
\- Executor of the consumer's estate;  
\- Administrator of the consumer's estate.  
  
\*\*Important implications:\*\*  
  
\- Communications \*with\* these persons about the consumer's debt are \*not\* third-party communications under § 1692c(b) — they are communications "with the consumer." That permits estate-administration and household contact.  
\- But the \*protections\* of § 1692c(a) apply: no calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local to the spouse; no contact at a known-inconvenient place; no contact if the spouse is represented by counsel on the debt; cease-and-desist rights apply.  
\- The list is exclusive. Adult children, siblings, and roommates are \*not\* "consumers" under § 1692c(d); communicating with them about the debt is a § 1692c(b) third-party violation absent consent.  
\- Notably, the consumer's \*attorney\* is not listed in § 1692c(d) — the attorney is separately authorized as a recipient by § 1692c(b).

\#\# 8. Regulation F Overlay — 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6, § 1006.14, § 1006.22  
  
\#\#\# 8.1 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6 — Communications in Connection with Debt Collection  
  
Regulation F § 1006.6 is the CFPB's operationalization of § 1692c. It restates the time/place, attorney, workplace, third-party, and cease-and-desist rules and adds detailed coverage of \*electronic communications\* (email and text), which the 1977 FDCPA did not address.  
  
\*\*Key Reg F additions:\*\*  
  
\- \*\*Inconvenient electronic-communication channels.\*\* A consumer may identify a specific email address or phone number for text as inconvenient; the collector must observe. 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6(b)(1).  
\- \*\*Workplace email.\*\* A collector "must not communicate or attempt to communicate with a consumer by sending an electronic communication to an email address that the debt collector knows is provided to the consumer by the consumer's employer" unless certain conditions are met. 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6(d)(4)(ii).  
\- \*\*Procedures for unauthorized third-party email/text disclosure.\*\* Reg F § 1006.6(d)(3) provides a procedures-based safe harbor: if the collector follows reasonable procedures designed to ensure that an email or text reaches the consumer alone (and not third parties), inadvertent disclosure to a third party will not violate § 1692c(b). The safe harbor requires specific advance-notice and opt-out elements.  
\- \*\*Opt-out requirement for electronic communications.\*\* Each electronic communication must include "a reasonable and simple method by which the consumer can opt out of receiving further electronic communications" at the email address or phone number used. 12 C.F.R. § 1006.6(e).  
  
\#\#\# 8.2 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14 — Harassing, Oppressive, or Abusive Conduct (including 7-in-7 limits)  
  
Reg F implements § 1692d via § 1006.14. Of particular relevance to § 1692c communication patterns:  
  
\- \*\*7-in-7 call frequency.\*\* A debt collector is \*presumed\* to comply with § 1692d(5) if it places telephone calls to a particular person in connection with a particular debt no more than seven times within a seven-day period, and not within seven days after a telephone conversation with that person about the debt. 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2). The presumption is rebuttable; exceeding the limits creates a presumption of \*violation\*.  
\- \*\*Per debt, not per consumer.\*\* The presumption operates per particular debt. A consumer who owes multiple debts to the same collector may receive more than seven total calls in seven days without breaching the presumption.  
\- \*\*Voicemails and limited-content messages count\*\* as call attempts. \*See\* CFPB official commentary at Comment 1006.14(b)(2)(i)-1.  
\- \*\*Inbound calls\*\* from the consumer do not count.  
  
\#\#\# 8.3 12 C.F.R. § 1006.22 — Unfair or Unconscionable Means  
  
Reg F § 1006.22 implements § 1692f and includes the social-media restriction: a debt collector cannot post or communicate on a publicly viewable portion of a social-media platform in connection with collection of a debt. 12 C.F.R. § 1006.22(f)(4). Private-message contact on social media is governed by the broader § 1006.6 communications rules.

\#\# 9. Recurring CFPB Supervisory Highlights Issues  
  
The CFPB has flagged the following recurring § 1692c / § 1006.6 compliance failures in published Supervisory Highlights and consent orders:  
  
\- Continued contact with the consumer after a documented cease-and-desist (§ 1692c(c)).  
\- Calls to consumers before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time due to inadequate time-zone routing in dialer software (§ 1692c(a)(1)).  
\- Failure to suppress consumer contact upon notice of attorney representation (§ 1692c(a)(2)).  
\- Sending dunning emails to known work email addresses (§ 1006.6(d)(4)(ii)).  
\- Failing to include opt-out language in electronic communications (§ 1006.6(e)).  
\- Disclosing debts to neighbors, family, and employers beyond § 1692a(7) location information.

\#\# 10. Practical Litigation and Compliance Notes  
  
\*\*For plaintiff counsel:\*\*  
  
\- Plead the precise subsection violated; § 1692c is a strict-liability statute and each subsection supports a separate count.  
\- Discover the call-detail records and dialer-system audit logs to establish time-zone-routing failures.  
\- Pair § 1692c(c) cease-and-desist claims with documented written notices and tracking-receipt evidence.  
\- For represented-consumer cases, lock in the timeline of the attorney representation letter and the first post-letter contact.  
\- For workplace cases, develop facts about employer policies and the consumer's prior request that the workplace not be called.  
  
\*\*For defense counsel:\*\*  
  
\- The "directly to the debt collector" consent requirement of § 1692c(b) is a frequent plaintiff vulnerability — many consumer "consents" were given to the original creditor or a prior servicer.  
\- \*Hunstein\*-standing arguments remain viable in many circuits on letter-vendor and similar internal-disclosure theories.  
\- Reg F § 1006.6(d)(3) procedures safe harbor is documentation-intensive but provides meaningful protection for email/text channels.  
\- Cease-and-desist exception (3) ("intends to invoke a specified remedy") permits one closing notice — overuse converts the exception into the rule and exposes liability.  
  
\*\*Common operational defects:\*\*  
  
\- Time-zone routing keyed to area code (now unreliable due to portable numbers) instead of confirmed billing-address ZIP code.  
\- Manual representation flags not synchronized across multiple collection platforms.  
\- Cease-and-desist queues with manual review backlog producing post-notice contacts.

\#\# 11. Interaction with TCPA and State Mini-FDCPAs  
  
Section 1692c call-frequency, time, and electronic-communication issues frequently arise alongside Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) claims under 47 U.S.C. § 227. The TCPA imposes separate consent requirements for autodialed and prerecorded calls and texts. A single course of conduct can violate both statutes; pleading both is common practice.  
  
State consumer-protection statutes (California Rosenthal Act, New York General Business Law § 600 et seq., Texas Debt Collection Act) often incorporate § 1692c by reference or impose parallel standards with broader definitional scope (e.g., reaching original creditors).
