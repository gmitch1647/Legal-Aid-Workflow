# FDCPA Remedies, Damages, and Statute of Limitations (15 U.S.C. § 1692k)

**Topic:** Civil liability for FDCPA violations; actual damages; statutory damages; attorney fees; statute of limitations; class actions; standing  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692k (FDCPA § 813)  
**Cross-references:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692l (administrative enforcement); 28 U.S.C. § 1331 (federal question jurisdiction); Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330 (2016) (standing)  
**Last updated:** May 17, 2026  

---

## Introduction

Section 1692k provides the FDCPA's private right of action and is the engine that drives enforcement of the statute. Any debt collector who fails to comply with any provision of the FDCPA is liable to the affected consumer for actual damages, statutory damages, attorney fees, and costs. The availability of mandatory attorney fees to prevailing plaintiffs is a critical feature — it enables consumers who have suffered relatively small individual damages to obtain counsel and pursue claims that serve broader deterrence goals.

The FDCPA provides for a one-year statute of limitations, which has generated substantial litigation over when the limitations period begins to run and whether continuing violations can extend it.

---

## § 1692k(a) — Categories of Damages

### § 1692k(a)(1) — Actual Damages

**Statutory text:** "any actual damage sustained by such person as a result of such failure"

**Key features:**
- No cap on actual damages — the consumer may recover the full extent of provable harm
- Actual damages are compensatory — they aim to restore the consumer to the position they would have occupied but for the violation
- Available in both individual and class actions

**Types of actual damages recoverable:**

**Emotional distress:**
- Anxiety, stress, embarrassment, humiliation
- Sleep disruption, physical manifestations of stress
- Need not be accompanied by physical injury
- Testimony of the consumer alone may suffice — no medical records or expert testimony required (though they strengthen the claim)
- *Jacobson v. Healthcare Financial Services, Inc.*, 516 F.3d 85 (2d Cir. 2008) — Emotional distress damages available under FDCPA without proof of physical injury

**Financial harm:**
- Amounts overpaid due to false representations of the debt amount
- Overdraft fees from premature deposit of post-dated checks
- Late fees and credit damage caused by misapplied payments
- Lost wages from taking time off work to deal with collection harassment
- Travel costs incurred to defend a suit filed in an improper venue
- Attorney consultation fees incurred before filing the FDCPA action
- Costs of credit monitoring or repair necessitated by false credit reporting

**Credit damage:**
- Diminished credit score resulting from unauthorized or inaccurate reporting
- Higher interest rates on subsequent credit applications
- Denied credit, employment, or housing
- Quantifiable through before-and-after credit score comparison and interest differential calculations

**Out-of-pocket costs:**
- Costs of obtaining credit reports to verify false reporting
- Postage for dispute letters
- Phone charges from repeated calls
- Medical expenses for stress-related treatment

**Practice tip:** Document actual damages contemporaneously. Have clients keep a journal of emotional distress symptoms, save pay stubs showing missed work, retain bank statements showing overdraft fees, and obtain credit reports before and after the violation period.

### § 1692k(a)(2)(A) — Individual Statutory Damages

**Statutory text:** "in the case of any action by an individual, such additional damages as the court may allow, but not exceeding $1,000"

**Key features:**
- Discretionary with the court — "as the court may allow"
- Cap of $1,000 per action (not per violation)
- Available regardless of whether actual damages are proven
- Serves punitive/deterrent function
- The court considers factors listed in § 1692k(b) in determining the amount

**Important limitations:**
- The $1,000 cap applies per lawsuit, not per violation — even if a collector committed ten separate violations, the maximum statutory damages remain $1,000 in an individual action
- Statutory damages are "additional" to actual damages — a consumer may recover both actual damages (uncapped) plus up to $1,000 in statutory damages

---

### § 1692k(a)(2)(B) — Class Action Statutory Damages

**Statutory text:** "in the case of a class action, (i) such amount for each named plaintiff as could be recovered under subparagraph (A), and (ii) such amount as the court may allow for all other class members, without regard to a minimum individual recovery, not to exceed the lesser of $500,000 or 1 percent of the net worth of the debt collector"

**Key features:**
- Named plaintiffs can recover up to $1,000 each (same as individual actions)
- Absent class members share a pool capped at the lesser of: (a) $500,000 OR (b) 1% of the collector's net worth
- The court has discretion to set the total class award within that cap
- Individual class members may receive less than $1,000 (and often receive far less due to the pool being divided among many members)
- The court is not required to award the maximum — § 1692k(b) factors apply

**Class action damages calculation example:**
- Debt collector's net worth: $10 million
- 1% of net worth: $100,000
- $500,000 cap
- Maximum class recovery: $100,000 (lesser of the two)
- If class has 5,000 members: $100,000 / 5,000 = $20 per class member
- Named plaintiff also receives up to $1,000 individually

**Practice tip:** Discovery of the collector's net worth is essential in class actions. Request audited financial statements, balance sheets, and SEC filings (if publicly traded). The "net worth" is calculated at the time of trial, not the time of violation.

### § 1692k(a)(3) — Attorney Fees and Costs

**Statutory text:** "in the case of any successful action to enforce the foregoing liability, the costs of the action, together with a reasonable attorney's fee as determined by the court"

**Key features:**
- **Mandatory** for prevailing plaintiffs — the statute says "shall" not "may"
- Includes both attorney fees AND costs of the action
- "Successful action" means the plaintiff prevailed on the merits — need not win on every claim
- Attorney fees are the primary incentive for attorneys to take FDCPA cases, which typically involve small individual damages
- Fees are calculated using the lodestar method (reasonable hourly rate x reasonable hours expended)
- Fee awards often exceed the underlying damages — this is by design and permitted

**Lodestar calculation:**
- Reasonable hourly rate: determined by the prevailing market rate for attorneys of comparable skill and experience in the relevant community
- Reasonable hours: hours actually and necessarily expended on the litigation
- Possible enhancement: in rare circumstances (e.g., exceptional results, contingency risk), courts may apply a multiplier — but post-*Perdue v. Kenny A.*, 559 U.S. 542 (2010), enhancements are disfavored

**Costs recoverable:**
- Filing fees
- Service of process fees
- Deposition costs (reporter fees, transcripts)
- Expert witness fees (in some circuits)
- Copying and postage
- Travel expenses related to litigation
- Electronic discovery costs

**Prevailing defendant fees:**
- A debt collector who prevails may recover attorney fees from the plaintiff ONLY upon a finding that the action was brought "in bad faith and for the purpose of harassment." § 1692k(a)(3). This is an extremely high standard — mere failure to prevail does not expose the consumer to fee-shifting.

## § 1692k(b) — Factors for Determining Statutory Damages

**Statutory text:** "In determining the amount of liability in any action under subsection (a), the court shall consider, among other relevant factors — (1) in any individual action under subsection (a)(2)(A), the frequency and persistence of noncompliance by the debt collector, the nature of such noncompliance, and the extent to which such noncompliance was intentional; or (2) in any class action under subsection (a)(2)(B), the frequency and persistence of noncompliance by the debt collector, the nature of such noncompliance, the resources of the debt collector, the number of persons adversely affected, and the extent to which the debt collector's noncompliance was intentional."

**Individual action factors:**
1. Frequency and persistence of noncompliance (repeated violations vs. isolated incident)
2. Nature of the noncompliance (severity — threatening arrest vs. minor technical error)
3. Extent to which noncompliance was intentional (willful vs. negligent)

**Class action factors (all of the above, plus):**
4. Resources of the debt collector (ability to absorb the judgment)
5. Number of persons adversely affected (scope of harm)

**Practice tips for maximizing statutory damages:**
- Demonstrate a pattern of similar violations across multiple consumers (discovery of other complaints)
- Show the collector was previously sued for similar conduct or received regulatory warnings
- Prove the violation resulted from a deliberate business practice rather than an isolated error
- Present evidence of the collector's awareness that its conduct was problematic (training materials, compliance audits, prior litigation)

## § 1692k(d) — Statute of Limitations

**Statutory text:** "An action to enforce any liability created by this subchapter may be brought in any appropriate United States district court without regard to the amount in controversy, or in any other court of competent jurisdiction, within one year from the date on which the violation occurs."

### The One-Year Period

- The FDCPA statute of limitations is **one year from the date on which the violation occurs**
- This is among the shortest limitations periods in federal consumer protection law
- The claim must be FILED within one year — tolling doctrines may apply in limited circumstances

### Accrual: Discovery Rule vs. Occurrence Rule (Circuit Split)

**Occurrence rule (majority):** The limitations period begins to run when the violation occurs, regardless of when the consumer discovers it.
- The date the deceptive letter is received
- The date the unlawful phone call is made
- The date suit is filed in the wrong venue

**Discovery rule (minority/equitable tolling):** Some courts apply the discovery rule, beginning the limitations period when the consumer knew or should have known of the violation.
- Particularly relevant where the violation is not self-evident (e.g., overstated balance requiring comparison to contract terms)
- Courts reluctant to apply the discovery rule to facially apparent violations (e.g., missing mini-Miranda, threatening arrest)

**Equitable tolling:** Available in limited circumstances where:
- The consumer was prevented from filing by the collector's conduct (fraudulent concealment)
- The consumer exercised reasonable diligence but could not have discovered the violation
- Extraordinary circumstances beyond the consumer's control prevented timely filing

**Circuit positions:**
- Eleventh Circuit: Generally applies the occurrence rule but recognizes equitable tolling in appropriate circumstances
- Sixth Circuit: Has applied the discovery rule where violations were not facially apparent
- Ninth Circuit: Applies the occurrence rule with equitable tolling available
- Third Circuit: Occurrence rule with limited equitable tolling

---

### Continuing Violation Doctrine

**The question:** When a collector engages in a course of conduct spanning more than one year, does each individual act restart the limitations period?

**General rule:** Each discrete violation triggers its own one-year limitations period. A letter sent on January 1 and another letter sent on February 1 each have separate one-year periods.

**Continuing violation theory:** Some courts have held that where a collector's conduct constitutes a "continuing violation" — an ongoing pattern of unlawful behavior — the limitations period does not begin until the last violative act in the series.

**When courts apply the continuing violation doctrine:**
- Repeated harassing phone calls extending over time — the last call in the series can render earlier calls timely
- A pattern of false reporting to CRAs — each month the false report persists may be a new violation
- Collecting on a debt after dispute without verification — each collection attempt after the required cessation is a new violation

**When courts reject the continuing violation doctrine:**
- Each letter is a discrete communication with its own limitations period
- Filing suit in the wrong venue is a single act, not a continuing violation
- The initial failure to provide a validation notice is complete at the five-day mark

**Practice tip:** When a client's claim appears time-barred based on the first violative act, investigate whether subsequent acts continued within the one-year period. Document all contacts, and argue that each subsequent communication or collection attempt constitutes a fresh violation restarting the clock.

## Standing After Spokeo

### Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330 (2016)

The Supreme Court held that Article III standing requires a "concrete" injury, and that a bare procedural violation of a statute — without more — may not satisfy this requirement. The case was decided in the FCRA context but applies equally to FDCPA claims.

### Application to FDCPA Claims

**Claims where standing is generally not an issue:**
- Harassment (§ 1692d) — calls, threats, and abusive language cause concrete harm by definition
- False representations that induced payment (§ 1692e) — financial harm is concrete
- Collection of unauthorized amounts (§ 1692f(1)) — overpayment is concrete financial harm
- Filing suit in wrong venue (§ 1692i) — cost and burden of defending in wrong location

**Claims where standing may be contested:**
- Technical validation notice deficiencies (§ 1692g) where the consumer never intended to dispute
- Missing mini-Miranda (§ 1692e(11)) where the consumer already knew they were being contacted by a collector
- Minor inaccuracies in the stated amount that did not affect the consumer's behavior

### Demonstrating Concrete Harm

To establish standing post-*Spokeo*, FDCPA plaintiffs should demonstrate:
1. **Informational injury:** The consumer was deprived of information to which they were legally entitled, and this deprivation caused a downstream concrete effect (confusion, inability to exercise rights, payment of disputed amount)
2. **Financial harm:** The consumer paid an amount they would not have paid but for the violation
3. **Emotional harm:** The violation caused anxiety, stress, or humiliation
4. **Risk of real harm:** The violation created a material risk of concrete harm (e.g., a false credit report creates the risk of denied credit)

**Key post-Spokeo FDCPA decisions:**
- *Church v. Accretive Health, Inc.*, 654 F. App'x 990 (11th Cir. 2016) — Remanded for consideration of whether failure to provide validation notice caused concrete harm
- *Trichell v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 964 F.3d 990 (11th Cir. 2020) — A consumer who received a collection letter on a time-barred debt had standing because the letter created a risk of paying a debt that was not legally enforceable

## Jurisdiction and Venue

### Federal Question Jurisdiction

FDCPA claims arise under federal law and may be brought in "any appropriate United States district court without regard to the amount in controversy." § 1692k(d). No minimum amount-in-controversy requirement applies.

### State Court Jurisdiction

FDCPA claims may also be brought in "any other court of competent jurisdiction." § 1692k(d). This includes state courts of general jurisdiction. FDCPA claims filed in state court are removable to federal court under 28 U.S.C. § 1441 (federal question removal).

### Supplemental/Pendent State Claims

FDCPA claims are frequently paired with state-law claims (e.g., state debt collection statutes, UDAP claims, state-law harassment/IIED). Federal courts have supplemental jurisdiction over related state claims under 28 U.S.C. § 1367.

## Class Action Considerations

### When Class Actions Are Appropriate

FDCPA class actions are most effective where:
- The collector engaged in a uniform practice affecting many consumers (form letters, systemic fees, blanket policies)
- The violation is susceptible to common proof (same letter, same fee, same practice)
- Individual damages are small (making individual litigation uneconomical)
- The statutory damages cap provides meaningful aggregate recovery

### Class Certification Issues

**Rule 23(a) requirements:**
- Numerosity: Typically satisfied where the collector used a form letter or standard practice affecting hundreds or thousands of consumers
- Commonality/typicality: Satisfied where the violation stems from a uniform policy rather than individualized conduct
- Adequacy: Named plaintiff must have claims typical of the class and no conflicts

**Rule 23(b)(3) predominance:**
- Common questions predominate where the violation is standard (same letter, same fee structure)
- Individual issues may predominate where damages require consumer-specific proof (actual damages)
- Statutory damages classes are easier to certify because damages are determined by § 1692k(b) factors rather than individual proof

### Damages Calculation in Class Actions

1. Named plaintiffs: up to $1,000 each
2. Class members: share a pool capped at the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of net worth
3. Actual damages: individually assessed (may not be suitable for class treatment)
4. Attorney fees: on top of damages — not subject to the statutory cap

**Practice tip:** In class actions, the attorney fee award often dwarfs the class recovery. This is permitted and reflects Congress's intent to incentivize private enforcement. Courts have approved fee awards exceeding the class recovery where the lodestar is supported.

## Interaction with Other Remedies

### State Consumer Protection Statutes

Many states have their own debt collection statutes with independent remedies:
- Florida Consumer Collection Practices Act (FCCPA), Fla. Stat. § 559.55 et seq. — provides additional damages and may have longer limitations periods
- State UDAP/DTPA claims — broader unfairness standards, sometimes with treble damages
- State common law claims — harassment, IIED, invasion of privacy

### Bankruptcy Considerations

- FDCPA claims are property of the bankruptcy estate if they accrued pre-petition
- Post-petition violations create new claims belonging to the consumer (not the estate)
- Collecting on a discharged debt may violate both the discharge injunction (11 U.S.C. § 524) AND the FDCPA

### Relationship to TCPA Claims

Collection calls may also violate the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (47 U.S.C. § 227) if:
- Made using an automatic telephone dialing system (ATDS) to a cell phone without prior express consent
- The consumer previously revoked consent
- TCPA damages ($500-$1,500 per call) are separate from and additional to FDCPA damages

## Practice Tips for Maximizing Recovery

### Pre-Litigation

1. **Preserve evidence immediately** — save all letters, voicemails, call logs, texts, and emails
2. **Document emotional distress** — journal entries, therapy records, doctor visits, prescription records
3. **Obtain credit reports** — before-and-after comparison for credit damage claims
4. **Calculate financial harm** — overdraft fees, interest differentials, lost wages
5. **Identify the collector's other victims** — online reviews, CFPB complaint database, court records

### During Litigation

1. **Discovery the collector's net worth** — essential for class claims; relevant to § 1692k(b) factors
2. **Discover the collector's complaint history** — CFPB complaints, BBB complaints, prior lawsuits
3. **Identify the form letter or standard practice** — strengthens class allegations and shows intentional/persistent noncompliance
4. **Track attorney time meticulously** — detailed contemporaneous billing records are essential for fee petitions
5. **Consider Rule 68 offers of judgment** — collectors may try to moot individual claims; evaluate carefully whether the offer includes fees and costs

### Fee Petition Best Practices

- Maintain detailed contemporaneous time records (task, time, attorney)
- Establish the prevailing market rate through fee declarations or surveys
- Document the complexity of the case and the skill required
- Prepare a detailed fee petition with categorized entries
- Address any potential objections preemptively (block billing, excessive hours, clerical tasks)
- Include paralegal and law clerk time at appropriate rates

## Checklist: Evaluating Remedies in an FDCPA Case

- [ ] Identify all actual damages (emotional, financial, credit)
- [ ] Confirm statutory damages eligibility (up to $1,000 individual)
- [ ] Assess § 1692k(b) factors (frequency, persistence, intent, nature)
- [ ] Confirm the action is timely (filed within 1 year of violation)
- [ ] Identify the accrual date (occurrence vs. discovery rule in your circuit)
- [ ] Evaluate continuing violation arguments if initial violation is time-barred
- [ ] Assess standing (concrete injury post-*Spokeo*)
- [ ] Consider class action viability (uniform practice, numerosity, predominance)
- [ ] Calculate class damages cap (lesser of $500,000 or 1% of net worth)
- [ ] Identify supplemental state law claims for additional damages
- [ ] Document attorney time for mandatory fee award
- [ ] Evaluate TCPA overlay if calls were made to cell phones
