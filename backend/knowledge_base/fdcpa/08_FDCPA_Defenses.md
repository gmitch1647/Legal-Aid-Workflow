# FDCPA Defenses (15 U.S.C. § 1692k(c) and Common Law)

**Topic:** Defenses available to debt collectors in FDCPA litigation; bona fide error; definitional defenses; statute of limitations; consent; litigation privilege  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(c) (bona fide error defense); 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (definition of debt collector); 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(5) (definition of debt); 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(d) (statute of limitations)  
**Cross-references:** Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich, 559 U.S. 573 (2010); Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 582 U.S. 79 (2017)  
**Last updated:** May 17, 2026  

---

## Introduction

The FDCPA imposes strict liability — a debt collector's good intentions do not excuse violations. However, the statute and case law provide several affirmative defenses and threshold arguments that collectors routinely raise. Understanding these defenses is essential for consumer attorneys to anticipate and overcome them, and for evaluating the strength of a prospective FDCPA claim.

The defenses fall into several categories:
1. **Statutory defense:** Bona fide error (§ 1692k(c))
2. **Definitional defenses:** Not a "debt collector," not a "debt," no "communication"
3. **Limitations defense:** One-year statute of limitations (§ 1692k(d))
4. **Consent defense:** Consumer authorized the conduct
5. **Litigation privilege:** Communications in judicial proceedings
6. **Compliance defenses:** Good-faith reliance on advisory opinions, Regulation F safe harbors

## The Bona Fide Error Defense — § 1692k(c)

### Statutory Text

"A debt collector may not be held liable in any action brought under this subchapter if the debt collector shows by a preponderance of the evidence that the violation was not intentional and resulted from a bona fide error notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error."

### Three Required Elements

The debt collector bears the burden of proof (preponderance of the evidence) on ALL three elements:

**Element 1: The violation was not intentional**
- The collector must show it did not intend to violate the FDCPA
- Intent refers to intent to engage in the violative conduct, not intent to violate the law
- A deliberate business decision that turns out to violate the FDCPA is "intentional" even if the collector did not know it was illegal
- Clerical errors, computer glitches, human data-entry mistakes = not intentional
- A policy decision to send a particular form letter = intentional (even if the collector believed the letter was compliant)

**Element 2: The violation resulted from a bona fide error**
- The error must be genuine — not a calculated risk or knowing shortcut
- Examples of bona fide errors: mathematical miscalculation of interest; transposition of digits in account number; wrong letter sent to wrong consumer due to computer error; mail processing error
- The error must be the actual cause of the violation

**Element 3: Maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid the error**
- The collector must demonstrate it had procedures in place to prevent the type of error that occurred
- Must be actual, implemented procedures — not merely written policies that are not followed
- Procedures must be "reasonably adapted" to the specific error at issue — generic compliance programs are insufficient
- The collector must show the procedures were in place at the time of the violation and were being followed

### Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich, 559 U.S. 573 (2010)

**The most important decision limiting the bona fide error defense.**

**Holding:** The bona fide error defense does NOT apply to violations resulting from a debt collector's mistaken interpretation of the FDCPA's legal requirements. The defense is limited to clerical, factual, and procedural errors — not errors of legal judgment.

**Facts:** A law firm sent a validation notice that required consumers to put disputes "in writing," which the plaintiff argued violated § 1692g by adding a requirement not in the statute. The law firm argued this was a bona fide error based on their good-faith interpretation of the statute.

**Reasoning:**
- The statutory text refers to "error" — which historically means a mistake of fact, not a mistake of law
- The defense's requirement of "procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error" makes sense only for factual/clerical mistakes — there are no "procedures" to avoid a legal misinterpretation
- Reading the defense to cover legal errors would effectively convert the FDCPA from a strict liability statute into a negligence regime
- Congress specifically limited the defense to unintentional violations resulting from errors despite adequate procedures — legal errors by definition involve intentional policy choices

**Post-Jerman implications:**
- A collector cannot defend by saying "we thought this was legal" or "our attorney advised us this was permissible"
- A collector cannot defend by saying "the law was unclear and we adopted a reasonable interpretation"
- The defense is now limited to truly accidental mistakes: wrong balance due to data entry, letter sent to wrong address due to database error, timing violation due to mail delay
- This makes the FDCPA effectively a strict liability statute with only a narrow escape valve for genuine accidents

**Practice tip for consumer attorneys:** When the collector raises the bona fide error defense, immediately assess whether the violation resulted from a factual/clerical error or from a policy/legal judgment. If the latter (e.g., a form letter template, a standard business practice, an interpretation of what the law requires), cite *Jerman* to eliminate the defense as a matter of law.

### Proving the Defense Fails

**Discovery to defeat the bona fide error defense:**
- Request the collector's written compliance policies and procedures
- Depose the compliance officer about implementation and monitoring
- Request training materials provided to collection agents
- Ask: "What specific procedure was in place to prevent this exact type of error?"
- Ask: "How often are procedures audited for effectiveness?"
- Request records of other similar violations (showing procedures are inadequate)
- Request quality assurance records and error rates

**Arguments to defeat the defense:**
- The violation was intentional (a deliberate business practice, form letter, or policy)
- The error was legal, not factual (citing *Jerman*)
- No procedures existed to prevent the error
- Procedures existed on paper but were not implemented
- Procedures were inadequate for the type of error (generic compliance training does not address specific violation)
- High error rates demonstrate procedures are not "reasonably adapted"

## Definitional Defenses

### "Not a Debt Collector" Defense

**The FDCPA applies only to "debt collectors" as defined in § 1692a(6).** If the defendant is not a debt collector, the FDCPA does not apply regardless of the conduct.

**Who IS a debt collector:**
- Any person whose principal purpose is the collection of debts
- Any person who regularly collects debts owed to another (third-party collection)
- Any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce in any business whose principal purpose is debt collection
- Under Regulation F: debt buyers who acquire debts already in default (see § 1006.2(i))

**Who is NOT a debt collector:**

**(a) Original creditors collecting their own debts:**
- A bank collecting on its own credit card account
- A hospital collecting its own medical bills
- A utility company collecting past-due accounts
- EXCEPTION: An original creditor that uses a name other than its own suggesting a third party is involved IS treated as a debt collector under § 1692a(6)

**(b) Debt buyers — the Henson decision:**

**Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 582 U.S. 79 (2017)**

**Holding:** An entity that purchases defaulted debt and collects it for its own account is NOT a "debt collector" under the statutory definition requiring that the person collect debts "owed or due another." Once the debt buyer owns the debt, it is collecting its own debt, not a debt owed to "another."

**Critical impact:** After *Henson*, debt buyers who purchase portfolios of defaulted consumer debt are generally NOT covered by the FDCPA's provisions (absent their using a false name under § 1692a(6)).

**HOWEVER — Regulation F partially overrules Henson's practical effect:**
Regulation F, 12 C.F.R. § 1006.2(i), defines "debt collector" to include a person who acquires a debt and collects it when "the debt was in default at the time it was obtained." This definition brings debt buyers back within the scope of Regulation F's requirements (though not necessarily the FDCPA statute itself for purposes of § 1692k liability).

**Practice tip:** When facing a debt buyer defendant:
1. Check whether the debt buyer also regularly collects debts for others (making it a debt collector under the "regularly collects" prong regardless of *Henson*)
2. Check whether the debt buyer uses a name suggesting a third party is collecting
3. Plead Regulation F violations if the conduct occurred after November 30, 2021
4. Check state debt collection statutes — many cover debt buyers explicitly

**(c) Other exempt entities under § 1692a(6):**
- Officers or employees of a creditor collecting debts for the creditor in the creditor's name
- Government officials performing official duties
- Persons collecting debts incidental to a fiduciary obligation (e.g., trustees, executors)
- Process servers serving legal process
- Nonprofit credit counseling organizations (if they meet specific criteria)
- Persons collecting debts they originated (even if later sold and reacquired)

### "Not a Debt" Defense

**The FDCPA applies only to "debts" as defined in § 1692a(5):** "any obligation or alleged obligation of a consumer to pay money arising out of a transaction in which the money, property, insurance, or services which are the subject of the transaction are primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, whether or not such obligation has been reduced to judgment."

**What IS a debt:**
- Credit card balances
- Medical bills
- Student loans (private; federal loans have separate issues)
- Auto loans
- Personal loans
- Utility bills (residential)
- Rent obligations
- Gym memberships and personal services
- Telecommunications bills (personal phone, internet, cable)

**What is NOT a debt:**
- Business/commercial obligations — a loan taken for business purposes
- Government obligations — taxes, fines, criminal restitution, traffic tickets, regulatory penalties
- Tort judgments — a personal injury judgment against the consumer (no underlying "transaction")
- Child support and alimony (government-ordered obligations)
- HOA assessments — circuit split (some hold these are "debts" because of the transaction element in purchasing the home; others disagree)
- Obligations not arising from a "transaction" — involuntary obligations

**Key analysis:** The obligation must arise from a "transaction" that was "primarily for personal, family, or household purposes." Both elements must be satisfied.

**Practice tip:** When the defendant argues the obligation is not a "debt," analyze: (1) Was there a "transaction"? (a consensual exchange); (2) Was the transaction primarily for personal/family/household purposes? If both answers are yes, the obligation is a "debt" under the FDCPA.

### "No Communication" Defense

**Many FDCPA provisions regulate "communications" as defined in § 1692a(2):** "the conveying of information regarding a debt directly or indirectly to any person through any medium."

**When the defense applies:**
- The collector argues its conduct did not constitute a "communication" triggering statutory requirements
- Example: A collector leaves a voicemail that does not reference the debt — some courts have held this is not a "communication" under § 1692a(2) because no "information regarding a debt" was conveyed
- Regulation F's "limited-content message" framework (§ 1006.2(j)) creates a defined category of messages that are NOT "communications" for FDCPA purposes

**When the defense fails:**
- Any message that references the debt, the amount, the creditor, or the consumer's obligation conveys "information regarding a debt"
- Even indirect references (e.g., "please call us about an important financial matter") may qualify as conveying information regarding a debt
- The overall context determines whether a reasonable consumer would understand the communication relates to a debt

## Statute of Limitations Defense — § 1692k(d)

**The one-year limitations period is the most commonly successful defense in FDCPA litigation.**

### Application

- The collector demonstrates the last violative act occurred more than one year before the action was filed
- The burden is on the plaintiff to show the claim is timely — but the defendant typically raises the limitations defense in a motion to dismiss or motion for summary judgment

### Consumer's Responses to the Limitations Defense

1. **Later accrual date:** The violation did not "occur" until a later date (e.g., each phone call is a separate violation; continued collection after dispute is ongoing)
2. **Discovery rule:** The consumer could not reasonably have discovered the violation until a later date (limited availability — see remedies file)
3. **Equitable tolling:** The consumer was prevented from filing by extraordinary circumstances
4. **Continuing violation:** The collector's conduct constituted an ongoing course of conduct, and the last act was within one year
5. **Fraudulent concealment:** The collector actively concealed the violation (equitable tolling variant)

### Practice Tips

- Calendar the limitations date from the FIRST violative act — do not wait
- If the first violation is time-barred, identify subsequent violations within the one-year window
- Each letter, each call, and each collection attempt may be independently actionable
- Filing suit promptly is essential — FDCPA cases have no excuse for missing the one-year window if the violation is apparent

## Consent Defense

### Theory

The collector argues the consumer consented to the conduct that would otherwise violate the FDCPA.

### Application and Limitations

**Where consent may apply:**
- Consumer agreed to receive calls at specific times (waiving time-of-day restrictions under § 1692c(a)(1))
- Consumer agreed to communications at work (overcoming the workplace-call prohibition)
- Consumer agreed to specific payment arrangements that the collector followed
- Consumer opted in to electronic communications (relevant under Regulation F)

**Where consent does NOT apply:**
- A consumer cannot consent to harassment — § 1692d rights are non-waivable as a matter of public policy
- A consumer cannot waive the right to receive a validation notice — § 1692g is non-waivable
- A consumer cannot consent to false representations — the FDCPA protects against deception regardless of consumer "agreement"
- Contract provisions purporting to waive FDCPA rights are void under § 1692n ("Any waiver by any consumer of any protection provided by or any right of the consumer under [the FDCPA] is not valid")
- § 1692n is absolute — NO waiver of FDCPA rights is valid, whether in the original credit agreement or in any subsequent communication

**Practice tip:** When the collector claims consent, check § 1692n. The FDCPA's anti-waiver provision is one of the broadest in consumer protection law. Pre-dispute waiver clauses in credit agreements are unenforceable as applied to FDCPA rights. The only "consent" that may apply is real-time, informed consent to specific communications — not blanket contractual waivers.

## Litigation Privilege Defense

### Theory

Communications made in connection with judicial proceedings are protected by the litigation privilege and cannot form the basis of FDCPA liability.

### Application

**Strong version (minority of courts):** All statements made in court filings, pleadings, and litigation-related communications are absolutely privileged and cannot violate the FDCPA.

**Weak version (majority — and the statutory approach):** The FDCPA does apply to litigation conduct, but § 1692e(11) creates a narrow exception: the mini-Miranda disclosure is not required in "a formal pleading made in connection with a legal action." Beyond this narrow statutory exception, the FDCPA fully applies to attorneys and law firms engaged in debt collection through litigation.

### Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291 (1995)

**Holding:** The FDCPA applies to attorneys who "regularly" engage in consumer-debt-collection activity, even when that activity consists of litigation. The statute's definition of "debt collector" makes no exception for litigation, and the specific § 1692e(11) exception for formal pleadings confirms that Congress intended other FDCPA provisions to apply to litigation.

**Implications:**
- Filing a lawsuit can violate the FDCPA (e.g., filing in wrong venue under § 1692i)
- A complaint that misstates the amount owed can violate § 1692e(2)
- Litigation threats that the collector cannot or does not intend to carry out violate § 1692e(4)/(5)
- Discovery abuse can constitute harassment under § 1692d

### What the Litigation Privilege Does NOT Protect

- Filing suit on a time-barred debt (violates § 1692e and § 1692f)
- Filing suit in an improper venue (violates § 1692i)
- Including false sworn statements in affidavits (violates § 1692e)
- Filing suit without meaningful attorney review (violates § 1692e(3))
- Making false representations in court filings about the amount owed
- Serving process in a manner designed to avoid actual notice

### Practice Tip

When a collector-defendant raises the litigation privilege:
1. Cite *Heintz* — the Supreme Court definitively rejected a blanket litigation privilege for FDCPA purposes
2. Point to the narrow § 1692e(11) exception — Congress knew how to create litigation exceptions and did so only for the mini-Miranda
3. The expressive negative principle (*expressio unius est exclusio alterius*) — Congress's creation of one narrow litigation exception implies no others exist
4. Argue the policy purpose — exempting litigation would eliminate FDCPA protection precisely where consumers are most vulnerable (facing lawsuits)

## Good-Faith Compliance Defenses

### Advisory Opinion Reliance — § 1692k(e)

**Statutory text:** "No provision of this section imposing any liability shall apply to any act done or omitted in good faith in conformity with any advisory opinion of the Bureau [CFPB], notwithstanding that after such act or omission has occurred, such opinion is amended, rescinded, or determined by judicial or other authority to be invalid for any reason."

**Requirements:**
- The collector must have actually relied on a CFPB (formerly FTC) advisory opinion
- The reliance must be in good faith
- The specific act must conform to the advisory opinion
- This safe harbor survives even if the opinion is later invalidated

**Practice tip:** Advisory opinions are relatively rare. If the collector raises this defense, demand production of the specific opinion relied upon and evidence that the collector was aware of and actually followed it.

### Regulation F Safe Harbors

Regulation F creates several safe harbors where compliance with specified requirements establishes compliance with the underlying FDCPA provision:
- Model validation notice form (§ 1006.34(d)(2)) — substantial compliance = FDCPA § 1692g compliance
- Call frequency safe harbor (§ 1006.14(b)(3)) — 7 or fewer calls in 7 days creates a presumption of compliance with § 1692d(5)
- Limited-content message format (§ 1006.2(j)) — properly formatted limited-content messages are not "communications" triggering mini-Miranda

## Strategies for Overcoming Defenses

### Bona Fide Error
- Was the error factual/clerical or legal/interpretive? (*Jerman* eliminates legal errors)
- What specific procedures existed? (Demand production of compliance manuals, training records, audits)
- Were procedures actually followed? (Depose compliance officers, review implementation records)
- How frequently does this error occur? (Pattern evidence shows inadequate procedures)

### Not a Debt Collector
- Does the entity regularly collect debts for others? (Review business records, contracts with creditors)
- Does it use a name suggesting third-party collection? (§ 1692a(6) false-name provision)
- Is it a debt buyer covered by Regulation F? (Post-November 2021 conduct)
- Do state consumer protection statutes apply even if the FDCPA does not?

### Not a Debt
- Trace the obligation to its origin — what was the underlying transaction?
- Was the transaction primarily for personal/family/household purposes?
- Medical, utility, and telecom debts are almost always "debts" under the FDCPA

### Statute of Limitations
- Identify ALL violative acts — are any within the one-year window?
- Argue continuing violation for ongoing conduct
- Argue equitable tolling if the consumer could not have discovered the violation
- File promptly — never let the limitations period run on an apparent violation

## Checklist: Anticipating Defenses in FDCPA Litigation

- [ ] Is the defendant clearly a "debt collector"? If marginal, prepare to prove it at class cert/trial
- [ ] Is the obligation clearly a "debt"? If marginal, gather evidence of the underlying transaction
- [ ] Was the violative conduct within the last year? If marginal, identify tolling/continuing violation arguments
- [ ] Is the violation factual/clerical (bona fide error possible) or policy/legal (defense barred by Jerman)?
- [ ] What procedures did the collector have? (Discovery priority — compliance manuals, training, audits)
- [ ] Did the consumer "consent" to anything? Check § 1692n — waivers are void
- [ ] Is the violation based on litigation conduct? Cite Heintz — FDCPA applies to litigating attorneys
- [ ] Is there a Regulation F safe harbor that applies? If so, did the collector actually comply with its requirements?
- [ ] Did the collector rely on a CFPB advisory opinion? If so, was reliance in good faith and actually conforming?
