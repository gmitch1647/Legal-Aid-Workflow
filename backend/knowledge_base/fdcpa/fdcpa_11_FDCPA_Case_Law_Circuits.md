\#\# 1. Who is a "debt collector"  
  
The Supreme Court's \*Heintz\*, \*Henson\*, and \*Obduskey\* decisions framed the federal definition under § 1692a(6). The circuits have applied that framework to debt buyers, servicers, originators using assumed names, and foreclosure firms.  
  
\#\#\# Tepper v. Amos Financial, LLC, 898 F.3d 364 (3d Cir. 2018)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2018  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Amos Financial bought a defaulted home-equity loan from the FDIC and tried to collect it for its own account. Tepper sued under the FDCPA, arguing Amos was a debt collector. Following the Supreme Court's 2017 \*Henson\* decision, Amos argued it was the owner of the debt and therefore not "regularly collecting for another."  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Amos was a "debt collector" under § 1692a(6)'s "principal purpose" prong — the prong \*Henson\* expressly reserved. An entity whose principal business is buying and collecting defaulted consumer debt qualifies as a debt collector even when it owns the debts it collects.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* \*Tepper\* is the leading post-\*Henson\* circuit decision foreclosing debt buyers' attempts to escape the FDCPA. The CFPB endorsed this reading in commentary to Regulation F, and most district courts have followed \*Tepper\*. Bot should treat a debt buyer as covered unless the entity also originates/holds performing loans (in which case fact-specific inquiry into "principal purpose" is required).  
  
\#\#\# Barbato v. Greystone Alliance, LLC, 916 F.3d 260 (3d Cir. 2019)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2019  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Crown Asset Management bought defaulted consumer debt and hired Greystone Alliance to collect on its behalf. Crown argued it was not a debt collector because it outsourced all collection. The Third Circuit, following its \*Tepper\* reasoning, disagreed.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A passive debt buyer that purchases defaulted debt and outsources collection is nonetheless a "debt collector" under the "principal purpose" prong of § 1692a(6), so long as the business's principal purpose is the collection of debts.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Confirms that outsourcing collection activity does not shield a passive debt-buyer entity from FDCPA liability. Reinforces that under \*Henson\*'s reserved question, the answer in most cases is debt-buyer = debt collector under the principal-purpose prong.  
  
\#\#\# Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 720 F.3d 1204 (9th Cir. 2013)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2013  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Wells Fargo serviced a mortgage that was in default when Wells Fargo's affiliated entity took it over. Borrowers sued under the FDCPA. The bank moved to dismiss, arguing it was a creditor or a servicer, not a debt collector.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* To plead that a defendant is a "debt collector" under the "principal purpose" prong, a complaint must allege facts showing that debt collection is the principal purpose of the entity's business, not just that the entity collects some debts. Conclusory allegations are insufficient.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Establishes the pleading standard for "principal purpose" allegations against large diversified financial institutions. Sets up frequent motion-to-dismiss battles in mortgage and credit-card servicing cases.  
  
\#\#\# Bridge v. Ocwen Federal Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355 (6th Cir. 2012)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2012  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Ocwen acquired a defaulted residential mortgage and serviced it. The borrowers sued, claiming Ocwen was an FDCPA "debt collector."  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Under § 1692a(6)(F)(iii)'s exclusion for entities collecting debts acquired before default, a servicer is excluded from the "debt collector" definition only if the debt was \*not\* in default when it was acquired. Where the debt was in default at acquisition, the servicer is a debt collector.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* This default-status distinction governs much of mortgage servicing FDCPA litigation. Combined with \*Henson\*'s holding for "owners" collecting for themselves, the operative line for many servicers is whether they hold or service debts that were performing at the time of transfer.  
  
\#\#\# Maguire v. Citicorp Retail Services, Inc., 147 F.3d 232 (2d Cir. 1998)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1998  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Citicorp Retail Services used the name "Debtor Assistance" on its collection letters. Plaintiff alleged that this falsely implied the involvement of a third-party collector when the entity sending the letter was the creditor itself, in violation of the "false name" provision in § 1692a(6).  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A creditor who uses a name other than its own when collecting its own debts is treated as a "debt collector" subject to the FDCPA when the use of that name reasonably leads the debtor to believe a third party is involved. This is the "false name" exception in § 1692a(6).  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Original creditors generally are \*not\* FDCPA debt collectors, but the false-name exception sweeps in originators who hide behind assumed names (e.g., separately branded internal collection divisions). The bot should flag in-house "collection units" that use names different from the originator.

\#\# 2. What is a "debt"  
  
The FDCPA only covers obligations arising from consumer transactions. The circuits have decided several recurring questions about what counts.  
  
\#\#\# Bass v. Stolper, Koritzinsky, Brewster & Neider, S.C., 111 F.3d 1322 (7th Cir. 1997)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1997  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Bass wrote a check to a grocery store that bounced; the law firm Stolper Koritzinsky attempted collection. Bass sued under the FDCPA. The firm argued there was no "debt" because no extension of credit was involved.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A dishonored check used to purchase consumer goods creates an "obligation ... to pay money arising out of a transaction" for personal, family, or household purposes within § 1692a(5). Extension of credit is not required; what matters is that the underlying transaction was consumer in nature.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Bounced-check collection is squarely covered by the FDCPA. The bot should not let a debt collector argue otherwise based on the absence of credit.  
  
\#\#\# Newman v. Boehm, Pearlstein & Bright, Ltd., 119 F.3d 477 (7th Cir. 1997)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1997  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* A condominium association hired Boehm, Pearlstein & Bright to collect overdue assessments from unit owners. The owners sued under the FDCPA. The firm argued the assessments were not "debts" but tax-like obligations.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Homeowner-association and condominium assessments are "debts" under § 1692a(5) because they arise from the consumer's purchase of the residence — a transaction for personal, family, or household purposes — and constitute an obligation to pay money.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Resolved the question for HOA, COA, and similar association fees. Followed by \*Ladick v. Van Gemert\*, 146 F.3d 1205 (10th Cir. 1998), and most other circuits.  
  
\#\#\# Ladick v. Van Gemert, 146 F.3d 1205 (10th Cir. 1998)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1998  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* A condominium owner challenged a law firm's collection of past-due association assessments, arguing the assessments were not "debts" because all owners benefited from the underlying services.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* HOA assessments are "debts" because they arise from the consumer transaction of acquiring residential real estate. The "shared benefit" point does not change the analysis.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Aligns the Tenth Circuit with the Seventh, providing strong cross-circuit authority for plaintiffs collecting HOA fee disputes.  
  
\#\#\# Hawthorne v. Mac Adjustment, Inc., 140 F.3d 1367 (11th Cir. 1998)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1998  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Mac Adjustment, acting as subrogee for the victim's insurer, tried to collect from Hawthorne for damages caused by an auto accident. Hawthorne sued under the FDCPA.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A tort obligation arising from a car accident is not a "debt" within the FDCPA. The statutory term "transaction" requires "a consensual or contractual arrangement," not damages "thrust upon" a tortfeasor by negligence.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Subrogation claims arising from accidents are categorically outside the FDCPA. Combined with cases like \*Turner v. Cook\*, 362 F.3d 1219 (9th Cir. 2004), the consensual-transaction requirement is broadly accepted.  
  
\#\#\# Berman v. GC Services Ltd. Partnership, 146 F.3d 482 (7th Cir. 1998) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1998  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Educational student-loan debt was at issue; defendant argued certain government-related obligations were not "consumer" debts.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Federally guaranteed student loans, when used for personal educational purposes, are consumer "debts" within the meaning of the FDCPA.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Establishes student loans as FDCPA-covered. The citation as given here is the commonly cited form but the bot should verify before quoting in a court filing.

\#\# 3. "Least sophisticated consumer" / "unsophisticated consumer" standard  
  
The circuits agree that § 1692e and § 1692g are evaluated under an objective standard tied to an unsophisticated consumer's understanding, but they differ slightly in wording.  
  
\#\#\# Clomon v. Jackson, 988 F.2d 1314 (2d Cir. 1993)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1993  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Attorney P.D. Jackson allowed a debt collector to mass-produce collection letters bearing his facsimile signature. The letters stated that Jackson was "suggesting" certain actions and had "told" his client what could "lawfully" be done. Jackson did not personally review the accounts.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Adopting the "least sophisticated consumer" standard, the Second Circuit held that the letters falsely implied meaningful attorney involvement in violation of § 1692e(3) (false representation that a communication is from an attorney) and § 1692e(10) (false or misleading representation). The standard "protects all consumers, the gullible as well as the shrewd," while "preserv\[ing\] a quotient of reasonableness" through an objective lens.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* The foundational FDCPA standard-setting case. Defines (1) the least-sophisticated-consumer test and (2) the "meaningful attorney involvement" doctrine. Adopted by the Third, Fourth, Sixth, Ninth, Eleventh, and other circuits.  
  
\#\#\# Wilson v. Quadramed Corp., 225 F.3d 350 (3d Cir. 2000)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2000  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Quadramed sent Wilson a letter labeled "immediate collection" stating it would "afford \[him\] the opportunity to pay this bill immediately and avoid further action." Wilson alleged the letter overshadowed the § 1692g validation notice.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Applying the "least sophisticated debtor" standard, the Third Circuit held the letter did not overshadow the validation rights. The standard protects naïve consumers but "presum\[es\] a basic level of understanding and willingness to read with care," shielding collectors from "bizarre or idiosyncratic" readings.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Articulates the protective limits of the least-sophisticated-consumer test. Cited extensively for the proposition that the standard is not a license for absurd interpretations.  
  
\#\#\# Donohue v. Quick Collect, Inc., 592 F.3d 1027 (9th Cir. 2010)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2010  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* A collection complaint mischaracterized the components of the debt (labeling pre-assignment finance charges and post-assignment interest together as "interest \[on the principal\]" at 12%). Donohue alleged a § 1692e violation.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Under the "least sophisticated debtor" standard, false statements violate § 1692e only if they are \*material\* — i.e., they could affect the consumer's ability to intelligently decide how to respond. Immaterial inaccuracies are not actionable.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Adds a materiality gate to FDCPA false-statement claims in the Ninth Circuit. Similar reasoning has been adopted by the Sixth Circuit (\*Miller v. Javitch, Block & Rathbone\*, 561 F.3d 588 (6th Cir. 2009)) and others. The Seventh Circuit has been more skeptical of a freestanding materiality requirement.  
  
\#\#\# Gammon v. GC Services Ltd. Partnership, 27 F.3d 1254 (7th Cir. 1994)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1994  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* GC Services sent Gammon a collection letter stating that the company had "provided the systems used by a major branch of the federal government and various state governments to collect delinquent taxes." Gammon alleged this falsely implied a governmental affiliation in violation of § 1692e(9).  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* The Seventh Circuit adopted the "unsophisticated consumer" formulation (rather than "least sophisticated"), reasoning that "the least sophisticated consumer is not merely below average, but is the very last rung on the sophistication ladder." The unsophisticated standard "protects the consumer who is uninformed, naive, or trusting, yet ... admits an objective element of reasonableness."  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* The Seventh Circuit's "unsophisticated consumer" framing is verbally different from other circuits but operates similarly. The bot should know that \*Gammon\*'s framing applies in the Seventh Circuit and that \*Bartlett v. Heibl\*, 128 F.3d 497 (7th Cir. 1997), is the controlling overshadowing case under that standard.

\#\# 4. § 1692e false/misleading interpretation — time-barred debt and interest disclosures  
  
\#\#\# McMahon v. LVNV Funding, LLC, 744 F.3d 1010 (7th Cir. 2014)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit (consolidated appeals)  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2014  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Two consumers received letters from debt collectors offering to settle credit-card debts on which the statute of limitations had run. The letters did not threaten suit but offered "settlement" of the time-barred obligation.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A settlement offer on a debt the collector cannot legally enforce can violate § 1692e(2) (misrepresenting "the character, amount, or legal status" of a debt) and § 1692e(10) (any deceptive means). An unsophisticated consumer could read a settlement offer as confirming the debt is legally enforceable; that implication is false.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Leading authority that settlement offers on time-barred debts can violate the FDCPA even without an express threat of suit, provided the letter does not clearly disclose the limitations bar. Catalyst for the CFPB's later Regulation F provisions (12 C.F.R. § 1006.26) requiring time-barred-debt disclosures.  
  
\#\#\# Daugherty v. Convergent Outsourcing, Inc., 836 F.3d 507 (5th Cir. 2016)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2016  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Convergent sent Daugherty a letter offering alternative settlement options on a time-barred credit card debt without disclosing that the debt was beyond the limitations period or threatening suit.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A settlement letter offering payment options on a time-barred debt can violate § 1692e if the language could mislead an unsophisticated consumer to believe the debt is legally enforceable, even absent a threat of litigation.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Aligns the Fifth Circuit with the Seventh (\*McMahon\*) and Sixth Circuits, against the Third and Eighth, on whether absence of a litigation threat saves a stale-debt collection letter. Important for bot-given advice in southern states.  
  
\#\#\# Crawford v. LVNV Funding, LLC, 758 F.3d 1254 (11th Cir. 2014)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2014  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Crawford filed Chapter 13 bankruptcy. LVNV filed a proof of claim for a debt where the limitations period had run more than four years before. Crawford brought an adversary proceeding under the FDCPA.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Filing a proof of claim on an obviously time-barred debt in a bankruptcy proceeding is an "attempt to collect" within the FDCPA and violates § 1692e and § 1692f.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Largely superseded by \*Midland Funding, LLC v. Johnson\*, 581 U.S. 224 (2017), which held that such proof-of-claim filings are not FDCPA violations under federal bankruptcy law (provided state law treats the stale debt as a "right to payment"). Bot should treat \*Crawford\* as historical for federal bankruptcy issues but useful for state-law extinguishment jurisdictions (Wisconsin, Mississippi).  
  
\#\#\# Avila v. Riexinger & Associates, LLC, 817 F.3d 72 (2d Cir. 2016)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2016  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Plaintiffs received collection notices stating their "current balance" without disclosing that the balance continued to accrue interest and fees. Avila alleged interest was accruing daily at an effective rate of 500% per year.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A collection letter that states the consumer's "current balance" without disclosing that the balance may increase due to interest, fees, or other charges violates § 1692e because it can mislead the least sophisticated consumer who relies on the stated amount to satisfy the debt. The court adopted the Seventh Circuit's \*Miller\* safe harbor.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* The leading Second Circuit interest-disclosure case. Establishes that simply stating a "balance" without a \*Miller\*-style disclaimer can be deceptive when interest is in fact accruing. Practitioners draft "current balance" language with \*Avila\* and \*Miller\* in mind.  
  
\#\#\# Miller v. McCalla, Raymer, Padrick, Cobb, Nichols, & Clark, L.L.C., 214 F.3d 872 (7th Cir. 2000)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2000  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* A law firm sent a § 1692g validation notice that disclosed only the "unpaid principal balance," omitting accrued interest, fees, and other charges. Miller alleged the letter failed to state the "amount of the debt" required by § 1692g(a)(1).  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* The Seventh Circuit fashioned a now-famous safe-harbor disclosure: "As of the date of this letter, you owe $\_\_\_. Because of interest, late charges, and other charges that may vary from day to day, the amount due on the day you pay may be greater. Hence, if you pay the amount shown above, an adjustment may be necessary after we receive your check, in which event we will inform you before depositing the check for collection."  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Universal-citation safe harbor language. Adopted by the Second Circuit (\*Avila\*) and recognized in most others. Note the Seventh Circuit's caveat in \*Boucher v. Finance System of Green Bay\*, 880 F.3d 362 (7th Cir. 2018), that mere recitation of the \*Miller\* language does not "immunize" a collector if the underlying disclosure is inaccurate.

\#\# 5. § 1692g validation notice — overshadowing and content  
  
\#\#\# Bartlett v. Heibl, 128 F.3d 497 (7th Cir. 1997)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1997 (Posner, J.)  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Attorney Heibl sent Bartlett a letter demanding payment within one week or warning that "legal action will be necessary," and then included on the bottom of the page a § 1692g paraphrase telling Bartlett he had 30 days to dispute. The letter said "suit may be commenced at any time before the expiration of this thirty (30) day\[ \] \[period\]."  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* The juxtaposition of a one-week demand with a 30-day dispute right is confusing to the unsophisticated consumer and "overshadows" the statutory notice in violation of § 1692g. Judge Posner suggested model "safe harbor" language explicitly reconciling the two timeframes (the so-called "\*Bartlett\* form notice").  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* The leading Seventh Circuit overshadowing decision and an early example of the court drafting model compliance language. Bot should use the \*Bartlett\* template when explaining how to reconcile early-action demands with 30-day dispute rights.  
  
\#\#\# Russell v. Equifax A.R.S., 74 F.3d 30 (2d Cir. 1996)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1996  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Equifax A.R.S. sent Russell a notice captioned "IMMEDIATE COLLECTION NOTICE" stating that the debt would be posted to her credit file unless she did not dispute it and paid within 10 days; a follow-up letter captioned "CONTACT THIS OFFICE AT ONCE" demanded payment within five days. The § 1692g validation rights were printed on the back of the first notice.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Language on the front of a collection letter can overshadow or contradict the validation notice on the back, violating § 1692g, when from the perspective of the least sophisticated consumer the front-of-letter language creates uncertainty about the consumer's statutory rights. A threat or coercion is not required; confusion alone suffices.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Seminal Second Circuit overshadowing case. Establishes the front-of-letter / back-of-letter problem and the consumer-confusion (not threat) test. Cited in nearly every overshadowing decision in the Second and Third Circuits.  
  
\#\#\# Caceres v. McCalla Raymer, LLC, 755 F.3d 1299 (11th Cir. 2014) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2014  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Validation notice content sufficiency under § 1692g in connection with foreclosure communications.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Validation notice substantially complying with § 1692g(a) requirements satisfies the statute where the consumer can ascertain her rights from the notice.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Eleventh Circuit content-sufficiency precedent. Bot should verify the precise citation before relying on this case in a brief; the doctrine is well-settled even if pinning a single representative decision warrants checking.  
  
\#\#\# Spears v. Brennan, 745 N.E.2d 862 (Ind. Ct. App. 2001) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
State authority sometimes paralleling federal § 1692g doctrine; cited cautiously.

\#\# 6. Meaningful attorney involvement  
  
\#\#\# Clomon v. Jackson, 988 F.2d 1314 (2d Cir. 1993)  
  
(See Section 3 above.) The foundational case: an attorney's facsimile signature on a mass-mailed letter, without case-specific review, violates § 1692e(3) and § 1692e(10).  
  
\#\#\# Miller v. Wolpoff & Abramson, L.L.P., 321 F.3d 292 (2d Cir. 2003) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2003  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Law firm sent collection letters that the consumer alleged were not the product of meaningful attorney involvement.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A letter from a law firm implies attorney involvement; if the underlying file has not been meaningfully reviewed by an attorney, that implication is false in violation of § 1692e.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* The bot can reference the \*Clomon/Miller\* line for any letter on attorney letterhead. The exact citation given here corresponds to the commonly-cited form; verify before quoting in court.  
  
\#\#\# Avila v. Rubin, 84 F.3d 222 (7th Cir. 1996)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1996  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* A debt collection law firm sent letters from "Attorney Rubin" that were not personally reviewed by Rubin. The Seventh Circuit considered whether the letters were deceptive under § 1692e.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Letters on attorney letterhead, signed by an attorney, falsely imply meaningful attorney involvement when no attorney reviewed the file. Such letters violate § 1692e(3) and § 1692e(10).  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* The Seventh Circuit companion to \*Clomon\*. The \*Clomon/Avila\* line is now the rule in essentially every circuit.

\#\# 7. § 1692c third-party communications and Hunstein  
  
\#\#\# Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Management Services, Inc., 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, en banc  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2022  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Preferred Collection transferred Hunstein's debt information to a letter-vendor service provider (Compumail), which prepared and mailed the dunning letter. Hunstein sued, alleging the disclosure to the vendor was a "communication ... with \[a\] third party in connection with the collection of any debt" prohibited by § 1692c(b). After a panel decision finding standing and a remand following \*TransUnion v. Ramirez\*, the en banc Eleventh Circuit dismissed for lack of Article III standing.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Hunstein lacked a "concrete" injury sufficient for Article III standing under \*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez\*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021). A purely private disclosure to a single mail-vendor intermediary is not "publicity" akin to the common-law tort of public disclosure of private facts. Without traditional concrete harm, the alleged statutory violation cannot support federal-court standing.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* The en banc decision effectively shut down the "letter-vendor theory" in the Eleventh Circuit. Several other circuits have followed (or will follow) in concluding that vendor disclosures, without more, do not confer Article III standing. State courts may still entertain such claims where state standing rules are less demanding. The bot should treat letter-vendor cases as practically dead in federal court but viable as state-law claims in some jurisdictions.  
  
\#\#\# Brown v. Van Ru Credit Corp., 804 F.3d 740 (6th Cir. 2015) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2015  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Plaintiff alleged that a debt collector's communications with her employer violated § 1692c(b).  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Communications with third parties relating to a consumer's debt require the consumer's prior consent, the third party's consent, or location-information narrow exception under § 1692b.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Reminder that \*Hunstein\* concerned standing, not the merits of § 1692c(b). Many courts continue to find third-party disclosure claims meritorious where standing is satisfied (e.g., embarrassing employer or family communications).

\#\# 8. § 1692d call frequency  
  
\#\#\# Akalwadi v. Risk Management Alternatives, Inc., 336 F. Supp. 2d 492 (D. Md. 2004) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. District Court for the District of Maryland  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2004  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Plaintiff alleged that the defendant placed numerous calls — often multiple per day — to collect on a debt. The court considered when call frequency rises to harassment under § 1692d(5) (causing a phone to ring "repeatedly or continuously with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass").  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Call volume, time of day, and pattern (including calls placed after the consumer has asked the collector to stop) are relevant factors. There is no bright-line numerical threshold; the analysis is fact-intensive.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Representative district-court application of the fact-intensive § 1692d(5) inquiry. The case is widely cited; bot should verify the citation in current sources before filing.  
  
\#\#\# Krapf v. Collectors Training Institute, 2010 WL 2901732 (E.D. Pa. 2010) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
District court analysis of call frequency.  
  
\#\#\# Reg F § 1006.14(b) — 7-in-7 presumption  
  
The CFPB's Regulation F, 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2), creates a \*presumption\* that a debt collector violates § 1692d(5) if it places telephone calls to a particular consumer in connection with the collection of a particular debt more than seven times within seven consecutive days, or within seven consecutive days after engaging in a telephone conversation with the person in connection with the collection of such debt. The presumption is rebuttable and is regulatory, not statutory.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Most modern call-frequency disputes track Reg F's 7-in-7 rule. The bot should know that older case-law on call frequency (\*Akalwadi\*, \*Krapf\*, \*Pugliese\*) coexists with the regulatory presumption — falling under the 7-in-7 line does not automatically immunize a collector if the totality of circumstances shows harassment.

\#\# 9. Bona fide error defense  
  
\#\#\# Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA, 559 U.S. 573 (2010)  
  
(Supreme Court — see \`fdcpa\_10\_FDCPA\_Case\_Law\_SCOTUS.md\`.) The defense in § 1692k(c) does not extend to mistakes of law.  
  
\#\#\# Reichert v. National Credit Systems, Inc., 531 F.3d 1002 (9th Cir. 2008)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2008  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* A property manager wrongly reported that a tenant owed a $225 fee. NCS, the collector, raised the bona fide error defense based on its general manager's declaration that NCS had "extensive procedures" and that the landlord had not previously provided incorrect information.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* The bona fide error defense requires the collector to prove by a preponderance that it actually \*maintained procedures reasonably adapted to avoid the specific error\*. A conclusory description of "extensive procedures," plus past reliance on accurate creditor data, is insufficient.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Leading authority for the procedural element of the defense. Note: this is a Ninth Circuit decision (commonly miscited as Seventh Circuit). The bot should require documented, error-specific procedures in any defense workup.  
  
\#\#\# Owen v. I.C. System, Inc., 629 F.3d 1263 (11th Cir. 2011)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2011  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* I.C. System collected debt after the consumer's account had been settled. The collector argued bona fide error.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A collector must show concrete, specific procedures designed to avoid the type of error that occurred. Vague references to "training" or "policy" without documentation will not satisfy § 1692k(c).  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* A typical post-\*Jerman\* application of the procedural rigor required. Useful Eleventh Circuit authority for plaintiffs opposing the defense at summary judgment.  
  
\#\#\# Johnson v. Riddle, 305 F.3d 1107 (10th Cir. 2002) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2002  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Attorney sought § 1692k(c) shelter for a misapplication of state law.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Pre-\*Jerman\*, some circuits allowed the bona fide error defense to extend to legal mistakes. \*Jerman\* abrogated that aspect of decisions like \*Johnson\*; the procedural-error portion remains useful authority.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Helpful for the bot's chronological understanding of why \*Jerman\* mattered.

\#\# 10. Standing post-\*TransUnion v. Ramirez\* in FDCPA cases  
  
The Supreme Court's \*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez\*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), tightened the requirement that an FDCPA plaintiff allege a "concrete" Article III injury, not merely a statutory violation. Several circuits have applied the rule to dismiss FDCPA suits.  
  
\#\#\# Maddox v. Bank of New York Mellon Trust Co., N.A., 19 F.4th 58 (2d Cir. 2021)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2021  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* The Maddoxes alleged that BNY Mellon failed timely to record a mortgage discharge in violation of a New York statute. They sought statutory penalties. After the Second Circuit's initial decision found standing, the Supreme Court decided \*TransUnion\*; on rehearing the Second Circuit reversed.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A statutory violation plus a "perfunctory allegation of emotional distress" wholly disproportionate to the underlying violation does not satisfy Article III after \*TransUnion\*. The case must be dismissed for lack of standing.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Second Circuit's strict application of \*TransUnion\* in a consumer-finance context. Set up parallel reasoning in FDCPA litigation across the circuit.  
  
\#\#\# Pierre v. Midland Credit Management, Inc., 29 F.4th 934 (7th Cir. 2022)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2022  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Midland sent Pierre a settlement offer on a time-barred credit-card debt. A class-action jury verdict of $350,000 was entered. Pierre's claimed injuries were confusion, emotional distress, and the risk that she might pay or promise to pay and thereby revive the stale debt.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Confusion and minimal emotional distress are not "concrete" injuries under \*TransUnion\*. A risk of harm supports standing only for injunctive relief, not for damages. Pierre lacked standing; vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* A blockbuster decision that vacated a six-figure plaintiff's verdict. Strongly signals to the Seventh Circuit that intangible "informational" injuries will not suffice for damages standing in FDCPA cases. Bot should flag that Seventh Circuit FDCPA plaintiffs need genuine, particularized concrete harm.  
  
\#\#\# Trichell v. Midland Credit Management, Inc., 964 F.3d 990 (11th Cir. 2020)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2020  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Trichell and a co-plaintiff received settlement-offer letters on time-barred debt. They did not pay, did not respond, and did not allege they were personally misled — only that the letters would mislead the "least sophisticated consumer."  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* A consumer who is not herself misled by an allegedly misleading collection letter, and suffers no tangible injury (no payment, no wasted time), lacks Article III standing to sue under the FDCPA. The "least sophisticated consumer" merits standard does not establish the \*plaintiff's\* standing.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Eleventh Circuit's leading FDCPA standing case (pre-dating \*TransUnion\* but largely confirmed by it). Together with \*Pierre\*, \*Trichell\* sets up tough motion-to-dismiss exposure for FDCPA plaintiffs whose only injury is statutory.  
  
\#\#\# Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Management Services, Inc., 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc)  
  
(See Section 7.) Letter-vendor disclosure is not "publicity," hence no concrete injury after \*TransUnion\*.  
  
\*Note on Cherry v. Dometic Corp., 986 F.3d 1296 (11th Cir. 2021):\* The user's outline references \*Cherry\* as an FDCPA standing case. WebSearch verification confirms that \*Cherry\* is a Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act / Rule 23 ascertainability decision, \*\*not\*\* an FDCPA case. The bot should not cite \*Cherry\* for FDCPA standing; \*Hunstein\* (en banc) and \*Trichell\* are the Eleventh Circuit FDCPA-standing authorities.

\#\# 11. State-law debt collection laws and § 1692n preemption  
  
\#\#\# Statutory framework  
  
Section 1692n provides: "This subchapter does not annul, alter, or affect, or exempt any person subject to the provisions of this subchapter from complying with the laws of any State with respect to debt collection practices, except to the extent that those laws are inconsistent with any provision of this subchapter, and then only to the extent of the inconsistency. ... \[A\] State law is not inconsistent with \[the FDCPA\] if the protection such law affords any consumer is greater than the protection provided by this subchapter."  
  
This is a classic "consumer-floor" non-preemption framework. The FDCPA preempts state laws only when (a) they are genuinely inconsistent with the federal statute and (b) provide \*less\* consumer protection. Most state debt-collection acts (e.g., California's Rosenthal Act, Cal. Civ. Code § 1788 et seq.; Florida's CCPA, Fla. Stat. § 559.55 et seq.; Texas Debt Collection Act, Tex. Fin. Code § 392) provide \*more\* protection — by covering creditors, longer SOLs, or higher damages — and therefore are not preempted.  
  
\#\#\# Carlson v. Long Island Jewish Medical Center, 378 F. Supp. 2d 128 (E.D.N.Y. 2005) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
District court analysis confirming that state laws expanding consumer protection are non-preempted.  
  
\#\#\# Riggs v. Prober & Raphael, 681 F.3d 1097 (9th Cir. 2012) \[VERIFY CITATION\]  
  
Ninth Circuit recognition that state-law debt collection claims can proceed alongside FDCPA claims, with juries instructed under each statute.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* The bot should always check state debt-collection statutes in parallel with FDCPA analysis. State actions are particularly useful (1) when federal standing is shaky after \*TransUnion\* (state courts often have laxer standing rules); (2) when the federal one-year SOL has run but state SOL is longer; or (3) when the defendant is a creditor (not a § 1692a(6) debt collector) but covered by state law.

\#\# 12. Class action FDCPA  
  
\#\#\# Mace v. Van Ru Credit Corp., 109 F.3d 338 (7th Cir. 1997)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 1997  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Mace sued Van Ru on behalf of Wisconsin consumers who received certain collection letters. The district court conditioned class certification on the class being nationwide because of the FDCPA's class-damages cap.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* The FDCPA's class-damages cap (the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of the collector's net worth, § 1692k(a)(2)(B)) does not require statewide or substate FDCPA class actions to be expanded into nationwide classes. State-by-state classes are appropriate. The court flagged that "multiple or serial class actions" against the same defendant for the same misconduct might warrant a different approach.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Foundational FDCPA class-action authority. The bot should know that statewide and even substate classes are permissible despite the modest damages cap.  
  
\#\#\# Sanders v. Jackson, 209 F.3d 998 (7th Cir. 2000)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2000  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Sanders brought a class action under the FDCPA. The dispute concerned whether Universal Fidelity Corporation's "net worth" for purposes of the 1%-of-net-worth cap meant book value (about $100,000) or fair market value (about $1.8 million).  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* "Net worth" under § 1692k(a)(2)(B) means \*book net worth\* (balance-sheet assets minus liabilities) determined under GAAP. Goodwill is not added; the cap is designed to identify assets that could safely be liquidated to satisfy a judgment without forcing the company's breakup.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Controls the calculation of the class-damages cap in the Seventh Circuit and is widely followed elsewhere. Sharply limits class-action recoveries against thinly capitalized collection firms.  
  
\#\#\# Wahl v. Midland Credit Management, Inc., 556 F.3d 643 (7th Cir. 2009)  
  
\*\*Court:\*\* U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
\*\*Year:\*\* 2009  
  
\*\*Facts.\*\* Wahl brought a class action alleging Midland's letters misleadingly described the "principal balance" of a debt that had ballooned with pre-charge-off interest and fees. The district court certified a class as to one count and granted Midland summary judgment.  
  
\*\*Holding.\*\* Midland's description of the post-charge-off, pre-purchase balance as "principal" was not misleading to an unsophisticated consumer because that aggregated amount was treated as principal at the point of sale to the debt buyer, and Midland separately accrued interest from that base.  
  
\*\*Why it matters.\*\* Bot should understand that \*Wahl\* is a substantive § 1692e decision arising in a class-certified posture — not a class-certification doctrine case. Important for debt-buyer "balance" language disputes.  
  
\#\#\# Crawford v. LVNV Funding, LLC, 758 F.3d 1254 (11th Cir. 2014)  
  
(See Section 4.) Class-action context for stale-debt proof-of-claim litigation. Largely superseded by \*Midland Funding v. Johnson\* in federal bankruptcy but still relevant in state-extinguishment jurisdictions.
