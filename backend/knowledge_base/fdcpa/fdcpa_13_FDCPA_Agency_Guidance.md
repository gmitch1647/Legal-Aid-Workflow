\# FDCPA Corpus File 13 — Agency Guidance (CFPB and FTC)  
  
\*\*Topic:\*\* Administrative interpretive guidance on the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act  
\*\*Primary Statute:\*\* 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692–1692p (FDCPA)  
\*\*Regulation:\*\* 12 C.F.R. Part 1006 (Regulation F)  
\*\*Last Updated:\*\* May 16, 2026  
  
\---  
  
\#\# Overview  
  
Two federal agencies have shaped FDCPA interpretation: the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), which administered the FDCPA from 1977 until July 21, 2011, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), which assumed primary FDCPA rulemaking, supervisory, and enforcement authority on the Dodd-Frank "designated transfer date." Both agencies retain enforcement authority over debt collectors (FTC under FTC Act § 5; CFPB under Title X of Dodd-Frank and the FDCPA), and both publish interpretive material that courts treat as persuasive authority.  
  
The most important guidance documents fall into four categories: (A) the CFPB Final Rule preamble for Regulation F and follow-on advisory opinions; (B) CFPB Supervisory Highlights and FDCPA Annual Reports; (C) CFPB consent orders against major debt collectors; and (D) the FTC's 1988 Staff Commentary (still cited as the most comprehensive section-by-section gloss on the statutory text) and the FTC's continuing annual reports and enforcement actions.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# A. CFPB Guidance  
  
\#\#\# 1. Final Rule preamble for Regulation F (2020–2021)  
\*\*Citation:\*\* 85 Fed. Reg. 76734 (Nov. 30, 2020) (October 2020 Final Rule, covering communications and prohibitions); 86 Fed. Reg. 5766 (Jan. 19, 2021) (December 2020 Final Rule, covering validation information and time-barred-debt litigation prohibitions).  
\*\*Effective Date:\*\* November 30, 2021.  
  
The Final Rule preambles are extensive (over 800 Federal Register pages combined) and function as the principal interpretive companion to Regulation F. Key preamble positions include:  
  
\- \*\*7-in-7 telephone call frequency.\*\* The CFPB declined to adopt a flat prohibition and instead set forth rebuttable presumptions. The preamble explains that the threshold reflects CFPB analysis of consumer complaint patterns and industry call records, balancing consumer protection against legitimate collection.  
\- \*\*Limited-content messages.\*\* The CFPB created the limited-content-message concept by regulation. The preamble rejects industry arguments that any informational voicemail should be treated as a non-communication, while protecting collectors from the \*Foti v. NCO Financial Systems\* (D. Conn. 2006) line of cases that treated even minimal voicemails as third-party disclosures.  
\- \*\*Electronic communications.\*\* The preamble explains the "Bedrock Procedures" framework for permissible email and text use, including the reassigned-number safe harbor.  
\- \*\*Validation notice overhaul.\*\* The CFPB defended the itemization-table format and the five permissible itemization dates as a compromise between debt-buyer practicalities and consumer comprehensibility.  
\- \*\*Time-barred-debt litigation bar.\*\* The CFPB adopted a strict-liability prohibition on suing or threatening to sue on a time-barred debt, explicitly rejecting a "knew or should have known" element.  
  
The preamble is regularly cited by district courts in Reg F disputes; it benefits from \*Kisor v. Wilkie\* deference as the CFPB's contemporaneous interpretation of its own regulation.  
  
\#\#\# 2. CFPB Advisory Opinions on FDCPA Topics  
The CFPB's Advisory Opinion Program (launched 2020) is the principal mechanism for non-rulemaking interpretive guidance. Each opinion is published in the Federal Register and bound on the CFPB.  
  
\*\*(a) Pay-to-Pay Fees (Convenience Fees)\*\*  
\- \*\*Citation:\*\* 87 Fed. Reg. 39733 (July 5, 2022) ("Debt Collection Practices (Regulation F); Pay-to-Pay Fees").  
\- \*\*Holding:\*\* The FDCPA § 1692f(1) and 12 C.F.R. § 1006.22(b) prohibit debt collectors from collecting any amount — including fees for processing payments by telephone, online, or other particular method — unless that fee is expressly authorized by the agreement creating the debt OR expressly permitted by law. The CFPB rejected the line of district court cases reading § 1692f(1) to permit collection of any "incidental" fee not expressly prohibited.  
\- \*\*Vicarious liability:\*\* A debt collector that uses a third-party payment processor that charges unauthorized fees may also be liable if the collector receives a kickback or otherwise causes the fee to be charged.  
\- \*\*Why it matters:\*\* Pay-to-pay-fee claims are now a major FDCPA class-action theory. The CFPB has filed amicus briefs (e.g., \*Glover and Booze v. Ocwen\*, 11th Cir. 2024) advancing this interpretation.  
  
\*\*(b) Time-Barred Debt — Foreclosure Actions\*\*  
\- \*\*Citation:\*\* 88 Fed. Reg. 26475 (May 1, 2023) ("Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (Regulation F); Time-Barred Debt").  
\- \*\*Holding:\*\* The FDCPA and § 1006.26 prohibit a debt collector from filing or threatening to file a collection lawsuit — including state-law mortgage foreclosure proceedings — on a time-barred debt. The prohibition is \*\*strict liability\*\*: the collector's knowledge of time-barred status is not a defense. Foreclosure attorneys and law firms collecting on residential mortgages can be FDCPA "debt collectors."  
\- \*\*Why it matters:\*\* Confirms the strict-liability standard urged by consumer plaintiffs and rejected by some circuits. Important for zombie second-mortgage foreclosure cases.  
  
\*\*(c) Medical Debt Collection Advisory Opinion\*\*  
\- \*\*Citation:\*\* CFPB Advisory Opinion, October 2024 ("Deceptive and Unfair Collection of Medical Debt").  
\- \*\*Substance:\*\* Identifies as deceptive or unfair under § 1006.18 and § 1006.22 the collection of medical bills that are inflated, not legally owed, or for amounts covered by insurance/financial assistance programs (e.g., Hospital Price Transparency Rule, No Surprises Act, IRS § 501(r) financial-assistance rules). Distinct from the proposed CFPB Medical Debt Reporting Rule.  
  
\*\*(d) Consumer Disclosure / Original-Creditor Information\*\*  
The CFPB has issued informal guidance via Compliance Aid materials and FAQ pages emphasizing that "name of creditor to whom the debt is owed" under § 1006.34(c)(2)(iv) must identify the current creditor (i.e., the entity with present authority to collect), not merely the original lender.  
  
\#\#\# 3. CFPB Supervisory Highlights  
The CFPB publishes Supervisory Highlights two to four times annually, summarizing findings from non-public examinations.  
  
\- \*\*Supervisory Highlights, Issue 34 (Summer 2024)\*\* — "Servicing and Collection of Consumer Debt" — documented validation-notice content errors, opt-out mechanism failures, and recordkeeping deficiencies (§ 1006.100). Examiners cited failures to itemize using a permissible itemization date and missing "current creditor" identification.  
\- \*\*Supervisory Highlights, Issue 30 (Summer 2023)\*\* — first major Reg F supervisory cycle — flagged passive-collection violations under § 1006.30(a) and call-frequency tracking deficiencies under § 1006.14(b).  
\- \*\*Supervisory Highlights, Issue 25 (Fall 2021)\*\* — pre-Reg F effective date; flagged misrepresentations about consumer-report effects of payment, in violation of FDCPA § 1692e(10).  
\- \*\*Recurring findings:\*\*  
 - Failure to send validation notice within 5 days (§ 1006.34(a))  
 - Misrepresentations about credit-report deletion ("pay-for-delete" promises)  
 - Aggregated rather than per-debt 7-in-7 counters  
 - Continued collection after a written dispute, in violation of § 1692g(b) / § 1006.38  
 - Furnishing tradelines without complying with § 1006.30(a) pre-furnishing requirements  
  
\#\#\# 4. CFPB Consent Orders Against Major Debt Collectors  
  
\*\*(a) Encore Capital Group / Midland Funding / Midland Credit Management / Asset Acceptance\*\*  
\- \*\*Initial Consent Order:\*\* September 9, 2015 (2015-CFPB-0022). $42 million in restitution + $10 million civil penalty; halted collection of $125 million in debts; required Encore to obtain original-account documentation before collecting; banned reselling debt; required cease of suit on time-barred debt and disclosure when SOL had expired; banned robo-signed affidavits.  
\- \*\*Subsequent Action:\*\* October 2020 — CFPB sued Encore for violating the 2015 consent order by continuing to file suits without supporting documentation. Settled with additional $79,000 in consumer redress and $15 million penalty, plus extended consent-order term.  
\- \*\*Significance:\*\* Encore is the largest U.S. debt buyer; the orders define industry standards for debt-buyer documentation, time-barred debt disclosures, and prohibition on suing without documentation.  
  
\*\*(b) Portfolio Recovery Associates (PRA)\*\*  
\- \*\*Initial Consent Order:\*\* September 9, 2015. $19 million restitution + $8 million civil penalty; same prohibitions on suing without documentation, robo-signing, and time-barred suits; restrictions on auto-dialed cell phone calls obtained through misleading consents.  
\- \*\*Repeat-Offender Consent Order:\*\* March 23, 2023. CFPB found PRA had violated the 2015 order by collecting on debts known to be inaccurate, suing without documentation, and improperly reporting to credit bureaus. PRA paid more than $24 million ($12.18 million restitution + $12 million penalty).  
\- \*\*Significance:\*\* First major CFPB "repeat offender" enforcement action under Director Chopra's policy of cumulative penalties for serial violators.  
  
\*\*(c) JPMorgan Chase Bank — Debt Sales Practices\*\*  
\- \*\*Consent Order:\*\* July 8, 2015 (CFPB and 47 state AGs). Chase sold credit-card debt portfolios without verifying account information; sold debts past the SOL or already paid; provided robo-signed affidavits to debt buyers. Required to halt sales of accounts lacking documentation; ordered $50 million in restitution + $30 million civil penalty.  
\- \*\*Significance:\*\* Establishes original-creditor-side responsibility for debt sales, an issue often missed in FDCPA analysis (which focuses on the third-party collector).  
  
\*\*(d) Other notable CFPB enforcement actions:\*\*  
\- \*In re Hanna\* (2015) — first CFPB action against a debt-collection law firm using mass-produced complaints without attorney review; alleged § 1692e violations.  
\- \*In re Frederick J. Hanna & Assocs.\* — settled with $3.1 million penalty and supervision of attorney workflow.  
\- \*In re Cross River Bank / RentGrow / various debt collectors\* — recurring smaller-scale enforcement actions on validation, dispute handling, and credit reporting.  
  
\#\#\# 5. Recent CFPB Enforcement Trends  
The CFPB's recent FDCPA enforcement actions (per the November 2025 FDCPA Annual Report) emphasize:  
\- Convenience fees / pay-to-pay fees under § 1692f(1).  
\- Misuse of social media, text, and email communications without complying with § 1006.6.  
\- Furnishing without pre-furnishing communication (§ 1006.30(a) "parking").  
\- "Repeat offender" doctrine — cumulative penalties for collectors who violate prior consent orders.  
\- Collection of mortgage debt past state SOL, especially for "zombie second mortgages."

\#\# B. FTC Guidance  
  
\#\#\# 1. FTC Staff Commentary on the FDCPA (1988)  
\- \*\*Citation:\*\* 53 Fed. Reg. 50097 (Dec. 13, 1988) — "Statements of General Policy or Interpretation; Staff Commentary on the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act."  
\- \*\*Status:\*\* Issued by FTC staff (not the Commission); not legally binding, but the most comprehensive section-by-section analysis of the FDCPA ever published. Courts of all circuits routinely cite the Staff Commentary as persuasive authority on questions left open by the statutory text.  
\- \*\*Continuing relevance:\*\* Although authority transferred to the CFPB in 2011, the Staff Commentary remains uncodified guidance. The CFPB has neither superseded it nor formally endorsed it; Reg F's Final Rule preamble explicitly references the Staff Commentary on several points (e.g., the "consumer" definition's reference to surviving spouses, the place-of-employment communication rules).  
\- \*\*Key passages frequently cited:\*\* Sections on what constitutes a "consumer transaction" (§ 1692a(5)); the third-party-disclosure rule under § 1692c(b); validation-notice timing and overshadowing under § 1692g; the meaning of "least sophisticated consumer."  
  
\#\#\# 2. FTC Annual FDCPA Report  
\- Required by 15 U.S.C. § 1692m(a). The FTC and CFPB jointly produce or coordinate the annual FDCPA Report to Congress.  
\- The CFPB's reports (most recently November 2025) now dominate the joint reporting; the FTC supplements with its own enforcement summary, complaint data, and recommendations.  
\- The reports are persuasive authority on the prevalence of particular violations and on the CFPB/FTC's emerging enforcement priorities.  
  
\#\#\# 3. FTC FDCPA Enforcement (Post-2011)  
The FTC retains FDCPA enforcement authority over non-bank debt collectors (it lost rulemaking authority and lost supervisory authority over "larger participants"). Post-2011 FTC enforcement focuses on:  
\- "Phantom debt" / fictitious-debt scams (collectors purporting to collect debts that do not exist).  
\- Threats of arrest, lawsuits, or wage garnishment that cannot legally be made.  
\- Smaller, non-bank-affiliated collection operations and overseas call centers.  
  
Representative actions:  
\- \*FTC v. Consumer Impact Recovery\* (N.D. Ga. 2024) — collector threatened consumers with arrest, wage garnishment, and lawsuits over fictitious debts; permanent injunction.  
\- \*FTC v. Critical Resolution Mediation\* — phantom-debt enforcement.  
\- \*FTC v. American Credit Crunchers\* — phantom-debt enforcement, $4 million judgment.  
  
The FTC also engages in industry-wide education (Consumer.gov, consumer alerts about scam collectors) and amicus briefs supporting consumer plaintiffs.  
  
\#\#\# 4. FTC Reports on Collection Industry  
\- \*FTC Workshop Report, "Repairing a Broken System"\* (Feb. 2009) — landmark study of the consumer-debt litigation system; identified problems including default judgments based on incomplete documentation, robo-signed affidavits, and improper service. Cited in CFPB Reg F preamble.  
\- \*FTC Report, "The Structure and Practices of the Debt Buying Industry"\* (Jan. 2013) — first empirical study of debt buyers, finding that documentation deteriorates with each sale.

\#\# C. Major Issues Addressed in Guidance  
  
\#\#\# 1. Validation Notice Content (Reg F Overhaul)  
The Final Rule preamble (85 Fed. Reg. 76734) and Reg F § 1006.34 fundamentally restructured § 1692g's validation requirements: a defined itemization date, an itemization table, consumer-response options, and a safe-harbor model form. The CFPB rejected industry requests to dispense with itemization for older debts and rejected consumer-advocate requests for a longer dispute window or required original-creditor documentation.  
  
\#\#\# 2. Pay-to-Pay Fees  
The 2022 CFPB Advisory Opinion (87 Fed. Reg. 39733) resolved a split among district courts in favor of the consumer-protective reading: any unauthorized fee — even a "convenience" fee for paying by a particular method — violates § 1692f(1) and § 1006.22(b).  
  
\#\#\# 3. Time-Barred Debt — "Out-of-Statute" Collection  
The 2023 CFPB Advisory Opinion (88 Fed. Reg. 26475) and Reg F § 1006.26 establish a strict-liability bar on suits and threats of suit. The advisory opinion specifically addressed state-law foreclosure proceedings on time-barred mortgages, an area of active litigation. The Reg F preamble explained the CFPB's deferral of a separate rulemaking on disclosure requirements for collecting (rather than suing on) time-barred debts.  
  
\#\#\# 4. Communications via Text and Email — Reg F Consent  
The CFPB's "Bedrock Procedures" framework under § 1006.6(d) was a substantial regulatory innovation. The CFPB rejected an opt-in model in favor of an opt-out plus permissible-acquisition model. Guidance documents (CFPB Debt Collection Rule FAQs; April 2021 Small Entity Compliance Guide) detail acceptable procedures, including reassigned-number database use for text messages.  
  
\#\#\# 5. Limited-Content Messages and "Initial Communication" Trigger  
The Final Rule preamble explains that a limited-content message is NOT a "communication" under § 1006.2(d), so leaving one does not trigger:  
\- The § 1692g(a) five-day validation-notice clock;  
\- The § 1692e(11) "mini-Miranda" requirement in subsequent communications.  
  
But a limited-content message IS an "attempt to communicate," meaning:  
\- It counts toward the 7-in-7 call-frequency cap (§ 1006.14(b)).  
\- It is subject to time/place restrictions (§ 1006.6(b)).  
  
This bifurcation is the principal innovation of Reg F's voicemail framework.  
  
\#\#\# 6. Debt Buyer Documentation  
The Encore (2015, 2020) and PRA (2015, 2023) consent orders impose enhanced documentation requirements on debt buyers, including:  
\- Possession of original account-level documentation before initiating suit.  
\- Disclosure to consumers of debt-buyer status.  
\- Prohibition on selling debt that lacks documentation, is paid, settled, discharged in bankruptcy, or out-of-statute.  
  
The FTC's 2013 debt-buyer study and the CFPB's Reg F preamble both cite the systematic documentation gap that arises when debts are sold through multiple buyers.  
  
\#\#\# 7. COVID-Related Collection Forbearance  
\- \*\*CFPB Interim Final Rule on Debt Collection Communications During COVID-19\*\* (April 2021): Required debt collectors collecting on landlord-tenant debts to provide the CDC eviction-moratorium disclosure. Now superseded.  
\- \*\*Joint CFPB / FTC / Federal Reserve / OCC / FDIC / NCUA Statement on Working with Customers Affected by Coronavirus\*\* (March 2020): Encouraged forbearance and clarified that good-faith forbearance would not be criticized.  
\- The CFPB's COVID-related FDCPA bulletins generally encouraged accommodation but did not amend statutory requirements.

\#\# Why Agency Guidance Matters for FDCPA Litigation  
  
1\. \*\*Statutory ambiguity is endemic.\*\* The FDCPA is short (less than 12 pages of text), enacted in 1977, and silent on many modern collection practices (email, text, social media, voicemail, electronic payments). Agency guidance fills the gap.  
2\. \*\*Deference doctrines apply asymmetrically.\*\* Reg F itself receives \*Chevron\*-style deference (where still available post-\*Loper Bright\*) or \*Skidmore\* deference. The CFPB's interpretation of its own Reg F regulation in Supplement I and the Final Rule preamble receives \*Auer/Kisor\* deference. Advisory opinions receive \*Skidmore\* weight. The FTC Staff Commentary is treated as persuasive but not entitled to \*Chevron\* or \*Auer\* deference.  
3\. \*\*Consent orders shape industry practice.\*\* The Encore and PRA consent orders set documentation and disclosure standards adopted by competitors even where not legally binding.  
4\. \*\*Supervisory findings predict enforcement.\*\* Themes appearing in two or more Supervisory Highlights frequently presage public enforcement actions and amicus interventions.
