# FDCPA False, Deceptive, or Misleading Representations (15 U.S.C. § 1692e)

**Topic:** Prohibition against false, deceptive, or misleading representations or means in connection with debt collection  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692e (FDCPA § 807)  
**Cross-references:** 15 U.S.C. § 1692f (unfair practices); 15 U.S.C. § 1692d (harassment); 12 C.F.R. § 1006.18 (Regulation F — false or misleading representations); 15 U.S.C. § 1692k (civil liability)  
**Last updated:** May 17, 2026  

---

## Introduction

Section 1692e is the broadest and most frequently litigated provision of the FDCPA. It contains a general prohibition — a debt collector may not "use any false, deceptive, or misleading representation or means in connection with the collection of any debt" — followed by sixteen specifically enumerated violations that are per se violations of the section. The general prohibition functions as a catch-all: conduct need not fit within subsections (1) through (16) to violate § 1692e. A single communication can violate multiple subsections simultaneously and can also violate § 1692f (unfair practices) based on the same conduct.

Courts in the Eleventh Circuit apply the "least sophisticated consumer" standard (sometimes called the "unsophisticated consumer" standard) to evaluate whether a communication is false, deceptive, or misleading. Under this objective test, the question is not whether the particular plaintiff was actually misled, but whether the least sophisticated consumer — one who is uninformed, naive, and trusting, but who possesses a rudimentary amount of information about the world and a willingness to read a collection letter with some care — would be deceived by the communication.

---

## The General Prohibition — § 1692e (Opening Clause)

**Statutory text:** "A debt collector may not use any false, deceptive, or misleading representation or means in connection with the collection of any debt. Without limiting the general application of the foregoing, the following conduct is a violation of this section..."

**Key points:**
- The word "means" extends the prohibition beyond affirmative statements to encompass deceptive conduct, formatting, and omissions that create a false impression.
- The phrase "in connection with the collection of any debt" requires a nexus to collection activity, but courts construe this broadly — any communication whose animating purpose is to induce payment qualifies.
- The list in (1)-(16) is explicitly non-exhaustive ("without limiting the general application of the foregoing").

---

## Specifically Enumerated Violations — § 1692e(1) through § 1692e(16)

### § 1692e(1) — False Affiliation with Government

"The false representation or implication that the debt collector is vouched for, bonded by, or affiliated with the United States or any State, including the use of any badge, uniform, or facsimile thereof."

**Examples:**
- Using a name that implies governmental authority (e.g., "Federal Collection Bureau")
- Using quasi-official seals, insignias, or letterhead designed to mimic government correspondence
- Implying the debt is owed to the government when it is owed to a private party
- Using terms like "Compliance Division" or "Regulatory Enforcement Unit"

**Practice tip:** Look for formatting that mimics IRS notices, court papers, or state agency correspondence. Even subtle design elements — eagles, columns, official-looking fonts — can violate this subsection.

---

### § 1692e(2) — False Character, Amount, or Legal Status of Debt

"The false representation of — (A) the character, amount, or legal status of any debt; or (B) any services rendered or compensation which may be lawfully received by any debt collector for the collection of a debt."

**This is the most commonly violated subsection.** Examples include:

**False amount:**
- Demanding payment of an amount that includes unauthorized fees, interest, or charges not permitted by the original agreement or applicable law
- Failing to credit payments already made
- Misrepresenting the principal balance
- Adding collection costs not authorized by contract or statute

**False character:**
- Representing a time-barred debt as legally enforceable
- Representing a discharged debt as still owed
- Representing an unsecured debt as secured
- Misrepresenting the type of debt (e.g., calling a medical debt a "loan")

**False legal status:**
- Claiming a judgment exists when none has been entered
- Claiming a lien exists when none has been perfected
- Representing that the statute of limitations has not expired when it has
- Misrepresenting the consumer's legal obligations regarding the debt

**Key case law:**
- *Jeter v. Credit Bureau, Inc.*, 760 F.2d 1168 (11th Cir. 1985) — The Eleventh Circuit held that adding unauthorized collection fees to the amount demanded violated § 1692e(2)(A). The court emphasized that any demand for an amount exceeding what is legally owed constitutes a false representation of the amount of the debt.
- *LeBlanc v. Unifin, Inc.*, 601 F.3d 1185 (11th Cir. 2010) — The court held that a debt collector's letter that overstated the amount owed — even if due to a computational error — violated § 1692e(2)(A) because the statute imposes strict liability and does not require intent to deceive.

---

### § 1692e(3) — False Representation of Legal Status of Collector

"The false representation or implication that any individual is an attorney or that any communication is from an attorney."

**Examples:**
- Mass-produced letters bearing an attorney's signature when the attorney exercised no meaningful review
- Using law firm letterhead when no attorney is involved in the collection
- A non-attorney signing a letter "for" an attorney without the attorney's review
- Using "Esq." or "J.D." after a non-attorney's name

**Key case law:**
- *Clomon v. Jackson*, 988 F.2d 1314 (2d Cir. 1993) — Attorney signature on mass-produced dunning letters reviewed by no attorney violated § 1692e(3). The least sophisticated consumer would assume the letter reflected an attorney's professional judgment.
- *Gonzalez v. Kay*, 577 F.3d 600 (5th Cir. 2009) — A letter from a law firm that collected debts violated § 1692e(3) where the named attorney had no meaningful involvement in reviewing the consumer's file.

**Practice tip:** Discovery should focus on whether the attorney whose name appears on the letter actually reviewed the consumer's account, the volume of letters sent versus the attorney's capacity for review, and any "auto-pen" or electronic signature practices.

---

### § 1692e(4) — Threat of Action That Cannot Be Taken or Is Not Intended

"The representation or implication that nonpayment of any debt will result in the arrest or imprisonment of any person or the seizure, garnishment, attachment, or sale of any property or wages of any person unless such action is lawful and the debt collector or creditor intends to take such action."

**Two-pronged test:** The threat violates this subsection if EITHER (1) the threatened action is not lawful, OR (2) the debt collector does not intend to take the action.

**Examples:**
- Threatening to garnish wages in a state that prohibits wage garnishment for the type of debt at issue
- Threatening to seize exempt property (e.g., homestead, Social Security)
- Threatening arrest for failure to pay a civil debt
- Threatening to sue when the collector has no present intention to file suit
- Threatening to repossess when the collector has no security interest or self-help right
- Threatening actions on a time-barred debt

**Practice tip:** Deposition the collector on (1) how many accounts threatened with suit actually resulted in a filed lawsuit (low percentages suggest no intent), and (2) whether the collector obtained management/attorney approval to file suit before sending the threat.

---

### § 1692e(5) — Threat to Take Unauthorized Action

"The threat to take any action that cannot legally be taken or that is not intended to be taken."

**This is the broader version of § 1692e(4)** — it applies to any threatened action, not just arrest, seizure, garnishment, or sale.

**Examples:**
- Threatening to report to a CRA when the collector does not actually furnish data
- Threatening to contact the consumer's employer for purposes other than locating the consumer
- Threatening to file a criminal complaint
- Threatening to take legal action on a time-barred debt
- Threatening to suspend a driver's license or professional license without authority to do so
- Threatening actions requiring a court order when no suit has been filed

---

### § 1692e(6) — False Representation of Threat of Sale or Transfer

"The false representation or implication that a sale, referral, or other transfer of any interest in a debt shall cause the consumer to — (A) lose any claim or defense to payment of the debt; or (B) become subject to any practice prohibited by this subchapter."

**Example:** "If you don't pay now, we will refer this to a collection agency that won't be as nice as us" — implying the next collector might use illegal practices.

---

### § 1692e(7) — False Representation Regarding Consumer's Conduct

"The false representation or implication that the consumer committed any crime or other conduct in order to disgrace the consumer."

**Examples:**
- Claiming the consumer committed fraud when no fraud occurred
- Stating the consumer "stole" services by not paying a bill
- Implying criminal liability for bounced checks when the state's threshold has not been met
- Accusing the consumer of "theft of services"

---

### § 1692e(8) — False Credit Reporting Threats

"Communicating or threatening to communicate to any person credit information which is known or which should be known to be false, including the failure to communicate that a disputed debt is disputed."

**Two parts:**
1. Affirmatively reporting false information to a CRA (or threatening to)
2. Failing to report that a debt is disputed when the consumer has disputed it

**Practice tip:** If the consumer disputed the debt in writing and the collector reports to a CRA without noting the dispute, this is a per se violation regardless of whether the underlying debt is valid.

---

### § 1692e(9) — False Document Misrepresentations

"The use or distribution of any written communication which simulates or is falsely represented to be a document authorized, issued, or approved by any court, official, or agency of the United States or any State, or which creates a false impression as to its source, authorization, or approval."

**Examples:**
- Letters designed to look like court summonses
- Documents formatted to resemble government tax notices
- Using fake case numbers or docket numbers
- Creating the appearance of an official judicial process

---

### § 1692e(10) — False Representations (General Catch-All for Deceptive Means)

"The use of any false representation or deceptive means to collect or attempt to collect any debt or to obtain information concerning a consumer."

**This is the most commonly invoked subsection** because it serves as a general catch-all within the already-general § 1692e prohibition.

**Examples litigated under § 1692e(10):**
- Sending a letter that creates a false sense of urgency ("FINAL NOTICE" when further notices will follow)
- Implying legal action is imminent when it is not
- Using deceptive envelope markings (e.g., "TIME SENSITIVE LEGAL DOCUMENT")
- Failing to disclose that a debt is time-barred when attempting to collect it
- Misrepresenting the collector's identity or authority
- Deceptive settlement offers ("one-time offer" that is actually available indefinitely)
- Using a fictitious company name

---

### § 1692e(11) — Failure to Disclose Communication Is from Debt Collector

"The failure to disclose in the initial written communication with the consumer and, in addition, if the initial communication with the consumer is oral, in that initial oral communication, that the debt collector is attempting to collect a debt and that any information obtained will be used for that purpose, and the failure to disclose in subsequent communications that the communication is from a debt collector, except that this paragraph shall not apply to a formal pleading made in connection with a legal action."

**This is the "mini-Miranda" requirement:**
- Initial communication (written or oral): Must state (1) the collector is attempting to collect a debt AND (2) any information obtained will be used for that purpose
- Subsequent communications: Must state the communication is from a debt collector
- Exception: Formal pleadings in legal actions need not include the disclosure

**Practice tip:** Check every piece of correspondence. Many collectors omit the mini-Miranda from emails, text messages, or informal follow-up letters. Each omission is a separate violation.

---

### § 1692e(12) — False Representation of Nonjudicial Action

"The false representation or implication that accounts have been turned over to innocent purchasers for value."

---

### § 1692e(13) — False Representation of Document's Legal Significance

"The false representation or implication that documents are legal process."

**Examples:**
- Sending a "SUMMONS" that is not actually a court-issued summons
- Labeling a collection letter as a "SUBPOENA" or "WARRANT"
- Using "NOTICE OF LEGAL ACTION" when no suit has been filed

---

### § 1692e(14) — Use of False Business Name

"The use of any business, company, or organization name other than the true name of the debt collector's business, company, or organization."

**Practice tip:** Check state business registrations. If the collector uses a DBA that is not properly registered, or uses multiple names to confuse consumers about who is contacting them, this subsection applies.

---

### § 1692e(15) — False Representation About Document as Not Legal Process

"The false representation or implication that documents are not legal process forms or do not require action by the consumer."

**Example:** A legitimate court summons sent with a cover letter stating "no response is required" or "this is not a legal document."

---

### § 1692e(16) — False Representation of Collector's Cooperation with Law Enforcement

"The false representation or implication that a debt collector operates or is employed by a consumer reporting agency."

## The Least Sophisticated Consumer Standard

### Standard Applied in the Eleventh Circuit

The Eleventh Circuit applies the "least sophisticated consumer" standard to determine whether a communication violates § 1692e. This is an objective test — it does not ask what the actual plaintiff understood, but what the hypothetical least sophisticated consumer would understand.

**Elements of the standard:**
- The consumer is uninformed, naive, and trusting
- The consumer possesses a rudimentary amount of information about the world
- The consumer is willing to read the communication with some care
- The standard protects against deception without requiring proof of actual confusion
- Bizarre or idiosyncratic interpretations are excluded — the consumer is unsophisticated but not irrational

**Key Eleventh Circuit formulations:**
- *Jeter v. Credit Bureau, Inc.*, 760 F.2d 1168 (11th Cir. 1985) — One of the earliest circuit-level adoptions of the standard in an FDCPA context. The court assessed whether the collection communication would mislead an unsophisticated debtor.
- *LeBlanc v. Unifin, Inc.*, 601 F.3d 1185 (11th Cir. 2010) — The court applied the least sophisticated consumer test to determine whether a letter demanding an incorrect amount was misleading, holding that even an inadvertent overstatement of the balance violates § 1692e because the test is objective and does not require intent.

**Practical implications:**
- Ambiguous language is construed in favor of the consumer
- Fine print, disclaimers, or legalese cannot cure a misleading headline
- The overall "net impression" of the communication controls
- Courts may resolve the question as a matter of law — no need for expert testimony or consumer surveys

## Common Violations in Practice

### 1. False Amount Owed
- Adding unauthorized fees, interest, or collection costs to the demanded balance
- Failing to apply partial payments
- Demanding post-judgment interest at an incorrect rate
- Including attorney fees not authorized by contract or statute

### 2. False Legal Status
- Representing a time-barred debt as legally enforceable
- Failing to disclose that a debt has been discharged in bankruptcy
- Claiming a judgment lien exists when it has expired
- Representing a subordinate lien as a first lien

### 3. Impersonating Attorneys
- Mass-mailed attorney letters without meaningful attorney review
- Non-attorneys using law firm letterhead
- Using "Legal Department" as a sender when no attorneys are involved
- Paralegals signing letters without identifying their role

### 4. Threatening Actions the Collector Cannot or Will Not Take
- Threatening suit on a time-barred debt
- Threatening garnishment in a state that limits garnishment
- Threatening repossession without a security interest
- Threatening criminal prosecution for a civil debt
- Threatening actions the collector's client has not authorized

### 5. False Urgency
- "FINAL NOTICE" when further collection will continue
- "ACT WITHIN 48 HOURS" with no legal basis for the deadline
- "YOUR ACCOUNT HAS BEEN REFERRED TO OUR LITIGATION DEPARTMENT" when no such referral occurred
- Artificial deadlines designed to pressure payment before the consumer can exercise dispute rights

## Relationship Between § 1692e and § 1692f

The same conduct frequently violates BOTH § 1692e (false representations) and § 1692f (unfair practices). Courts permit plaintiffs to assert both claims based on identical facts:

- Collecting unauthorized fees: violates § 1692e(2)(A) (false representation of amount) AND § 1692f(1) (collecting amounts not authorized by agreement or law)
- Threatening unauthorized action: violates § 1692e(4)/(5) AND may constitute an unfair practice under § 1692f's catch-all
- Deceptive communications about payment options: may violate both § 1692e(10) and § 1692f

**Practice tip:** Always plead both sections when the facts support it. Statutory damages are available under each independent violation, and some courts have found that distinct violations of different sections can support separate statutory damage awards.

---

## Strict Liability Standard

Section 1692e imposes strict liability — the debt collector's intent is irrelevant. A communication that is objectively false, deceptive, or misleading violates the statute regardless of whether the collector intended to mislead or knew the statement was false.

**Key implications:**
- Computational errors in balance amounts violate the statute
- Good-faith reliance on creditor-provided information does not excuse false statements
- The bona fide error defense under § 1692k(c) is the only escape valve, and post-*Jerman*, it does not cover legal errors

---

## Materiality Requirement (Circuit Split)

Some circuits have imposed an additional "materiality" requirement — the false statement must be capable of influencing the consumer's decision-making to be actionable. The Eleventh Circuit has not definitively adopted or rejected a standalone materiality threshold, but its application of the least sophisticated consumer standard inherently screens out trivially misleading statements.

**Practice considerations:**
- In jurisdictions requiring materiality, focus discovery on how the misrepresentation could have affected the consumer's decisions about payment, dispute, or legal consultation
- Even in materiality-required circuits, false statements about the amount owed, legal status of the debt, or threatened consequences are virtually always material

## Practice Tips for Litigation

### Building the Case
1. **Preserve all communications** — letters, voicemails, emails, text messages, envelopes
2. **Compare demanded amount to original contract** — check whether fees, interest, and costs are authorized
3. **Verify legal status** — confirm statute of limitations, judgment status, lien status, bankruptcy discharge
4. **Check attorney involvement** — in discovery, ask about the signing attorney's actual review of the file
5. **Examine the collector's form letters** — request template libraries to show systemic violations (strengthens class claims)

### Discovery Targets
- Collector's policies and procedures for verifying account information before dunning
- Volume of letters sent versus attorney review capacity
- Training materials on representations permitted to consumers
- Records of consumer complaints about false statements
- Collector's call scripts and approved language

### Damages
- Actual damages: emotional distress, out-of-pocket expenses (e.g., attorney consultation fees before filing suit), credit damage
- Statutory damages: up to $1,000 per action under § 1692k(a)(2)(A)
- Attorney fees: mandatory for prevailing plaintiffs under § 1692k(a)(3)
- Class action: up to the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of the debt collector's net worth under § 1692k(a)(2)(B)

## Regulation F Overlay — 12 C.F.R. § 1006.18

Regulation F, effective November 30, 2021, supplements § 1692e by:
- Codifying the prohibition against false representations regarding time-barred debt
- Requiring specific disclosures when collecting debts that the collector knows or should know are beyond the applicable statute of limitations
- Clarifying that certain electronic communications can constitute "false representations" if they misrepresent the nature of the communication
- Providing model language that creates a safe harbor for certain disclosures

## Checklist: Evaluating a § 1692e Claim

- [ ] Is the defendant a "debt collector" under § 1692a(6)?
- [ ] Was the communication "in connection with the collection of a debt"?
- [ ] Identify the specific false, deceptive, or misleading statement or implication
- [ ] Does the statement fit within a specific subsection (1)-(16)? (If yes, per se violation)
- [ ] Would the least sophisticated consumer be misled?
- [ ] Is the misrepresentation material (if required in the jurisdiction)?
- [ ] What are the actual damages? (emotional distress, financial harm, credit damage)
- [ ] Is there a pattern suggesting class-wide conduct?
- [ ] Has the one-year statute of limitations under § 1692k(d) run?
- [ ] Does the bona fide error defense potentially apply? (procedural error only — not legal error)
