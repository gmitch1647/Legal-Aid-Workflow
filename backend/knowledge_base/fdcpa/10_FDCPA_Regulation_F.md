# FDCPA Regulation F — CFPB Implementing Rule (12 C.F.R. Part 1006)

**Topic:** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau Regulation F; implementing regulations for the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act; call frequency limits; electronic communications; limited-content messages; validation notice model form; time-barred debt disclosures; deceased consumer protections  
**Primary regulation:** 12 C.F.R. Part 1006 (Regulation F)  
**Authorizing statute:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692-1692p (FDCPA); Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act § 1089  
**Effective date:** November 30, 2021  
**Last updated:** May 17, 2026  

---

## Introduction

Regulation F is the CFPB's comprehensive implementing regulation for the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act. Published as a final rule on October 30, 2020 (with supplemental rules published in December 2020), and effective November 30, 2021, Regulation F represents the first major regulatory implementation of the FDCPA since the statute's enactment in 1977. The regulation addresses issues that the FDCPA's text left ambiguous — particularly regarding modern communication technologies (email, text messages, social media) and provides concrete standards where the statute had only general prohibitions (call frequency, validation notice content).

Regulation F does not replace the FDCPA — it supplements it. A debt collector must comply with BOTH the statute and the regulation. In some areas, Regulation F is more specific than the statute; in others, it provides safe harbors for conduct that might otherwise be challenged under the statute's general prohibitions. Violations of Regulation F are treated as violations of the FDCPA for enforcement purposes.

## Scope and Definitions — § 1006.2

### Definition of "Debt Collector" Under Regulation F — § 1006.2(i)

Regulation F defines "debt collector" to include, significantly:

**"A person who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another"** — tracking the FDCPA statutory definition.

**CRITICAL ADDITION:** Regulation F also includes in the definition a person who acquired a debt and collects it, where **"the debt was in default at the time it was obtained."** This provision effectively brings debt buyers within the scope of Regulation F, partially counteracting the Supreme Court's decision in *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 582 U.S. 79 (2017), which held that debt buyers were not "debt collectors" under the statutory text because they collect for themselves rather than for "another."

**Practical significance:**
- Post-November 30, 2021, debt buyers who acquired defaulted debts are "debt collectors" under Regulation F
- They must comply with all Regulation F requirements (call frequency limits, validation notices, electronic communication rules, etc.)
- Violations of Regulation F by debt buyers can be enforced by the CFPB and state attorneys general
- Whether private plaintiffs can bring FDCPA § 1692k claims based solely on Regulation F violations by *Henson*-exempt debt buyers is an open and evolving legal question — some courts view Reg F violations as FDCPA violations actionable under § 1692k; others may require independent statutory violations

### Other Key Definitions

- **"Communication"** (§ 1006.2(d)): Conveying information regarding a debt directly or indirectly through any medium — same as § 1692a(2)
- **"Consumer"** (§ 1006.2(e)): Any natural person obligated or allegedly obligated to pay any debt
- **"Debt"** (§ 1006.2(f)): Any obligation or alleged obligation arising out of a primarily personal, family, or household transaction — same as § 1692a(5)
- **"Limited-content message"** (§ 1006.2(j)): A special category of voicemail/message that is NOT a "communication" — see detailed discussion below

## Call Frequency Limits — § 1006.14

### The "7-in-7" Rule

Regulation F establishes presumptions regarding telephone call frequency for purposes of § 1692d(5) (telephone harassment):

### Presumption of Violation — § 1006.14(b)(2)

A debt collector is PRESUMED to violate § 1692d(5) if it places a telephone call to a person in connection with the collection of a particular debt:
- **More than seven times within seven consecutive days**, OR
- **Within seven days after having had a telephone conversation with the person about the particular debt**

### Presumption of Compliance — § 1006.14(b)(3)

A debt collector is PRESUMED to comply with § 1692d(5) if it:
- Places **seven or fewer telephone calls** within seven consecutive days regarding a particular debt, AND
- Does NOT call within **seven days after a telephone conversation** about that particular debt

### Key Interpretive Points

**"Particular debt" counting:**
- The limits apply PER DEBT, not per consumer — if a consumer owes three debts to the same collector, the collector may make 7 calls per debt (21 total) within seven days
- However, even if per-debt limits are technically met, excessive aggregate calling may violate § 1692d's general harassment prohibition

**What counts as a "telephone call":**
- Answered calls
- Unanswered calls (including those that go to voicemail)
- Calls where the consumer hangs up immediately
- Calls from different numbers by the same collector
- Does NOT include text messages (texts are governed by separate electronic communication rules)
- Does NOT include limited-content messages (which are not "communications") — but the ACT of placing the call that results in a limited-content message voicemail DOES count toward the 7-call limit

**"Telephone conversation":**
- A two-way live dialogue between the collector and the person
- Leaving a voicemail is NOT a "conversation" — it does not trigger the 7-day post-conversation blackout
- A brief exchange where the person asks the collector not to call is a "conversation"

**Rebuttable presumptions:**
- The 7-in-7 presumptions are REBUTTABLE — a collector CAN be found harassing even with fewer than 7 calls if the circumstances show intent to annoy/abuse/harass
- Conversely, a collector that exceeds 7 calls may rebut the presumption of violation (though this is difficult in practice)

**Practice tip:** Obtain complete call logs from the collector (through discovery of their dialer system records) and from the consumer (phone records). Map each call to the specific debt being collected. If any 7-day window shows more than 7 calls regarding the same debt, there is a presumed violation.

## Electronic Communications — § 1006.6

### Email and Text Message Communications

Regulation F establishes a framework for debt collectors to communicate with consumers electronically (email and text messages), subject to consumer protection requirements:

### Opt-Out Requirements — § 1006.6(e)

Every electronic communication (email or text) sent by a debt collector must include:
- A **clear and conspicuous statement** describing a reasonable and simple method the consumer can use to opt out of receiving further electronic communications
- The opt-out method must be **accessible** — the consumer must be able to opt out using the same medium (e.g., reply STOP for texts; click unsubscribe for emails)
- Once the consumer opts out, the collector must honor the opt-out **promptly** and cease electronic communications through that medium

### Conditions for Electronic Communication — § 1006.6(d)

A debt collector may send an email or text to a consumer only if:

**For email (§ 1006.6(d)(4)):**
- The consumer used the email address to communicate with the collector about the debt
- The consumer gave prior consent (directly to the collector) to communicate by email
- The collector received the email address from the prior creditor and the prior creditor used it to communicate with the consumer about the account (with certain safeguards)

**For text messages (§ 1006.6(d)(5)):**
- The consumer used the phone number to communicate with the collector about the debt by text
- The consumer gave prior consent to communicate by text

### Privacy Protections for Electronic Communications — § 1006.6(d)(3)

Before sending an email or text, the collector must have procedures in place to:
- Ensure the email/phone is not a work address/number (unless the consumer used it to communicate about the debt)
- Ensure the communication will not be seen by unauthorized third parties (especially relevant for shared email accounts or shared phone lines)

### Practice Tips for Electronic Communications

- **Check opt-out compliance:** Was a clear opt-out mechanism included in every electronic communication? Was it honored when exercised?
- **Check authorization:** Did the collector have a proper basis to email/text the consumer? Request evidence of how the collector obtained the email address or phone number.
- **Third-party exposure:** Did the email/text reveal debt information that could be viewed by others (employer email, shared family phone)?
- **Frequency:** While the 7-in-7 call rule does not technically apply to texts, excessive texting may still violate the § 1692d catch-all prohibition on harassment.

## Limited-Content Messages — § 1006.2(j)

### Definition and Purpose

A "limited-content message" is a voicemail for a consumer that includes ONLY the following content:
1. A business name that does not indicate the caller is a debt collector
2. A request that the consumer reply to the message
3. The name or names of natural persons whom the consumer can contact to reply
4. A telephone number the consumer can use to reply

**And optionally:**
5. A salutation
6. The date and time of the message
7. A statement that if the consumer replies, the consumer may speak with any representative of the debt collector
8. A suggested date and time for the consumer to reply

### Why This Matters

A limited-content message is **NOT a "communication"** under the FDCPA/Regulation F definition. This means:
- No mini-Miranda disclosure is required (§ 1692e(11))
- No validation notice is triggered
- The message does not reveal to third parties who might hear it that the consumer is being contacted by a debt collector
- It resolves the *Owen v. I.C. System* tension between disclosure requirements and third-party privacy

### Strict Requirements

If the voicemail includes ANY content beyond what is permitted (items 1-8 above), it loses limited-content message status and becomes a full "communication" — triggering all FDCPA disclosure requirements.

**What would disqualify a limited-content message:**
- Mentioning the debt or the amount
- Stating that the call is from a debt collector
- Referencing the creditor's name
- Including urgency language ("important financial matter")
- Stating consequences of not calling back
- Including the account number

**Practice tip:** If you receive a voicemail from a collector that references the debt, the creditor, the amount owed, or the words "debt collector" — it is NOT a valid limited-content message and must comply with all FDCPA communication requirements, including the mini-Miranda.

## Validation Notice Model Form — § 1006.34(d)

### Model Form B-1

Regulation F provides a model validation notice form (Appendix B, Model Form B-1) that creates a safe harbor. A debt collector that uses Model Form B-1 substantially as provided is deemed to comply with § 1006.34(c) (validation information requirements) and § 1692g(a) (statutory validation requirements).

### Required Content Under § 1006.34(c)

The validation notice must include (see detailed discussion in File 06):

1. **Debt collector information:** Name, mailing address, telephone number for live person
2. **Consumer information:** Name and mailing address
3. **Creditor information:** Name of current creditor; name of original creditor if different
4. **Account information:** Account number (may be truncated); last four digits for credit cards
5. **Itemization date and amount:** Reference point amount (last statement, charge-off, last payment, or transaction date) plus itemization of how balance reached current amount
6. **Current amount owed:** As of the date of the notice
7. **Consumer rights disclosures:** Dispute rights, verification rights, 30-day window, original creditor request rights
8. **Consumer response information:** How to dispute or request information (mailing address; electronic method if available)
9. **Certain disclosures for specific situations:**
   - If the debt is time-barred: specific disclosures about unenforceability (§ 1006.34(c)(2)(iii))
   - If the consumer has made a partial payment that may restart the SOL: disclosure of that risk (if applicable under state law)

### Safe Harbor Effect

Use of Model Form B-1 creates a presumption of compliance. This means:
- Debt collectors who use the model form are protected from claims that their validation notice was insufficient
- Debt collectors who do NOT use the model form must demonstrate their alternative format includes all required information and does not overshadow dispute rights
- The safe harbor applies to the form's content and structure — not to factual inaccuracies (a model-form notice stating the wrong amount still violates § 1692e(2))

**Practice tip:** Always compare the collector's actual validation notice to Model Form B-1. Identify any deviations — missing itemization, absent creditor information, unclear dispute instructions, wrong dates or amounts. Deviations do not automatically violate the rule (the form is a safe harbor, not a mandate), but they expose the collector to challenges about sufficiency.

## Time-Barred Debt Disclosures — § 1006.26(b)

### Requirement

If a debt collector knows or should know that the applicable statute of limitations has expired, the collector must not bring or threaten to bring a legal action against the consumer to collect the debt. § 1006.26(b).

### Disclosure in Validation Notice

If the debt is time-barred, the validation notice must include a disclosure informing the consumer that the debt may be too old for the collector to sue on, and that making a payment may restart the statute of limitations (if applicable under state law). § 1006.34(c)(2)(iii).

### Prohibition on Revival Strategies

Regulation F prohibits collectors from engaging in conduct designed to induce the consumer to restart the statute of limitations without the consumer's knowledge — for example:
- Accepting a small payment without disclosing that it may restart the SOL
- Seeking a "written acknowledgment" of the debt in a form that may restart the SOL under state law
- Offering a "settlement" whose acceptance restarts the SOL

### Practice Significance

Before Regulation F, the question of whether collecting on time-barred debts was inherently deceptive was a circuit split issue. Regulation F provides clarity:
- Suing or threatening to sue on a time-barred debt is explicitly prohibited
- Specific disclosures are required when collecting time-barred debts
- Failure to provide these disclosures violates Regulation F and, by extension, the FDCPA

## Deceased Consumer Protections — § 1006.6(c) and § 1006.34(c)(4)

### Permitted Communications About Deceased Consumers

When a consumer dies, a debt collector may communicate about the deceased consumer's debt only with:
- The deceased consumer's spouse
- The deceased consumer's parent (if the deceased was a minor)
- The deceased consumer's legal guardian
- The executor or administrator of the deceased consumer's estate
- Any other person authorized to act on behalf of the deceased consumer's estate

### Restrictions

- The collector may NOT contact general family members (siblings, adult children) about the debt unless they are authorized representatives of the estate
- The collector MAY contact family members for the limited purpose of obtaining contact information for the estate's representative (location information under § 1692b)
- The collector must not state or imply that surviving family members are personally liable for the debt unless they are in fact liable (e.g., joint account holder, guarantor, community property state obligations)

### Validation Notice for Deceased Consumers

If the collector sends a validation notice regarding a deceased consumer's debt, it must be addressed to the estate representative and must not imply personal liability of the recipient unless the recipient is independently obligated.

## Relationship Between Regulation F and the FDCPA Statute

### Hierarchy

1. **The FDCPA statute (15 U.S.C. §§ 1692-1692p)** establishes the baseline prohibitions and private right of action
2. **Regulation F (12 C.F.R. Part 1006)** implements and interprets the statute — it may impose additional requirements but cannot subtract from statutory protections
3. **CFPB Official Interpretations (Supplement I to Part 1006)** provide guidance on the regulation's meaning — not independently enforceable but inform compliance expectations

### Where Regulation F Adds to the Statute

- **Call frequency:** The statute prohibits harassment but provides no numeric threshold; Regulation F creates the 7-in-7 presumption
- **Electronic communications:** The statute was written before email and texting; Regulation F creates a framework for digital communications
- **Limited-content messages:** Not addressed in the statute; Regulation F creates a safe harbor for voicemails
- **Validation notice content:** The statute requires five elements; Regulation F requires itemization, creditor identification, contact information, and more
- **Time-barred debt:** The statute is ambiguous about non-suit collection of time-barred debt; Regulation F requires specific disclosures
- **Debt buyer coverage:** The statute (per *Henson*) may not cover debt buyers; Regulation F's definition includes them

### Where Regulation F Creates Safe Harbors

- **Model validation notice:** Substantially following Model Form B-1 = presumed compliance with § 1692g
- **Call frequency safe harbor:** 7 or fewer calls in 7 days + no call within 7 days of conversation = presumed compliance with § 1692d(5)
- **Limited-content messages:** Properly formatted messages = not "communications" under FDCPA

### Enforcement

- **CFPB enforcement:** Can bring administrative actions or civil suits for Regulation F violations
- **State AG enforcement:** State attorneys general can enforce the FDCPA and Regulation F
- **Private right of action:** FDCPA § 1692k provides a private right of action for "any debt collector who fails to comply with any provision of this subchapter" — Regulation F violations are generally treated as FDCPA violations for § 1692k purposes, though some defendants argue that Regulation F creates "new" obligations beyond the statute that may not be privately actionable
- **FTC Act § 5:** Unfair or deceptive practices in debt collection can be enforced under the FTC Act as well

## Regulation F Compliance Issues in Practice

### Common Violations

1. **Exceeding call frequency limits** — calling more than 7 times in 7 days on a single debt
2. **Inadequate opt-out mechanisms** — failing to include clear opt-out instructions in emails/texts; failing to honor opt-outs
3. **Deficient validation notices** — missing itemization; failing to identify original creditor; incorrect amounts; missing telephone number for live person
4. **Improper electronic communications** — emailing without proper consent; texting work phone numbers; failing to ensure privacy
5. **Limited-content message failures** — including prohibited content in voicemails intended as limited-content messages
6. **Time-barred debt disclosures** — failing to include required disclosures when collecting on time-barred debts; threatening suit on expired debts
7. **Deceased consumer violations** — contacting family members who are not authorized estate representatives; implying personal liability

### Discovery in Regulation F Cases

- **Call logs and dialer records:** Essential for frequency violations — request complete records showing every call attempt (answered or not) for the relevant period, organized by debt/account
- **Electronic communication records:** All emails and texts sent to the consumer, including headers showing the technology used and opt-out mechanisms
- **Validation notice templates:** The collector's standard form compared to Model Form B-1; evidence of how itemization amounts are calculated
- **Policies and procedures:** The collector's written policies for call frequency management, electronic communication, opt-out processing, time-barred debt identification, and deceased consumer handling
- **Training materials:** Evidence of what staff are trained to do regarding compliance with Regulation F requirements
- **Compliance monitoring records:** Audits, quality assurance reviews, and error reports showing whether the collector actually monitors its own compliance

## Practice Tips

### For Consumer Advocates

1. **Count calls carefully:** Obtain phone records and count all attempts (not just connections) within rolling 7-day windows. Remember the count is per-debt — if the collector groups multiple debts, determine which debt each call concerned.

2. **Check electronic communication authorization:** How did the collector obtain the email/phone number? Was there proper consent? Was an opt-out mechanism included and honored?

3. **Analyze the validation notice against Model Form B-1:** Any significant deviation from the model form removes safe harbor protection and exposes the collector to challenges on sufficiency.

4. **Time-barred debt checklist:**
   - Is the debt beyond the applicable SOL?
   - Did the collector know or should it have known?
   - Were required disclosures provided?
   - Did the collector threaten or take legal action?
   - Was the consumer induced to restart the SOL without disclosure?

5. **Voicemail analysis:** Listen to every voicemail. Does it qualify as a limited-content message (only permitted elements 1-8)? If not, was the mini-Miranda included? Was the communication compliant with § 1692c(b) (third-party disclosure)?

6. **Debt buyer defendants:** Post-*Henson*, debt buyers may argue they are not "debt collectors" under the statute. Respond with: (a) Regulation F's expanded definition covers them; (b) they may also collect for others (meeting the statutory definition independently); (c) state law claims apply regardless.

### Damages for Regulation F Violations

- Regulation F violations are FDCPA violations — same damages framework applies
- Actual damages (no cap)
- Statutory damages (up to $1,000 individual; lesser of $500,000 or 1% net worth for class)
- Attorney fees (mandatory for prevailing plaintiff)
- Costs

### Regulation F Class Actions

The uniform, policy-driven nature of Regulation F violations makes them particularly suitable for class treatment:
- Call frequency: The collector's dialer system either exceeded limits systematically or it did not — common proof
- Validation notice deficiencies: Form letters are identical across the class — no individualized inquiry needed
- Electronic communication violations: Systemic failures to include opt-out mechanisms affect all recipients equally
- Time-barred debt disclosures: A collector's policy of not checking SOL status or not providing required disclosures affects all consumers with time-barred debts

## Checklist: Evaluating Regulation F Compliance

### Call Frequency (§ 1006.14)
- [ ] Count all call attempts in any 7-day window for each debt
- [ ] Did calls exceed 7 in any 7-day window? (Presumption of violation)
- [ ] Was a call placed within 7 days after a telephone conversation? (Presumption of violation)
- [ ] Even if within 7 calls: was there intent to harass? (Catch-all applies)

### Electronic Communications (§ 1006.6)
- [ ] Did the collector have proper authorization to email/text?
- [ ] Was a clear opt-out mechanism included?
- [ ] Was the opt-out honored when exercised?
- [ ] Were privacy protections observed (no work email without consent; no shared phone concerns)?

### Validation Notice (§ 1006.34)
- [ ] Provided within 5 days of initial communication?
- [ ] Includes debt collector name, address, and telephone number?
- [ ] Includes consumer name and address?
- [ ] Includes current creditor name?
- [ ] Includes original creditor name (if different)?
- [ ] Includes itemization from reference date to current balance?
- [ ] Includes current amount owed?
- [ ] Includes clear dispute rights disclosure?
- [ ] Includes instructions for how to dispute?
- [ ] No overshadowing language?

### Time-Barred Debt (§ 1006.26)
- [ ] Is the debt beyond the applicable SOL?
- [ ] Did the collector know or should it have known?
- [ ] Were required disclosures provided in the validation notice?
- [ ] Did the collector refrain from threatening or filing suit?
- [ ] Were revival risks disclosed before accepting payment?

### Limited-Content Messages (§ 1006.2(j))
- [ ] Does the voicemail contain ONLY permitted elements?
- [ ] No reference to the debt, amount, creditor, or collection?
- [ ] Business name used does not indicate debt collection?
- [ ] If the message fails the limited-content test: did it include the mini-Miranda?

### Deceased Consumers (§ 1006.6(c))
- [ ] Was the communication directed to an authorized person?
- [ ] Was personal liability implied only where it actually exists?
- [ ] Were family contacts limited to location-information purposes?
