\#\# 1. Overview  
  
The FDCPA's "back half" — §§ 1692h, 1692i, 1692j — fills three operational gaps that the headline sections do not directly reach: how payments are applied when the consumer owes multiple debts to the same collector; where a collector may sue; and the so-called "flat-rating" practice of furnishing collection forms designed to falsely suggest third-party collector involvement. The provisions are less frequently litigated than §§ 1692e, 1692f, and 1692g, but each is a frequent source of liability when violated, and each carries strict-liability consequences subject only to the § 1692k(c) bona fide error defense.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# 2. § 1692h — Multiple debts and payment application  
  
\#\#\# 2.1 The rule  
  
\> "If any consumer owes multiple debts and makes any single payment to any debt collector with respect to such debts, such debt collector may not apply such payment to any debt which is disputed by the consumer and, where applicable, shall apply such payment in accordance with the consumer's directions."  
\> — 15 U.S.C. § 1692h  
  
Two affirmative obligations:  
  
\- \*\*No application to disputed debts.\*\* When the consumer owes more than one debt to a single collector and makes a payment, the collector cannot apply any portion of that payment to a disputed debt.  
\- \*\*Honor consumer directions.\*\* The collector must apply the payment as the consumer directs. "Where applicable" recognizes that the consumer may have given no direction; in that case, § 1692h does not impose a default rule and the collector is generally free to apply the payment as it sees fit (subject to other law, such as state allocation rules or the underlying contract).  
  
\#\#\# 2.2 Triggers and scope  
  
\- \*\*"Multiple debts."\*\* § 1692h applies only when the same collector is collecting on more than one debt from the same consumer. It does not apply to a single debt subject to itemized charges.  
\- \*\*"Disputed."\*\* The dispute need not be a written § 1692g(b) dispute — an oral dispute or a dispute communicated in any form to the collector suffices. The dispute can be partial: only the disputed portion of a specific debt is protected from allocation.  
\- \*\*"Any single payment."\*\* Reaches lump-sum payments, partial payments, and recurring scheduled payments equally.  
  
\#\#\# 2.3 Common compliance issues  
  
\- \*\*Auto-allocation algorithms.\*\* Modern collection platforms often apply payments by "oldest debt first" or by "highest balance first." Without an exception coded for disputed debts, such systems systematically violate § 1692h.  
\- \*\*Payment plan agreements.\*\* Form payment-plan agreements that lock in an allocation contrary to the consumer's later direction can violate § 1692h if executed under collection pressure that overrides the right to redirect.  
\- \*\*Bulk settlement language.\*\* "Global" settlement letters that purport to apply a payment across all accounts (including disputed ones) without express consumer direction violate § 1692h.  
\- \*\*Account merging.\*\* Collectors who combine multiple debts under a single "consolidated" account number can lose track of which underlying debt is disputed, leading to inadvertent allocation violations.  
  
\#\#\# 2.4 Practical notes  
  
\- \*\*Pleading.\*\* Plead § 1692h as a stand-alone claim when payments have been applied across debts contrary to consumer direction. Pair with § 1692e(10) (deceptive application disclosures) where the collector's payment-allocation receipt is misleading.  
\- \*\*Discovery.\*\* Demand the collector's payment-allocation policy, the algorithm rules, the consumer's payment history, and any consumer correspondence concerning allocation directions.  
\- \*\*Damages.\*\* Statutory damages up to $1,000 under § 1692k; actual damages where the misallocation caused interest accrual, default, or credit-reporting harm.

\#\# 3. § 1692i — Venue for legal actions  
  
\#\#\# 3.1 The rule  
  
\> "Any debt collector who brings any legal action on a debt against any consumer shall —  
\> (1) in the case of an action to enforce an interest in real property securing the consumer's obligation, bring such action only in a judicial district or similar legal entity in which such real property is located; or  
\> (2) in the case of an action not described in paragraph (1), bring such action only in the judicial district or similar legal entity —  
\> (A) in which such consumer signed the contract sued upon; or  
\> (B) in which such consumer resides at the commencement of the action."  
\> — 15 U.S.C. § 1692i(a)  
  
Subsection (b): "Nothing in this subchapter shall be construed to authorize the bringing of legal actions by debt collectors."  
  
\#\#\# 3.2 Real-property actions — § 1692i(a)(1)  
  
A collector enforcing a security interest in real property must sue only where the property is located. The provision applies whether the underlying action is mortgage foreclosure, lien enforcement, or any action seeking to reach the property. The Supreme Court's decision in \*Obduskey v. McCarthy & Holthus LLP\*, 586 U.S. 466 (2019), held that entities engaged solely in non-judicial foreclosure are not "debt collectors" for most FDCPA purposes; § 1692i(a)(1) accordingly applies primarily to judicial foreclosure and related actions by entities that otherwise meet the § 1692a(6) definition.  
  
\#\#\# 3.3 Non-real-property actions — § 1692i(a)(2)  
  
Two permissible venues for non-real-property collection suits:  
  
\- \*\*Where the consumer signed the contract sued upon.\*\* Determined by the place of execution as evidenced by the signed contract. For online or click-through contracts, the place of "signing" is the consumer's location when they assented. For pre-1990s in-person credit applications, the location is typically the merchant's storefront or the bank branch.  
\- \*\*Where the consumer resides at the commencement of the action.\*\* "Commencement" is the date the complaint is filed (or the date of service under state procedural rules that treat service as commencement). A consumer who moves before suit cannot be sued in the former district under this prong.  
  
The two prongs are \*\*disjunctive\*\* — the collector may sue in either, but only in those venues. Suing in a third venue (e.g., the collector's own headquarters, or a court convenient to its counsel) violates § 1692i.  
  
\#\#\# 3.4 "Judicial district or similar legal entity"  
  
The phrase has produced doctrinal complexity. Three interpretations have emerged:  
  
\- \*\*State trial-court district / county-level.\*\* Most federal courts treat the smallest unit of geographic jurisdiction in the relevant state court system (e.g., county, district court division, or municipal court division) as the "judicial district or similar legal entity." See \*Newsom v. Friedman\*, 76 F.3d 813 (7th Cir. 1996) (Illinois municipal-court division of Cook County held to be the relevant "judicial district").  
\- \*\*State-court geographic district above county.\*\* A minority of courts read "judicial district" more broadly (e.g., a multi-county state circuit) where local court rules permit suit anywhere within the broader district.  
\- \*\*Federal-court district.\*\* When the collector sues in federal court, the federal district is the relevant "judicial district." Removed cases analyze venue under the state-court rules at the time of filing.  
  
The practical rule: if state court structures permit assignment to a sub-county venue and the collector chose a venue other than the consumer's residence or contract-signing venue within the county, the choice may violate § 1692i even if both are within the same broader court system.  
  
\#\#\# 3.5 Common debt-collector violations  
  
\- \*\*Headquarters venue.\*\* Filing in the collector's home jurisdiction rather than the consumer's residence or contract-signing district.  
\- \*\*"Forum-shopping" within a state.\*\* Filing in a county known for quick default judgments rather than the consumer's actual county of residence.  
\- \*\*Original-creditor venue.\*\* Filing in the venue where the original creditor is headquartered, despite the consumer never having signed or resided there.  
\- \*\*Outdated address.\*\* Filing based on the consumer's old address despite knowledge of the consumer's current residence.  
  
\#\#\# 3.6 Vacation, transfer, and consequences of improper venue  
  
§ 1692i does \*\*not\*\* itself transfer or vacate an improperly venued action. Three avenues are available:  
  
\- \*\*State motion to transfer.\*\* Under state procedural rules, the consumer may move to transfer to the proper county or to dismiss for improper venue.  
\- \*\*Federal removal + § 1404 / § 1406.\*\* If the action is removed, the federal court may transfer under 28 U.S.C. § 1404 (convenience) or § 1406 (improper venue).  
\- \*\*Separate FDCPA suit.\*\* The consumer may file a separate FDCPA action under § 1692i seeking statutory damages, actual damages, and attorney's fees for the venue violation.  
  
The underlying state-court suit may still proceed (in the original or transferred venue), but the collector remains liable under the FDCPA for the venue violation itself.  
  
\#\#\# 3.7 Class actions under § 1692i  
  
Venue violations are a frequent class-action theory because:  
\- The conduct is typically systematic (the collector's case-filing policy applies uniformly).  
\- Class-wide proof is straightforward (a database of filings can be cross-referenced against consumer addresses).  
\- Damages are statutorily defined under § 1692k(a)(2)(B) (the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of the collector's net worth for class statutory damages).  
  
Lead cases:  
\- \*Hess v. Cohen & Slamowitz LLP\*, 637 F.3d 117 (2d Cir. 2011): class certified on § 1692i venue theory.  
\- \*Jackson v. NCO Fin. Sys., Inc.\*, No. 09-cv-202 (E.D. Wash. 2009): certification approved where collector systematically filed in wrong county.  
  
Certification battles often turn on individual-issue defenses (e.g., the consumer "consented" to a forum, the consumer actually resided in the chosen district at filing). Predominant-issue analysis under FRCP 23(b)(3) generally favors certification because the collector's filing decisions are policy-driven.  
  
\#\#\# 3.8 Practical notes  
  
\- \*\*Pleading.\*\* Identify (i) the consumer's residence at suit; (ii) the venue where the consumer signed the contract (with documentary support); (iii) the venue chosen by the collector; and (iv) the geographic distance, with maps if appropriate.  
\- \*\*Discovery.\*\* Demand the collector's case-filing protocols, address-verification procedures, and rosters of filings against consumers in the relevant district. Subpoena state-court filing records for comparable consumers.  
\- \*\*Defense.\*\* Bona fide error under § 1692k(c) is narrow — the collector must show that the venue choice resulted from a clerical error (not a policy decision) and that reasonable procedures existed to avoid it. \*Jerman\* forecloses a "mistake of law" defense.

\#\# 4. § 1692j — Furnishing certain deceptive forms (the "flat-rating" prohibition)  
  
\#\#\# 4.1 The rule  
  
\> "(a) It is unlawful to design, compile, and furnish any form knowing that such form would be used to create the false belief in a consumer that a person other than the creditor of such consumer is participating in the collection of or in an attempt to collect a debt such consumer allegedly owes such creditor, when in fact such person is not so participating.  
\> (b) Any person who violates this section shall be liable to the same extent and in the same manner as a debt collector is liable under section 1692k of this title for failure to comply with a provision of this subchapter."  
\> — 15 U.S.C. § 1692j  
  
\#\#\# 4.2 The flat-rating practice  
  
"Flat-rating" is the historical practice by which a third party (typically a printing service or template provider) sells form letters to a creditor at a flat per-piece rate. The creditor mails the letters under the third-party's letterhead, creating the false impression to the consumer that the creditor has placed the debt with an independent collector. The third party performs no actual collection work — it merely lets its name be used.  
  
§ 1692j prohibits the design, compilation, \*\*and\*\* furnishing of such forms when the supplier "knows" they will be used to create the false impression. The provision applies even when the supplier is not itself a "debt collector" under § 1692a(6); it expands FDCPA liability to template providers, software vendors, and demand-letter services.  
  
\#\#\# 4.3 Elements  
  
A § 1692j claim requires:  
  
\- \*\*Design / compilation / furnishing of a form\*\* by the defendant.  
\- \*\*Knowledge\*\* that the form will be used in the prohibited manner. The knowledge requirement makes § 1692j one of the few FDCPA provisions with an express scienter element. The scienter is satisfied by actual knowledge or willful blindness; ordinary negligence is insufficient.  
\- \*\*Use to create false belief of participation.\*\* The form must be of a type that, if used, would cause the consumer to believe an entity other than the creditor is participating in the collection.  
\- \*\*Non-participation in fact.\*\* The third party whose name appears on the form must not in fact be participating in collection.  
  
\#\#\# 4.4 Examples of violations  
  
\- A printing service that prepares form demand letters on its own letterhead, sells them to creditors, and has no actual involvement in collection.  
\- A "collection software" vendor that drafts demand templates carrying its company name without doing any actual collection work.  
\- A creditor placing "copy to: ABC Collection Agency" on its in-house demand letter when ABC has no involvement.  
  
\#\#\# 4.5 Penalties and liability  
  
\- \*\*Civil liability under § 1692k.\*\* Statutory damages up to $1,000 per individual action; actual damages; class action statutory damages (lesser of $500,000 or 1% of net worth); costs and attorney's fees.  
\- \*\*No criminal penalty under the FDCPA.\*\* The FDCPA does not include a criminal-penalty provision; the title "Furnishing certain deceptive forms" sometimes leads to confusion, but § 1692j operates exclusively through civil remedies.  
\- \*\*FTC Act enforcement.\*\* Under 15 U.S.C. § 1692l, FDCPA violations are also "unfair or deceptive acts or practices" under § 5 of the FTC Act. The FTC and CFPB may seek administrative penalties, injunctive relief, and consumer redress; civil money penalties under § 5(m) of the FTC Act and 12 U.S.C. § 5565 can be substantial.  
  
\#\#\# 4.6 Reg F overlay  
  
Reg F does not expressly re-codify § 1692j, but the CFPB has authority to enforce § 1692j directly under 12 U.S.C. § 5481(12)(F) and § 5536(a)(1)(A). Reg F § 1006.18(d) on misrepresentation of identity and the chapeau prohibitions on false or misleading representations effectively reinforce § 1692j's substantive ban.  
  
\#\#\# 4.7 Practical notes  
  
\- \*\*Pleading.\*\* Identify the supplier defendant separately from the creditor or in-house collection unit. Allege the supplier's knowledge that the form would be used to create the false collector-participation impression. Identify specific letters and the third-party name appearing on them.  
\- \*\*Discovery.\*\* Subpoena the supplier's contracts, marketing materials, customer lists, and design files. The supplier's marketing of the service as "a collection-agency presence without the cost" is often dispositive of the knowledge element.  
\- \*\*Defense.\*\* The supplier may argue actual participation (e.g., the supplier processes returned mail, takes phone calls, or otherwise performs collection functions). The participation must be real and material, not nominal.  
  
\#\#\# 4.8 Modern variants  
  
\- \*\*Letter-mill litigation services.\*\* Mass-produced demand letters bearing an attorney's name without meaningful attorney involvement implicate both § 1692e(3) and § 1692j when the attorney's "name" is being furnished by a service to creditors who are not actually retaining counsel.  
\- \*\*White-label collection software.\*\* SaaS platforms that allow creditors to send demands under a vendor-provided "collector" identity may violate § 1692j depending on whether the vendor performs any collection function.
