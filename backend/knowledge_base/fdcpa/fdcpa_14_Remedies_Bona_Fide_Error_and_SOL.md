\# FDCPA Corpus File 14 — Remedies, Bona Fide Error Defense, and Statute of Limitations  
  
\*\*Topic:\*\* § 1692k civil liability, the § 1692k(c) bona fide error defense, the § 1692k(d) one-year statute of limitations, and post-\*TransUnion\* Article III standing  
\*\*Primary Statute:\*\* 15 U.S.C. § 1692k  
\*\*Reg F Cross-References:\*\* 12 C.F.R. § 1006.4 (reserved); private right of action arises under the statute, not the regulation  
\*\*Last Updated:\*\* May 16, 2026  
  
\---  
  
\#\# Overview  
  
Section 1692k is the heart of the FDCPA's private-enforcement regime. It provides actual damages, statutory damages, attorney's fees, and class-action remedies for any "violation" of the Act, subject only to the bona fide error defense and a one-year statute of limitations. Two Supreme Court decisions — \*Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA\*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010), and \*Rotkiske v. Klemm\*, 589 U.S. 8 (2019) — set the modern contours of the defense and the limitations period. A third case, \*Marx v. General Revenue Corp.\*, 568 U.S. 371 (2013), governs cost-shifting to prevailing defendants. Standing doctrine after \*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez\*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), now functions as a fourth gate to FDCPA recovery, requiring concrete injury beyond a bare procedural violation.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# § 1692k(a) Damages  
  
\#\#\# (1) Actual Damages — § 1692k(a)(1)  
Actual damages are recoverable for "any actual damage sustained by such person as a result of \[the\] failure."  
  
\- \*\*Emotional distress is recoverable.\*\* All circuits to address the issue agree. See, e.g., \*Donohue v. Quick Collect, Inc.\*, 592 F.3d 1027 (9th Cir. 2010); \*Panahiasl v. Gurney\*, 2007 WL 738642 (N.D. Cal. 2007); \*Smith v. Law Offices of Mitchell N. Kay\*, 124 F.3d 1041 (9th Cir. 1997).  
\- \*\*Out-of-pocket loss is recoverable.\*\* Postage, phone calls, lost wages from time spent disputing, and similar pecuniary harms qualify.  
\- \*\*Causation is required.\*\* Plaintiff must show the FDCPA violation (not the underlying debt) caused the harm.  
\- \*\*Proof standards vary.\*\* Some courts (e.g., 5th Cir., 11th Cir.) require corroborating evidence beyond plaintiff testimony for emotional distress; others (e.g., 9th Cir.) accept testimony alone if specific and credible.  
  
\#\#\# (2)(A) Statutory Damages (Individual Actions) — § 1692k(a)(2)(A)  
"Such additional damages as the court may allow, but not exceeding $1,000."  
  
\- \*\*$1,000 cap is per action, not per violation.\*\* This is the uniform circuit reading. See \*Wright v. Finance Service of Norwalk, Inc.\*, 22 F.3d 647 (6th Cir. 1994) (en banc); \*Goodmann v. People's Bank\*, 209 F. App'x 111 (3d Cir. 2006); \*Harper v. Better Bus. Servs., Inc.\*, 961 F.2d 1561 (11th Cir. 1992). Even multiple violations in multiple letters generally yield a single $1,000 cap per defendant per action.  
\- \*\*Per-defendant.\*\* Where multiple debt collectors violate the FDCPA, the cap applies separately to each. \*Clark v. Capital Credit & Collection Servs., Inc.\*, 460 F.3d 1162 (9th Cir. 2006).  
\- \*\*No proof of actual damages required.\*\* Statutory damages are available even where the plaintiff suffered no actual loss (subject to Article III standing).  
  
\#\#\# (2)(B) Statutory Damages (Class Actions) — § 1692k(a)(2)(B)  
"In the case of a class action — (i) such amount for each named plaintiff as could be recovered under subparagraph (A) \[up to $1,000 each\]; and (ii) such amount as the court may allow for all other class members, without regard to a minimum individual recovery, not to exceed the lesser of $500,000 or 1 per centum of the net worth of the debt collector."  
  
\- \*\*Net worth of the collector is the limiting factor for most defendants.\*\* Smaller collectors face caps of a few thousand dollars; for large debt buyers (e.g., Encore, PRA), 1% of net worth can be tens of millions.  
\- \*\*Per case, not per claim.\*\* A defendant in a single class action faces a single $500,000 / 1% cap.  
\- \*\*Net-worth proof and challenges.\*\* Defendants frequently challenge "net worth" methodology; majority rule is GAAP balance-sheet equity (\*Sanders v. Jackson\*, 209 F.3d 998 (7th Cir. 2000)), with some circuits using "fair market value" or alternative formulations.  
\- \*\*No named-plaintiff aggregation.\*\* Each named plaintiff may individually recover up to $1,000; the class-as-a-whole cap is separate.  
  
\#\#\# (3) Attorney's Fees and Costs — § 1692k(a)(3)  
"In the case of any successful action to enforce the foregoing liability, the costs of the action, together with a reasonable attorney's fee as determined by the court."  
  
\- \*\*Mandatory fee-shift to prevailing consumer.\*\* A consumer who establishes any FDCPA violation (and Article III standing) is entitled to fees. \*Carroll v. Wolpoff & Abramson\*, 53 F.3d 626 (4th Cir. 1995).  
\- \*\*Lodestar method governs.\*\* Hours reasonably expended x reasonable hourly rate, subject to the \*Hensley v. Eckerhart\* twelve-factor adjustments.  
\- \*\*De minimis recovery does not preclude fees.\*\* Even a $1 statutory damages award triggers § 1692k(a)(3). But see \*Farrar v. Hobby\*, 506 U.S. 103 (1992) (nominal damages may justify zero fees in extreme cases under § 1988); courts split on whether \*Farrar\* applies to § 1692k.  
\- \*\*Bad-faith defendant fee-shift.\*\* Section 1692k(a)(3) also allows the court to award attorney's fees to a prevailing \*\*defendant\*\* "upon a finding that the action was brought in bad faith and for the purpose of harassment." This is rarely awarded; the standard is demanding.  
  
\---  
  
\#\# § 1692k(b) Factors for Statutory Damages  
  
When determining the amount of statutory damages within the $1,000 cap (or the class-action cap), courts must consider:  
1\. The frequency and persistence of noncompliance;  
2\. The nature of the noncompliance;  
3\. The extent to which the noncompliance was intentional; and  
4\. (In class actions) the resources of the defendant, the number of persons adversely affected, and the extent to which the defendant's noncompliance was intentional.  
  
Courts also consider whether the conduct was technical vs. egregious, whether it caused harm, and the defendant's compliance history. See \*Sanchez v. Client Services, Inc.\*, 520 F. Supp. 2d 1149 (N.D. Cal. 2007); \*Crossley v. Lieberman\*, 868 F.2d 566 (3d Cir. 1989).

\#\# § 1692k(c) Bona Fide Error Defense  
  
\*\*Statutory text:\*\* "A debt collector may not be held liable in any action brought under this subchapter if the debt collector shows by a preponderance of evidence that the violation was not intentional and resulted from a bona fide error notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error."  
  
\#\#\# Three Elements  
The collector bears the burden, by a preponderance of the evidence, on each element:  
1\. \*\*The violation was not intentional;\*\*  
2\. \*\*The violation resulted from a bona fide error;\*\* AND  
3\. \*\*The collector maintained procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error.\*\*  
  
All three are required. Failure of any element defeats the defense.  
  
\#\#\# \*Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA\*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010)  
\- \*\*Holding:\*\* The bona fide error defense in § 1692k(c) does NOT apply to a violation resulting from a debt collector's mistaken interpretation of the legal requirements of the FDCPA.  
\- \*\*Facts:\*\* A debt-collection law firm sent a foreclosure complaint stating the debt would be assumed valid unless disputed "in writing." Sixth Circuit precedent at the time was unclear on whether § 1692g required disputes to be in writing. The district court held that the "in writing" requirement violated § 1692g. The law firm invoked bona fide error.  
\- \*\*Rationale:\*\* The Supreme Court emphasized the common-law principle that "ignorance of the law is no defense," and noted that other consumer-protection statutes (e.g., TILA) explicitly extend their bona fide error defenses to legal errors, while § 1692k(c) does not.  
\- \*\*Significance:\*\*  
 - Legal-mistake errors are not protected.  
 - Reliance on prior circuit precedent is not protected.  
 - Reliance on advice of counsel is not protected.  
 - The defense covers clerical, procedural, and computational errors — e.g., wrong amount due, wrong consumer, wrong time of day for a call.  
\- \*\*Quotation:\*\* "We conclude that the bona fide error defense in § 1692k(c) does not apply to a violation resulting from a debt collector's incorrect interpretation of the requirements of \[the FDCPA\]."  
  
\#\#\# "Procedures Reasonably Adapted" Element  
Even where the error is factual/clerical, the defense fails without proof of preventive procedures.  
  
\- Procedures must be "mechanical or other such 'regular orderly' steps" designed to avoid the specific category of error. \*Johnson v. Riddle\*, 305 F.3d 1107 (10th Cir. 2002).  
\- Conclusory testimony that procedures "exist" is insufficient. \*Reichert v. Nat'l Credit Sys., Inc.\*, 531 F.3d 1002 (9th Cir. 2008).  
\- Procedures must be reasonably adapted to avoid "any such error" — i.e., the type of error that actually occurred. \*Edwards v. Niagara Credit Solutions, Inc.\*, 584 F.3d 1350 (11th Cir. 2009).  
\- Industry-standard practices are not automatically reasonable; the collector must show actual implementation and monitoring. \*Owen v. I.C. System\*, 629 F.3d 1263 (11th Cir. 2011).  
  
\#\#\# Common Successful Invocations  
\- Software-glitch overcharge corrected on detection.  
\- Misdirected mail due to address-database error.  
\- Erroneous call to a number consumer had previously identified as inconvenient, where collector had recorded but failed to flag the restriction (sometimes successful, sometimes not).  
  
\#\#\# Common Failed Invocations  
\- Reliance on creditor-provided account information without independent verification (split among circuits; many courts hold the collector is the responsible party).  
\- Misinterpretation of FDCPA or Reg F (per \*Jerman\*).  
\- Reliance on opinion of counsel (per \*Jerman\*).  
\- Generic compliance program lacking targeted procedures.

\#\# § 1692k(d) Statute of Limitations  
  
\*\*Statutory text:\*\* "An action to enforce any liability created by this subchapter may be brought in any appropriate United States district court without regard to the amount in controversy, or in any other court of competent jurisdiction, within one year from the date on which the violation occurs."  
  
\#\#\# \*Rotkiske v. Klemm\*, 589 U.S. 8 (2019)  
\- \*\*Holding:\*\* Absent the application of an equitable doctrine, the statute of limitations in § 1692k(d) begins to run on the date on which the alleged FDCPA violation occurs, not the date on which the violation is discovered.  
\- \*\*Facts:\*\* A debt-collection law firm sued Rotkiske in state court without effective service (the summons was delivered to a former address). Rotkiske learned of the default judgment years later. He filed a federal FDCPA action more than a year after the state suit. The Third Circuit affirmed dismissal, rejecting a "discovery rule."  
\- \*\*Rationale:\*\* The plain text — "within one year from the date on which the violation occurs" — unambiguously fixes accrual to the violation date, not the discovery date. The Court reaffirmed that "atextual judicial supplementation" of limitations periods is disfavored.  
\- \*\*Equitable tolling possible.\*\* The Court expressly preserved the possibility of equitable tolling for fraudulent concealment or other recognized grounds, but Rotkiske had forfeited the issue.  
  
\#\#\# Operational Consequences  
\- \*\*Each violation has its own limitations clock.\*\* A new violation (e.g., a new letter, a new call) starts a new one-year window for THAT violation. See \*Solomon v. HSBC Mortgage Corp.\*, 395 F. App'x 494 (10th Cir. 2010).  
\- \*\*Continuing violations doctrine generally unavailable.\*\* Most circuits reject a true "continuing violation" theory for FDCPA, though a pattern of new violations within the year is actionable. See \*Purnell v. Arrow Fin. Servs.\*, 303 F. App'x 297 (6th Cir. 2008).  
\- \*\*Equitable tolling requires affirmative concealment.\*\* Standard tolling principles (e.g., \*Holmberg v. Armbrecht\*, 327 U.S. 392 (1946); \*Lozano v. Montoya Alvarez\*, 572 U.S. 1 (2014)) apply where the defendant fraudulently concealed the violation.  
\- \*\*Filing the wrong court does not toll.\*\* Filing in state court does not toll the federal limitations period. \*Burnett v. N.Y. Cent. R.R.\*, 380 U.S. 424 (1965), distinguishes; FDCPA permits filing "in any appropriate" court, so state-court filing satisfies § 1692k(d) if timely.

\#\# \*Marx v. General Revenue Corp.\*, 568 U.S. 371 (2013) — Costs to Prevailing Defendants  
  
\*\*Holding:\*\* Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(d)(1) provides district-court discretion to award costs to prevailing defendants in FDCPA actions without requiring a showing of "bad faith and for the purpose of harassment." Section 1692k(a)(3)'s reference to bad-faith fee-and-cost awards does NOT displace Rule 54(d).  
  
\*\*Significance:\*\*  
\- A prevailing defendant in an FDCPA action may obtain ordinary Rule 54(d) costs (filing fees, transcripts, printing) even without bad faith.  
\- Bad faith remains required for \*\*attorney's fees\*\* to a prevailing defendant.  
\- The decision modestly raises the cost of unsuccessful FDCPA litigation for consumers and is sometimes invoked in offer-of-judgment strategies.

\#\# Article III Standing After \*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez\*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021)  
  
\#\#\# The Standing Revolution  
\*TransUnion\* held: "Only those plaintiffs who have been concretely harmed by a defendant's statutory violation may sue that private defendant over the violation in federal court." A statutory violation alone is insufficient; the risk of future harm, standing alone, is insufficient.  
  
While \*TransUnion\* arose under the FCRA, its reasoning controls FDCPA standing. Lower courts have applied it to dismiss FDCPA cases lacking concrete harm.  
  
\#\#\# Application to FDCPA Claims  
  
\*\*Concrete harm typically established by:\*\*  
\- Actual payment of unauthorized amounts (e.g., pay-to-pay fees).  
\- Confusion or hesitation that caused the consumer to act on incorrect information.  
\- Emotional distress traceable to the violation (subject to circuit-specific proof standards).  
\- Time spent disputing or investigating the debt because of the violation.  
\- Decision-making impacts (e.g., consumer paid a debt she believed was time-barred because the validation notice was misleading).  
\- Reputational harm from third-party disclosure.  
\- Loss of statutory rights akin to common-law analog (e.g., right to be free from intrusion; right to be free from false statements).  
  
\*\*Concrete harm typically NOT established by:\*\*  
\- Bare receipt of a technically deficient letter.  
\- "Confusion" without any consequent action.  
\- Generalized stress at the existence of a debt.  
\- Letters opened but disregarded.  
  
\#\#\# Key Post-\*TransUnion\* FDCPA Standing Cases  
  
\*\*(a) \*Trichell v. Midland Credit Management, Inc.\*, 964 F.3d 990 (11th Cir. 2020)\*\* (decided pre-\*TransUnion\* but consistent with it). Plaintiffs alleged collection letters misleadingly suggested they could be sued on time-barred debt and that the debt could be reported to credit-rating agencies. The Eleventh Circuit found no concrete harm: plaintiffs received misleading letters but did not pay, did not act on them, and were not misled (they consulted counsel). Standing denied.  
  
\*\*(b) \*Maddox v. Bank of N.Y. Mellon Trust Co.\*, 19 F.4th 58 (2d Cir. 2021)\*\* — Although the original panel found standing based on state-statute violation, after \*TransUnion\* the Second Circuit reconsidered and held that the failure to record a mortgage satisfaction within statutory time did not, without more, confer Article III standing absent concrete consequences (denial of credit, transactional harm).  
  
\*\*(c) \*Pierre v. Midland Credit Management, Inc.\*, 29 F.4th 934 (7th Cir. 2022)\*\* — Misleading dunning letter alleging time-barred debt; plaintiff did not pay, was not deceived, did not take any action. Seventh Circuit found no Article III standing; bare confusion is not concrete harm.  
  
\*\*(d) \*Mack v. Resurgent Capital Services\*, 70 F.4th 395 (7th Cir. 2023)\*\* — Confusion-plus-detrimental-action could confer standing; not where consumer simply consulted lawyer and filed suit.  
  
\*\*(e) \*Markakos v. Medicredit, Inc.\*, 997 F.3d 778 (7th Cir. 2021)\*\* — Plaintiff received letter with allegedly inflated amount; she paid nothing and disputed the debt. No standing because no detrimental reliance.  
  
\*\*(f) \*Spuhler v. State Collection Service\*, 983 F.3d 282 (7th Cir. 2020)\*\* — Misleading statement about interest accrual; no concrete harm without detrimental reliance.  
  
\*\*(g) \*Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Management Services\* (11th Cir. en banc 2022)\*\* — Vacated panel decision; en banc court held plaintiff lacked concrete harm from collector's transmission of debt information to a third-party mail vendor. Decision narrowed third-party-disclosure standing significantly.  
  
\#\#\# FDCPA-Specific Concrete-Harm Types  
Working framework distilled from post-\*TransUnion\* circuit decisions:  
  
| Harm Type | Likely Concrete? | Authority |  
|---|---|---|  
| Payment of unauthorized fee | Yes | \*Wadsworth v. Kross\*, 12 F.4th 665 (7th Cir. 2021) |  
| Actual confusion + detrimental action | Yes | \*Mack\*, \*Markakos\* dicta |  
| Decision-making impact (paid time-barred debt) | Likely yes | \*Trichell\* dicta |  
| Anxiety / emotional distress with corroboration | Yes | \*Donohue\*, post-\*TransUnion\* district cases |  
| Time spent disputing | Maybe | Split |  
| Reputational harm (third-party disclosure) | Yes if disclosed to a real third party | \*Hunstein\* (en banc, narrowed) |  
| Bare receipt of misleading letter | No | \*Pierre\*, \*Trichell\* |  
| Bare procedural violation | No | \*TransUnion\* |  
| Mere "risk of future harm" | No | \*TransUnion\* |

\#\# Class Action FDCPA Mechanics  
  
\#\#\# Class Certification under Rule 23  
FDCPA class actions are common because:  
\- Violations are often uniform (form letters; standard scripts).  
\- Statutory damages permit class-wide recovery without individualized damages proof.  
\- The 1% net-worth cap creates a known maximum exposure for the defendant.  
  
Common certification issues:  
\- \*\*Commonality / Typicality:\*\* Easily satisfied for form-letter / form-call cases.  
\- \*\*Predominance (Rule 23(b)(3)):\*\* Generally satisfied; damages cap eliminates the principal individualized issue.  
\- \*\*Superiority:\*\* Often satisfied due to the small per-consumer recovery; offer-of-judgment defenses to named plaintiffs (e.g., \*Campbell-Ewald v. Gomez\*, 577 U.S. 153 (2016)) do not moot class claims.  
\- \*\*Standing for absent class members:\*\* Post-\*TransUnion\*, every class member must have Article III standing at the judgment stage; class definitions must therefore include only injured members or include a procedure to exclude uninjured members. See \*In re Deepwater Horizon\*, 739 F.3d 790 (5th Cir. 2014); \*Cordoba v. DIRECTV, LLC\*, 942 F.3d 1259 (11th Cir. 2019).  
  
\#\#\# Net-Worth Discovery  
Defendants resist net-worth discovery; the majority view permits early production where the 1% cap matters for case management. See \*Sanders v. Jackson\*, 209 F.3d 998 (7th Cir. 2000); \*Hutton v. C.B. Accounts, Inc.\*, 2010 WL 3021904 (C.D. Ill. 2010).  
  
\#\#\# Cy Pres  
FDCPA class settlements sometimes distribute unclaimed funds via cy pres to consumer-protection organizations. Courts apply \*In re Baby Products Antitrust Litig.\*, 708 F.3d 163 (3d Cir. 2013) and the 2018 Federal Rule 23(e) amendments.

\#\# Litigation Notes  
  
1\. \*\*Plead concrete harm with specificity.\*\* Post-\*TransUnion\*, FDCPA complaints should plead specific decisions made, actions taken, or financial losses incurred — not merely "confusion" or "concern."  
2\. \*\*Calendar the limitations period from each discrete violation.\*\* Each letter, call, or other violation has its own one-year clock.  
3\. \*\*Anticipate bona fide error.\*\* Discovery should target the existence (or non-existence) of "procedures reasonably adapted" to avoid the specific error. Depose compliance and operations personnel; obtain training materials and policy manuals.  
4\. \*\*Be cautious of fee-defendant exposure under \*Marx\*.\*\* Cost-shifting to prevailing defendants is available without bad faith; budget for adverse costs in close cases.  
5\. \*\*Watch the per-action / per-violation cap.\*\* Statutory damages are capped per action per defendant, not multiplied by each violation. This affects settlement valuation.
