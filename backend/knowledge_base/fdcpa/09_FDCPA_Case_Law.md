# FDCPA Key Case Law

**Topic:** Leading Supreme Court decisions, Eleventh Circuit authority, and foundational appellate cases interpreting the FDCPA  
**Primary statute:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692-1692p (Fair Debt Collection Practices Act)  
**Cross-references:** All substantive FDCPA knowledge base files; Regulation F (12 C.F.R. Part 1006)  
**Last updated:** May 17, 2026  

---

## Introduction

This file compiles the key judicial decisions interpreting the FDCPA, organized by court level and topic. These cases establish the legal standards applied in FDCPA litigation — the definition of "debt collector," the scope of the bona fide error defense, the least sophisticated consumer standard, the application of the statute to attorneys, and standing requirements. Understanding this case law is essential for evaluating claims, anticipating defenses, and crafting effective arguments.

---

## United States Supreme Court Decisions

### Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291 (1995)

**Issue:** Whether the FDCPA applies to attorneys who regularly engage in debt-collection activity, including litigation.

**Holding:** Yes. The FDCPA applies to attorneys who "regularly" engage in consumer-debt-collection activity, including litigation on behalf of creditors. The statutory definition of "debt collector" includes "any person... who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another," and this definition contains no exception for attorneys or for litigation activity.

**Key reasoning:**
- The 1986 amendment to the FDCPA removed the original exemption for attorneys — Congress specifically chose to bring attorneys within the statute's scope
- The narrow § 1692e(11) exception for "formal pleadings" confirms that Congress intended other FDCPA provisions to apply to litigation
- The word "regularly" requires more than isolated debt-collection activity — but attorneys whose practice includes a regular component of debt collection on behalf of creditors are covered

**Practical significance:**
- Collection law firms are subject to all FDCPA provisions (except the formal-pleading exception for mini-Miranda)
- Complaints, motions, affidavits, discovery requests, and settlement communications are all potentially actionable
- Litigation threats, filing decisions, and venue choices must comply with the FDCPA
- Law firms must maintain the same compliance procedures as non-attorney collection agencies

**Litigation tip:** In cases against law firms, use *Heintz* to defeat any argument that the FDCPA does not apply to their litigation conduct. Establish in discovery that the firm "regularly" collects debts (volume of collection cases filed, percentage of practice devoted to collections, number of collection clients).

### Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich, 559 U.S. 573 (2010)

**Issue:** Whether the bona fide error defense under § 1692k(c) applies to violations resulting from a debt collector's mistaken interpretation of the law.

**Holding:** No. The bona fide error defense is limited to errors of fact and procedure — it does not extend to mistakes of legal judgment or statutory interpretation. A debt collector that violates the FDCPA based on an incorrect reading of the statute cannot invoke the bona fide error defense, even if the legal interpretation was reasonable or made in reliance on counsel's advice.

**Facts:** A law firm's validation notice required consumers to dispute debts "in writing" — a requirement found in § 1692g(a)(4) for triggering the verification obligation, but arguably not required for a basic dispute under § 1692g(a)(3). The firm argued its interpretation was a bona fide error.

**Key reasoning:**
- The word "error" in the statute — combined with the requirement of "procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error" — refers to factual/mechanical mistakes (miscalculations, clerical errors, mail-processing mistakes)
- There are no "procedures" that can be adopted to avoid a legal misinterpretation — that is a matter of legal judgment, not procedural compliance
- Extending the defense to legal errors would effectively eliminate strict liability and transform every FDCPA case into a reasonableness inquiry
- The legislative history shows Congress intended the defense for errors like "a debt collector's reliance on erroneous computer printouts" — not for errors in legal interpretation
- The common-law distinction between mistakes of fact and mistakes of law supports limiting the defense to factual errors

**Practical significance:**
- Eliminates "we thought it was legal" as a defense
- Eliminates "our attorney advised us this was compliant" as a defense
- Eliminates "the law was unclear and we adopted a reasonable interpretation" as a defense
- The only surviving bona fide error claims involve genuinely accidental factual mistakes: data entry errors, computer glitches, mailing mistakes, mathematical miscalculations
- Makes the FDCPA effectively a strict liability statute for all policy-driven conduct

**Litigation tip:** Whenever a collector's defense amounts to "we interpreted the statute differently" or "we relied on our compliance counsel's advice," cite *Jerman*. The only question is whether the violation occurred — not whether the collector thought it was legal.

### Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 582 U.S. 79 (2017)

**Issue:** Whether an entity that purchases defaulted debt and collects it for its own account qualifies as a "debt collector" under § 1692a(6).

**Holding:** No. A debt buyer that acquires defaulted debts and collects them for its own account is not a "debt collector" under the statutory definition requiring that the person collect debts "owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." Once the debt buyer owns the debt, it is collecting for itself, not for "another."

**Key reasoning:**
- The statutory text focuses on the relationship between the collector and the creditor — "owed... another" means the person collecting must be doing so on behalf of a different entity
- A debt buyer that owns the debt is collecting for itself, even though the debt was originated by a different entity
- Congress could have defined "debt collector" to include anyone collecting defaulted debts, but chose instead to focus on the third-party collection relationship
- The decision is based on statutory text, not policy considerations

**Important limitations:**
- The decision does NOT exempt all debt buyers — only those collecting solely for their own account
- A debt buyer that ALSO collects debts for other entities (e.g., services multiple creditors) qualifies as a "debt collector" under the "regularly collects" prong
- A debt buyer using a false name suggesting a third party (§ 1692a(6)) is still covered
- Regulation F (effective November 30, 2021) defines "debt collector" to include entities that acquired debt in default — partially restoring coverage for debt buyers under the regulatory framework
- State debt collection statutes often cover debt buyers explicitly regardless of *Henson*

**Practical significance for consumer attorneys:**
1. If the defendant is a debt buyer collecting its own purchased debt exclusively, the FDCPA may not apply
2. Investigate whether the debt buyer also services accounts for other entities
3. Plead Regulation F violations for post-November 2021 conduct
4. Assert state statutory claims that are not limited by the *Henson* definition
5. Check whether the debt buyer uses a DBA or trade name that triggers the false-name provision

### Obduskey v. McCarthy & Holthus LLP, 586 U.S. 466 (2019)

**Issue:** Whether a law firm engaged in nonjudicial foreclosure proceedings is a "debt collector" subject to the full FDCPA.

**Holding:** A business engaged in nonjudicial foreclosure proceedings is generally NOT a "debt collector" subject to the FDCPA's general provisions, BUT it IS subject to § 1692f(6) (which specifically addresses nonjudicial action to dispossess property). The Court held that the "enforcement of security interests" language in § 1692a(6) creates a limited category of entities subject to only § 1692f(6) rather than the full statute.

**Key reasoning:**
- Section 1692a(6) defines "debt collector" to include "any person... in any business the principal purpose of which is the enforcement of security interests"
- This provision is limited in its reference — the full paragraph states the term applies to such persons "for the purpose of section 1692f(6)"
- This means nonjudicial foreclosure trustees/attorneys are debt collectors ONLY for purposes of § 1692f(6), not for the full range of FDCPA protections
- However, if the entity ALSO engages in traditional debt collection activity (sending demand letters, making collection calls on the underlying note), it may be a full-scope debt collector

**Practical significance:**
- Nonjudicial foreclosure firms can still violate § 1692f(6) — threatening nonjudicial action without a valid security interest
- If the firm sends communications demanding payment of the mortgage note (not just executing the foreclosure), those communications may bring the firm under the full FDCPA
- Judicial foreclosure (where the firm files a lawsuit) is different — *Heintz* may apply if the firm regularly collects debts through litigation
- The decision is narrow — it does not exempt all mortgage-related collection activity

### Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330 (2016)

**Issue (relevant to FDCPA):** Whether a plaintiff who alleges a bare procedural violation of a consumer protection statute — without concrete harm — has Article III standing.

**Holding:** Article III standing requires a "concrete" injury, and a bare procedural violation of a statute, without more, does not automatically satisfy this requirement. However, intangible injuries can be "concrete," and Congress's judgment in creating a statutory cause of action is relevant to (but not dispositive of) the concreteness inquiry.

**Application to FDCPA claims:**
- A technical validation notice deficiency does not automatically confer standing — the consumer must show the deficiency caused some concrete harm (confusion, inability to evaluate the debt, payment that would not have been made)
- Harassment claims (§ 1692d) inherently involve concrete harm — unwanted calls, threats, and abuse cause concrete injury by their nature
- False representations that affected the consumer's decision-making involve concrete harm
- Informational injuries (deprivation of legally required information) can be concrete if tied to a downstream harmful effect

**Post-Spokeo developments in the Eleventh Circuit:**
- Courts have found standing where consumers received misleading communications and altered their behavior (paid amounts they otherwise would not have)
- Courts have found standing where harassment caused emotional distress
- Courts have denied standing for purely technical violations with no demonstrated downstream effect

## Eleventh Circuit Key Decisions

### Jeter v. Credit Bureau, Inc., 760 F.2d 1168 (11th Cir. 1985)

**Issue:** Whether adding unauthorized collection fees to the amount demanded violates the FDCPA.

**Holding:** A debt collector's demand for payment of fees not authorized by the underlying debt instrument violates § 1692e(2)(A) (false representation of the amount of the debt) and § 1692f(1) (collecting unauthorized amounts). The court adopted the "least sophisticated consumer" standard for evaluating FDCPA claims in the Eleventh Circuit.

**Key principles established:**
- The "least sophisticated consumer" standard is the appropriate test — the question is whether the communication would mislead an unsophisticated but reasonable consumer
- Adding unauthorized fees to a collection demand is a per se violation — no need to show the consumer was actually misled
- The violation is established by comparing the demanded amount to the contractually authorized amount — any excess is false
- The FDCPA imposes strict liability — the collector's intent is irrelevant

**Litigation significance:** *Jeter* is the foundational Eleventh Circuit FDCPA decision. It establishes both the governing analytical standard (least sophisticated consumer) and the basic principle that demanding more than is owed violates the statute.

### LeBlanc v. Unifin, Inc., 601 F.3d 1185 (11th Cir. 2010)

**Issue:** Whether a debt collector's letter overstating the amount owed — even if due to a computational error — violates § 1692e.

**Holding:** Yes. A demand letter that overstates the amount owed violates § 1692e(2)(A) regardless of whether the overstatement resulted from intentional conduct or inadvertent error. The FDCPA imposes strict liability, and the least sophisticated consumer standard is objective — it does not inquire into the collector's state of mind.

**Key principles:**
- Strict liability applies to § 1692e — no intent required
- An inadvertent computational error resulting in an overstated balance is still a "false representation of the amount of the debt"
- The least sophisticated consumer would be misled by a demand for the wrong amount — this consumer lacks the sophistication to independently verify the balance
- The bona fide error defense (§ 1692k(c)) may apply to accidental errors, but that is an affirmative defense the collector must prove — it does not prevent the prima facie violation from being established
- The consumer need not show actual reliance or actual harm beyond the receipt of the misleading communication (though post-*Spokeo*, some concrete harm is now required for standing)

**Litigation significance:** *LeBlanc* is the go-to Eleventh Circuit case for the proposition that accuracy matters absolutely in collection communications. Even small overstatements are actionable. Combined with *Jerman* (which eliminates the bona fide error defense for legal errors), *LeBlanc* makes clear that collectors bear the risk of any inaccuracy in their demands.

### Owen v. I.C. System, Inc., 629 F.3d 1263 (11th Cir. 2011)

**Issue:** Whether a debt collector's voice message that did not disclose the call was from a debt collector or provide the mini-Miranda violated § 1692e(11).

**Holding:** The court addressed the tension between § 1692e(11) (requiring debt-collection disclosure) and § 1692c(b) (prohibiting disclosure of the debt to third parties). The court held that the FDCPA requires the debt collector to make the § 1692e(11) disclosure when communicating with the consumer, and that leaving a voicemail that third parties might hear does not excuse the disclosure requirement — the collector must find a way to comply with both provisions.

**Key principles:**
- The FDCPA's various provisions must be read harmoniously
- A collector cannot use potential third-party disclosure as an excuse to omit the mini-Miranda
- If the collector cannot leave a compliant voicemail (because of third-party exposure concerns), it should not leave a voicemail at all — or should use the limited-content message framework (now available under Regulation F)
- The mini-Miranda is required in every communication with the consumer (initial: full disclosure; subsequent: "this is from a debt collector")

**Post-Regulation F significance:** Regulation F's "limited-content message" framework (§ 1006.2(j)) now provides a safe harbor for voicemails — a properly formatted limited-content message is not a "communication" and does not trigger the mini-Miranda. This partially resolves the *Owen* tension, but collectors who leave voicemails that exceed the limited-content message parameters must still comply with § 1692e(11).

### Edwards v. Niagara Credit Solutions, Inc., 584 F.3d 1350 (11th Cir. 2009)

**Issue:** Whether a debt collector's letter threatening legal action violates § 1692e(5) when the collector did not intend to file suit.

**Holding:** A debt collector violates § 1692e(5) by threatening to take legal action that it does not actually intend to take. The court held that the threat of suit must be assessed at the time it was made — if the collector had no present intention to file suit when it sent the threatening letter, the threat is false regardless of whether the collector might file suit in the future.

**Key principles:**
- "Intent" is assessed at the time of the communication — not in hindsight
- A form letter threatening legal action, sent to all delinquent accounts regardless of whether suit is being contemplated, violates § 1692e(5)
- The collector need not have affirmatively decided NOT to sue — the absence of a present intention to sue is sufficient
- Statistical evidence is relevant: if a collector threatens suit in thousands of letters but files suit in fewer than 1% of cases, this supports finding no intent

**Litigation significance:** Discovery should focus on (1) how many accounts received the threatening letter, (2) how many of those accounts actually resulted in filed lawsuits, (3) what criteria (if any) the collector applies before sending litigation threats, and (4) whether this specific account had been approved for litigation before the threat was sent.

### Trichell v. Midland Credit Management, Inc., 964 F.3d 990 (11th Cir. 2020)

**Issue:** Whether a consumer has Article III standing based on receipt of a collection letter on a time-barred debt.

**Holding:** The consumer had standing because the collection letter on a time-barred debt created a concrete, if intangible, harm — the risk of unwittingly paying a legally unenforceable obligation. The letter's implicit representation that the debt was legally enforceable (when it was not) was a concrete informational injury sufficient for Article III purposes.

**Key principles (post-*Spokeo*):**
- Receipt of a misleading collection letter can constitute concrete injury for standing purposes
- The harm is the consumer's exposure to a risk of paying a debt they do not legally owe
- This is not a "bare procedural violation" — it is a substantive injury to the consumer's ability to make informed financial decisions
- The Eleventh Circuit takes a relatively generous view of standing in FDCPA cases involving misleading communications

## Other Critical Appellate Decisions

### Clomon v. Jackson, 988 F.2d 1314 (2d Cir. 1993)

**Issue:** Whether mass-produced dunning letters bearing an attorney's signature, when the attorney did not review individual consumer files, violate § 1692e(3).

**Holding:** Yes. A letter that bears an attorney's signature, or purports to be from an attorney, misleads the least sophisticated consumer into believing an attorney has reviewed the file and exercised professional judgment about the matter — when in fact the letter was mass-produced without individual attorney review.

**Significance:** Establishes that attorney-signed form letters require meaningful individual review. "Meaningful involvement" means the attorney must have actually reviewed the specific consumer's account information before the letter is sent. Rubber-stamping, auto-signing, or cursory review of batch lists is insufficient.

---

### Chaudhry v. Gallerizzo, 174 F.3d 394 (4th Cir. 1999)

**Issue:** What constitutes adequate "verification" under § 1692g(a)(4) when a consumer disputes a debt.

**Holding:** Verification under § 1692g requires the debt collector to confirm with the creditor that the amount being collected is what the creditor claims is owed, and to provide sufficient documentation to allow the consumer to "sufficiently dispute the payment obligation." Verification does not require production of the original signed agreement in every case, but it must be more than a mere restatement of the already-demanded amount.

**Significance:** Sets the minimum floor for verification — a collector cannot simply re-send the same information already provided. The verification must include some confirmation from the creditor's records establishing the consumer's obligation and the amount thereof.

---

### Swanson v. Southern Oregon Credit Service, Inc., 869 F.2d 1222 (9th Cir. 1988)

**Issue:** Whether a collection letter that demands payment within ten days "overshadows" the 30-day validation period.

**Holding:** Yes. A demand for payment within a period shorter than the 30-day dispute window overshadows the consumer's dispute rights in violation of § 1692g(b). The least sophisticated consumer, reading a demand for payment within 10 days alongside a 30-day dispute notice, would be confused about whether the right to dispute actually existed.

**Significance:** The foundational overshadowing case. Establishes that any deadline or demand within the 30-day validation period that could create confusion about the consumer's right to dispute violates the FDCPA. Courts since have extended this to settlement offers, "final notice" language, and any artificial urgency within the 30-day window.

---

### Suesz v. Med-1 Solutions, LLC, 757 F.3d 636 (7th Cir. 2014) (en banc)

**Issue:** What does "judicial district or similar legal entity" mean in § 1692i's venue restriction?

**Holding:** "Judicial district or similar legal entity" refers to the smallest geographic area relevant to venue determination — typically a county (or in federal court, a federal judicial district). A collector that files suit in the correct state but the wrong county violates § 1692i.

**Significance:** Prevents debt collectors from filing suit in a convenient (for them) courthouse within the state that is nevertheless burdensome for the consumer to reach. Filing in the correct county — where the consumer signed the contract or where the consumer resides — is required.

---

### Miller v. Payco-General American Credits, Inc., 943 F.2d 482 (4th Cir. 1991)

**Issue:** Whether a collection letter with a bold-print demand for immediate payment and small-print validation notice overshadows the consumer's rights.

**Holding:** Yes. The formatting of the letter — with the payment demand prominently displayed and the validation notice in small, difficult-to-read print — would leave the least sophisticated consumer uncertain about whether they actually had the right to dispute. The form and formatting of the communication, not just the literal text, must be evaluated for overshadowing.

**Significance:** Establishes that visual prominence matters. A validation notice buried in fine print, placed on the back of a page, or rendered in a small font relative to the payment demand can violate § 1692g even if the required text is technically present.

## Least Sophisticated Consumer Standard — Summary of Circuit Approaches

### The Standard

The "least sophisticated consumer" (or "unsophisticated consumer") standard is an objective test used to evaluate whether a communication is false, deceptive, or misleading under § 1692e, or whether a validation notice is overshadowed under § 1692g. The standard asks what the hypothetical least sophisticated consumer would understand — not what the actual plaintiff understood.

### Circuit Adoption

| Circuit | Standard Used | Key Case |
|---------|--------------|----------|
| 2d Circuit | Least sophisticated consumer | *Clomon v. Jackson*, 988 F.2d 1314 (1993) |
| 3d Circuit | Least sophisticated debtor | *Brown v. Card Service Center*, 31 F.3d 138 (1994) |
| 4th Circuit | Least sophisticated consumer | *Miller v. Payco-General*, 943 F.2d 482 (1991) |
| 5th Circuit | Unsophisticated or least sophisticated consumer | *Goswami v. American Collections Enterprise*, 377 F.3d 488 (2004) |
| 7th Circuit | Unsophisticated consumer | *Bartlett v. Heibl*, 128 F.3d 497 (1997) |
| 9th Circuit | Least sophisticated debtor | *Swanson v. Southern Oregon*, 869 F.2d 1222 (1988) |
| 11th Circuit | Least sophisticated consumer | *Jeter v. Credit Bureau*, 760 F.2d 1168 (1985) |

### Characteristics of the Least Sophisticated Consumer

Courts uniformly agree on these characteristics:
- **Uninformed:** Does not have specialized knowledge about debt collection law or practices
- **Naive:** Tends to take statements at face value; does not assume collectors are being deceptive
- **Trusting:** Gives the collector the benefit of the doubt
- **Not irrational:** Possesses a basic, rudimentary knowledge of the world — bizarre or wholly unreasonable interpretations are not protected
- **Willing to read:** Reads communications with some care (not a non-reader or a completely inattentive person)
- **Not a legal expert:** Does not parse language like a lawyer or understand technical legal distinctions

## Time-Barred Debt Collection — Key Decisions

Several circuits have addressed whether collecting on time-barred debts violates the FDCPA:

- *Huertas v. Galaxy Asset Management*, 641 F.3d 28 (3d Cir. 2011) — Filing suit on a time-barred debt violates § 1692e(2)(A) (false legal status) and § 1692f (unfair practice)
- *Kimber v. Federal Financial Corp.*, 668 F. Supp. 1480 (M.D. Ala. 1987) — Threatening suit on a time-barred debt violates § 1692e(5) (threat of action cannot legally be taken)
- *Trichell v. Midland Credit Mgmt.*, 964 F.3d 990 (11th Cir. 2020) — Collection letter on time-barred debt creates concrete injury for standing purposes

**Current consensus:**
- Filing suit on a time-barred debt: violates FDCPA in virtually all circuits
- Threatening suit on a time-barred debt: violates § 1692e(4)/(5)
- Simply requesting payment on a time-barred debt (without threatening suit): circuit split — some hold it violates § 1692e(2)(A) by implying legal enforceability; others hold that mere payment requests are permissible as long as no legal action is threatened
- Regulation F (§ 1006.26(b)) requires specific disclosures when collecting time-barred debt, resolving some of the ambiguity for post-2021 conduct

## Practice Tips for Using Case Law

### Building a Complaint

1. Identify the specific FDCPA provision violated
2. Identify the controlling circuit authority applying that provision
3. Apply the least sophisticated consumer standard (cite circuit's adoption case)
4. Cite *Jerman* preemptively to eliminate the bona fide error defense for policy-based violations
5. Cite *Heintz* if the defendant is a law firm arguing the FDCPA does not apply to litigation

### Opposing Summary Judgment

1. The least sophisticated consumer standard is typically a jury question — argue genuine disputes of material fact about how the communication would be perceived
2. Cite *LeBlanc* for strict liability — intent is irrelevant to the prima facie case
3. Force the defendant to prove the bona fide error defense — it is their burden

### Anticipating Defenses

| Defense | Response Case Law |
|---------|------------------|
| "We're not a debt collector" | *Heintz* (attorneys); check § 1692a(6) definition |
| "Bona fide error" | *Jerman* (no legal errors); demand proof of procedures |
| "Debt buyer — not collecting for another" | *Henson* — but check Reg F and state law |
| "Litigation privilege" | *Heintz* — FDCPA applies to litigation |
| "No concrete injury" | *Trichell* — misleading communications cause concrete harm |
| "Not a 'communication'" | Check § 1692a(2) definition and Reg F limited-content rules |
