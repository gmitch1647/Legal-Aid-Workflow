# FDCPA Pleading Strategy Overview  
  
**Topic:** Strategic considerations for pleading Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) claims  
**FRCP Cross-References:** Rules 8(a), 9(b), 11, 12(b)(6), 23, 41, 68  
**FDCPA Cross-References:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a, 1692c, 1692d, 1692e, 1692f, 1692g, 1692k  
**Regulation F Cross-References:** 12 C.F.R. §§ 1006.6, 1006.14, 1006.18, 1006.26, 1006.34, 1006.38  
**Last Updated:** May 16, 2026  
**Sources:** Public-domain primary materials only (FRCP, FDCPA statute, Regulation F, published federal opinions, CFPB/FTC public guidance)

## 1. Purpose and Scope  
  
This document provides a strategic overview for plaintiff's counsel drafting pleadings under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692 et seq. It is intended as a checklist of major decision points before a complaint is filed — forum, defendant, claim, standing, limitations, damages, and class allegations. Detailed claim-by-claim drafting guidance appears in `fdcpa_plead_02_Individual_Complaint_Drafting.md`, class allegations in `fdcpa_plead_03_Class_Action_Complaint_Drafting.md`, and full sample complaints in `fdcpa_plead_04_FDCPA_Complaint_Templates.md`.

## 2. Forum Selection  
  
### 2.1 Concurrent Jurisdiction  
  
The FDCPA confers concurrent state and federal jurisdiction. 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(d) ("An action to enforce any liability created by this subchapter may be brought in any appropriate United States district court without regard to the amount in controversy, or in any other court of competent jurisdiction . . . ."). Counsel may file in:  
  
- The federal district court where the violation occurred or where the consumer resides;  
- State court of general jurisdiction;  
- State small-claims court (where statutory amounts permit).  
  
### 2.2 Federal Court Considerations  
  
**Advantages of federal forum:**  
- Uniform application of Federal Rules of Civil Procedure;  
- Federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331;  
- Greater familiarity with FDCPA case law in many districts;  
- Class action procedure under Rule 23 is well-developed.  
  
**Disadvantages:**  
- *Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins*, 578 U.S. 330 (2016), and *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), have raised the standing bar in federal court. Bare statutory violations without concrete harm may be dismissed for lack of Article III standing.  
- Heightened scrutiny under *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544 (2007), and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662 (2009).  
- Federal Rule 68 offers of judgment create settlement pressure.  
  
### 2.3 State Court Considerations  
  
Many state courts apply less restrictive standing doctrines than Article III. After *TransUnion*, "no-injury" FDCPA cases that would be dismissed in federal court may survive in state court. See, e.g., *Maddox v. Bank of N.Y. Mellon Tr. Co., N.A.*, 19 F.4th 58 (2d Cir. 2021) (vacating judgment for lack of standing where plaintiff alleged only statutory violation). State court filing also avoids removal risk only where the case is non-removable; pure FDCPA claims are removable as federal-question cases under 28 U.S.C. § 1441.  
  
### 2.4 Class Action Fairness Act (CAFA)  
  
CAFA, 28 U.S.C. § 1332(d), permits removal of class actions with:  
- 100 or more class members;  
- Aggregate amount in controversy exceeding $5,000,000; and  
- Minimal diversity.  
  
FDCPA's 1% net worth cap on classwide statutory damages, 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(2)(B), often defeats CAFA's amount-in-controversy threshold for smaller debt collectors. Counsel pleading a state-court FDCPA class action against a small collector should plead facts demonstrating that the 1% cap will limit damages below $5 million. Conversely, for a large debt buyer, federal class filings are usually unavoidable.

## 3. Defendant Selection  
  
### 3.1 The "Debt Collector" Threshold  
  
The FDCPA applies only to "debt collectors" as defined in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The complaint must plead facts showing the defendant qualifies. Two distinct prongs:  
  
- **Principal-purpose collectors:** Entities whose principal purpose is the collection of debts.  
- **Regularly-collects collectors:** Entities that regularly collect debts owed to another.  
  
### 3.2 Debt Buyers After *Henson*  
  
In *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 582 U.S. 79 (2017), the Supreme Court held that a debt buyer collecting debts it purchased and now owns is not a "debt collector" under the "regularly collects . . . debts owed . . . another" prong. *Henson* did not address the "principal purpose" prong. Counsel suing a debt buyer must plead facts demonstrating that debt collection is the buyer's principal purpose — e.g., that the entity is organized for the purpose of acquiring and collecting on charged-off debt portfolios. See *Barbato v. Greystone Alliance, LLC*, 916 F.3d 260 (3d Cir. 2019); *Tepper v. Amos Fin., LLC*, 898 F.3d 364 (3d Cir. 2018) (entity whose only business is acquiring debts for collection qualifies under principal-purpose prong).  
  
### 3.3 Mortgage Servicers and *Obduskey*  
  
In *Obduskey v. McCarthy & Holthus LLP*, 586 U.S. 466 (2019), the Court held that a business engaged in no more than nonjudicial foreclosure is not a "debt collector" under most FDCPA provisions, except § 1692f(6). Pleading against a foreclosure firm should be limited to § 1692f(6) unless the defendant engaged in conduct beyond mere foreclosure.  
  
### 3.4 Attorneys  
  
*Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291 (1995), confirmed that attorneys who regularly collect consumer debts via litigation are "debt collectors." Counsel suing collection attorneys should plead the firm's volume of collection litigation to satisfy the "regularly" requirement.  
  
### 3.5 Creditor Exclusion  
  
Original creditors collecting their own debts in their own name are not "debt collectors." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The complaint should plead facts negating any false-name exception under § 1692a(6) where relevant.

## 4. Claim Selection  
  
The FDCPA's substantive prohibitions span §§ 1692b through 1692j. Common claim combinations:  
  
- **Harassing-call cases:** §§ 1692d, 1692d(5), 1692c(a)(1) (inconvenient time/place), and Reg F § 1006.14(b) (7-in-7 call frequency presumption).  
- **False letter cases:** §§ 1692e, 1692e(2)(A), 1692e(10), 1692f(1), and sometimes § 1692g.  
- **Time-barred debt cases:** § 1692e (misrepresentation of legal status) under *Phillips v. Asset Acceptance, LLC*, 736 F.3d 1076 (7th Cir. 2013) and *McMahon v. LVNV Funding, LLC*, 744 F.3d 1010 (7th Cir. 2014); Reg F § 1006.26.  
- **Third-party disclosure cases:** § 1692c(b), § 1692b. *Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Mgmt. Servs., Inc.*, 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc) (no Article III standing for letter-vendor disclosure absent concrete harm).  
- **Validation cases:** § 1692g + Reg F § 1006.34 (model validation notice).  
- **Venue cases:** § 1692i (suit must be brought in judicial district where consumer signed contract or where consumer resides).  
  
Plead all viable claims; the FDCPA permits cumulative violations, but statutory damages are capped at $1,000 per case per defendant under § 1692k(a)(2)(A). See `fdcpa_plead_02` for elemental drafting.

## 5. Iqbal/Twombly Applied to FDCPA  
  
Federal Rule 8(a)(2) requires "a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief." *Twombly* and *Iqbal* require facial plausibility — factual content allowing the court to draw a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable.  
  
### 5.1 What to Plead  
  
- **The communication itself:** Quote the letter, transcribe the call, or attach as an exhibit (Fed. R. Civ. P. 10(c)). Courts routinely dismiss FDCPA cases that paraphrase rather than reproduce the alleged violation.  
- **Debt-collector status:** Concrete facts, not labels (e.g., "Defendant is a Delaware LLC whose principal business activity is the acquisition and collection of charged-off consumer receivables").  
- **Consumer status and "debt":** That the obligation arose from a transaction primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(3), (5).  
- **The specific subsection violated:** Identify each § 1692 provision and tie the facts to it.  
  
### 5.2 What Not to Plead  
  
Avoid bare recitations such as "Defendant violated § 1692e." *Iqbal* requires that legal conclusions be supported by factual allegations. Courts dismissing under 12(b)(6) frequently cite the absence of the actual challenged communication. See, e.g., *Foti v. NCO Fin. Sys., Inc.*, 424 F. Supp. 2d 643 (S.D.N.Y. 2006) (rejecting motion to dismiss where complaint quoted the prerecorded voicemail messages at issue).

## 6. Article III Standing Post-*TransUnion*  
  
### 6.1 The Concrete-Harm Requirement  
  
*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), held that absent class members who did not have their inaccurate credit reports disseminated to third parties lacked Article III standing despite a statutory violation. The Court reiterated *Spokeo*'s rule: "Article III standing requires a concrete injury even in the context of a statutory violation."  
  
Key takeaway: "No concrete harm, no standing." *TransUnion*, 594 U.S. at 442.  
  
### 6.2 What Qualifies as Concrete Harm in FDCPA Cases  
  
Recognized concrete harms include:  
- Tangible financial loss (out-of-pocket expenses, paid sums on disputed debts);  
- Lost time (where pleaded with specificity);  
- Emotional distress, anxiety, sleeplessness (must be pleaded with factual support);  
- Reputational injury closely analogous to defamation (false-light, third-party disclosure);  
- Invasion of privacy closely analogous to intrusion upon seclusion (harassing calls).  
  
**Insufficient (post-*TransUnion*):**  
- Mere receipt of a statutorily defective letter without confusion, action, or harm. *Maddox v. Bank of N.Y. Mellon Tr. Co., N.A.*, 19 F.4th 58 (2d Cir. 2021).  
- Informational injury divorced from downstream consequence. *Trichell v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 964 F.3d 990 (11th Cir. 2020).  
- Risk of future harm in damages actions absent materialization. *TransUnion*, 594 U.S. at 435–37.  
  
### 6.3 Pleading Practice After *TransUnion*  
  
Every FDCPA complaint in federal court must include a paragraph block detailing concrete harm. Sample structure:  
  
> *Plaintiff suffered concrete harm as a result of Defendant's conduct, including but not limited to: (a) lost wages and time spent responding to Defendant's communications; (b) anxiety, frustration, and emotional distress; (c) invasion of privacy through repeated unwanted telephone contact analogous to common-law intrusion upon seclusion; and (d) out-of-pocket expenses incurred in retaining counsel to dispute the debt.*  
  
See *Pierre v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 29 F.4th 934 (7th Cir. 2022) (no standing where plaintiff did not pay disputed debt or otherwise act on misleading communication). Contrast cases where plaintiff alleged she paid the debt or took adverse action in reliance.

## 7. Bona Fide Error Defense — Anticipatory Pleading  
  
Section 1692k(c) provides an affirmative defense for unintentional violations resulting from a bona fide error notwithstanding maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid the error. *Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010), held that mistakes of law do not qualify.  
  
Although the defense is affirmative, smart pleading anticipates it:  
- Plead facts suggesting intentional conduct or willful blindness;  
- Plead repeated, systemic conduct that negates "unintentional";  
- Plead absence of procedures (e.g., "Defendant has no written compliance manual" — to be developed in discovery).  
  
See `fdcpa_07_Bona_Fide_Error_Defense.md` in the FDCPA Knowledge Base for full discussion.

## 8. Statute of Limitations — § 1692k(d) and *Rotkiske*  
  
The FDCPA's one-year limitations period runs "from the date on which the violation occurs." 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(d).  
  
*Rotkiske v. Klemm*, 589 U.S. 8 (2019), held that the limitations period runs from the date of the violation, not from the date of discovery. The Court reserved the question whether equitable tolling applies in cases of fraudulent concealment.  
  
### 8.1 Pleading Implications  
  
- Plead the **exact date** of each alleged violation.  
- For multi-letter or multi-call campaigns, plead each communication separately; each violation triggers its own limitations period under the "separate violation" approach.  
- If the case is filed more than one year after the earliest violation, plead facts supporting equitable tolling (fraudulent concealment, debtor unaware of operative facts).  
- Reg F § 1006.6(c)'s opt-out provisions do not affect limitations.

## 9. Damages Pleading  
  
### 9.1 Statutory Damages — § 1692k(a)(2)(A)  
  
In individual actions, statutory damages up to $1,000 per case per defendant. Plead:  
  
> *Pursuant to 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(2)(A), Plaintiff is entitled to statutory damages of up to $1,000.*  
  
Courts consider the frequency, persistence, and nature of the noncompliance in setting statutory damages. § 1692k(b)(1).  
  
### 9.2 Actual Damages — § 1692k(a)(1)  
  
Plead with specificity:  
- Out-of-pocket expenses;  
- Lost wages (calculated as hours times hourly rate);  
- Emotional distress (anxiety, embarrassment, sleeplessness — supported by factual narrative);  
- Damage to credit (where applicable and traceable to defendant's conduct).  
  
Emotional-distress damages do not require expert medical testimony in most circuits but should be pleaded with concrete sensory and behavioral detail.  
  
### 9.3 Attorney's Fees and Costs — § 1692k(a)(3)  
  
Mandatory for prevailing plaintiff. Plead:  
  
> *Pursuant to 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(3), Plaintiff is entitled to reasonable attorney's fees and the costs of this action.*

## 10. Class Allegations Checklist  
  
For class-action FDCPA cases (see `fdcpa_plead_03` for full treatment):  
  
- Class definition: Identify recipients of specific form letter or recipients of communications during defined period;  
- Numerosity: Plead reasonable estimate based on debt collector's business volume;  
- Commonality: Plead uniform language/practice;  
- Typicality: Allege named plaintiff received the same communication;  
- Adequacy: Plead competence of counsel and absence of conflicts;  
- Predominance (Rule 23(b)(3)): Statutory damages predominate over individual issues;  
- Ascertainability: Class members identifiable from defendant's records;  
- 1% net worth cap allegation: Plead defendant's approximate net worth as basis for maximum classwide damages;  
- *TransUnion* compliance: Plead concrete harm for named plaintiff; address standing of absent class members.

## 11. Pre-Filing Practical Checklist  
  
Before filing any FDCPA complaint, verify:  
  
- [ ] One-year limitations period  
- [ ] Concrete-harm allegations for federal forum  
- [ ] Defendant's debt-collector status pleaded with facts  
- [ ] Specific subsection violations identified  
- [ ] Challenged communication quoted or attached  
- [ ] Plaintiff is a "consumer" and obligation is a "debt"  
- [ ] Damages prayer includes statutory, actual, attorney's fees, costs  
- [ ] Jury demand under Fed. R. Civ. P. 38  
- [ ] If class, Rule 23 allegations complete  
- [ ] Rule 11 inquiry completed (factual investigation, legal basis)
