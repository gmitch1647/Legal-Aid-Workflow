## 1. The *TransUnion v. Ramirez* Framework  
  
### 1.1 The holding  
  
In *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), the Supreme Court (Kavanaugh, J., 5-4) reaffirmed that "Article III standing requires a **concrete injury even in the context of a statutory violation**. For standing purposes, therefore, an important difference exists between (i) a plaintiff's statutory cause of action to sue a defendant over the defendant's violation of federal law, and (ii) a plaintiff's suffering concrete harm because of the defendant's violation of federal law."  
  
The Court rejected the proposition that any statutory violation, without more, suffices for Article III injury: "**No concrete harm, no standing.**" *Id.* at 442.  
  
### 1.2 The "close historical or common-law analogue" test  
  
To assess whether an intangible injury is "concrete," courts look to whether the alleged harm has "a close relationship to harms traditionally recognized as providing a basis for lawsuits in American courts." *Id.* at 424–25. Reputational harm, disclosure of private information, intrusion upon seclusion, and similar common-law torts furnish recognized analogues.  
  
Bare procedural violations divorced from concrete harm are insufficient. *Id.* at 426–27 ("an injury in law is not an injury in fact").  
  
### 1.3 Application to FDCPA  
  
Lower courts have rigorously applied *TransUnion* to FDCPA suits. Three core questions in every FDCPA case:  
  
1. **What is the alleged statutory violation?** (procedural disclosure failure vs. substantive harm-inflicting act)  
2. **What concrete harm does the plaintiff identify?** (must be alleged with specificity)  
3. **What common-law analogue does the harm resemble?** (defamation, intrusion, conversion, disclosure, etc.)  
  
Plaintiffs who fail to plead specific concrete harm now routinely lose at the threshold.

## 2. Defense Rule 12(b)(1) Motion Practice  
  
### 2.1 Facial vs. factual attack  
  
Two species of 12(b)(1) motion:  
  
- **Facial attack:** challenges the sufficiency of the complaint's standing allegations. Court accepts well-pleaded allegations as true and asks whether they show concrete injury.  
- **Factual attack:** challenges the truth of standing allegations. Court may consider evidence outside the pleadings (declarations, deposition excerpts) and resolve disputed facts. Standing must be proved by a preponderance. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 561 (1992).  
  
The defense should specify which type of attack it is making. Facial attacks are more frequent at the pleading stage; factual attacks are more typical after standing discovery.  
  
### 2.2 Sample defense brief structure  
  
```  
INTRODUCTION  
 No concrete harm pleaded; TransUnion bars federal jurisdiction;  
 dismissal without prejudice for re-filing in state court  
  
LEGAL STANDARD  
 12(b)(1) framework; TransUnion's "concrete harm + analogue" test;  
 bare procedural violations insufficient  
  
ARGUMENT  
I. PLAINTIFF FAILS TO PLEAD A CONCRETE INJURY  
 A. The alleged statutory violation is procedural at best  
 B. The complaint pleads no concrete consequence of the violation  
 C. Conclusory "emotional distress" allegations are insufficient  
II. NO CLOSE COMMON-LAW ANALOGUE EXISTS  
 A. The alleged harm does not resemble defamation  
 B. No publicity element for disclosure tort (Hunstein)  
 C. No intrusion-upon-seclusion analogue  
III. DISMISSAL SHOULD BE WITHOUT PREJUDICE TO STATE-COURT REFILING  
  
CONCLUSION  
```  
  
### 2.3 Strategic considerations  
  
- **Without-prejudice dismissal.** 12(b)(1) dismissal is without prejudice and contemplates state-court refiling. Defendants who prefer state-court litigation (less robust discovery, no class action vehicle, defense-friendly judges) favor 12(b)(1).  
- **Concurrent 12(b)(6).** Defendants who want a merits-binding judgment can pair 12(b)(1) with 12(b)(6). If standing exists, the court reaches merits; if standing is absent, the court dismisses without merits ruling.  
- **Removal cases.** When defense removed and now moves for 12(b)(1) dismissal, *Wisconsin Dep't of Corrections v. Schacht*, 524 U.S. 381 (1998), and progeny permit remand rather than dismissal under 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c). Many defendants prefer remand over outright dismissal to lock in the state forum.

## 3. Plaintiff's Standing Pleading  
  
### 3.1 Plead concrete harm in every complaint  
  
Post-*TransUnion*, conclusory "FDCPA violation" allegations are insufficient. Plead specific facts:  
  
- **Emotional distress.** "Plaintiff suffered sleepless nights, anxiety, and inability to concentrate at work, requiring [over-the-counter medication / counseling] in [month/year]." Avoid bare "distress" labels.  
- **Time spent.** "Plaintiff spent approximately [X] hours over [Y] days disputing the debt, retrieving documents, drafting correspondence, and consulting counsel — time she would otherwise have spent on [paid work / family obligations]."  
- **Money paid.** "Plaintiff paid $[X] toward the unauthorized portion of the debt or fee, money she would not have paid absent defendant's misrepresentation."  
- **Reputational injury.** "Defendant published the disputed debt on plaintiff's credit reports, lowering her credit score by [X] points and causing denial of [credit / employment / housing] on [date]."  
- **Risk-of-harm.** "Defendant's failure to mark the debt as disputed under § 1692e(8) exposed plaintiff to a substantial risk of inaccurate adverse credit decisions." *Sayles v. Advanced Recovery Sys., Inc.*, 865 F.3d 246 (5th Cir. 2017).  
  
### 3.2 Identify the common-law analogue  
  
Match the harm to the closest tort:  
  
- **Defamation analogue:** publication of false statement about debt.  
- **Intrusion-upon-seclusion analogue:** harassing calls, contacts at unusual hours.  
- **Conversion analogue:** payment of unauthorized amount.  
- **Public-disclosure analogue:** dissemination of private financial information (though *Hunstein* limits this).  
  
Briefly explain why the statutory violation resembles the tort. Two sentences are sufficient at the pleading stage.  
  
### 3.3 Sample concrete-harm allegations (templates)  
  
**Distress + time-spent allegation:**  
> "As a direct result of Defendant's unlawful conduct, Plaintiff suffered emotional distress, including persistent anxiety, insomnia from August 15 through September 30, 2024, and recurring intrusive thoughts about whether she would be sued. She lost approximately 14 hours over three weeks researching her rights, retrieving records, and disputing the debt — time during which she would otherwise have been earning income or attending to her family."  
  
**Payment-of-unauthorized-amount allegation:**  
> "Believing Defendant's misrepresentation that the $375 collection fee was lawful, Plaintiff paid $375 on October 4, 2024. This payment was an unauthorized amount under § 1692f(1) and the cardholder agreement (Ex. A). But for Defendant's misrepresentation, Plaintiff would not have paid."  
  
**Reputational harm allegation:**  
> "Defendant reported the disputed debt to TransUnion, Equifax, and Experian without marking it as disputed, in violation of § 1692e(8). Plaintiff's FICO score fell from [X] to [Y] between [date] and [date]. On [date], Plaintiff was denied an auto loan with reference to the disputed tradeline (Ex. B)."

## 4. Claim-by-Claim FDCPA Standing Analysis  
  
### 4.1 § 1692c — Communications  
  
#### Communication with consumer (1692c(a))  
  
- **At unusual time/place:** intrusion-upon-seclusion analogue typically supports standing where the consumer alleges actual disruption.  
- **When represented by counsel:** typically a procedural violation; courts have split. Plaintiffs should plead specific anxiety or confusion resulting from the contact and time spent involving counsel.  
- **After cease letter:** analogous to intrusion or unwanted contact; courts generally find standing where the consumer pleads emotional consequences. *Lupia v. Medicredit, Inc.*, 8 F.4th 1184 (10th Cir. 2021) (call after cease letter, standing found).  
  
#### Third-party communication (1692c(b))  
  
- **Letter-vendor transmissions (*Hunstein*).** *Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Mgmt. Servs., Inc.*, 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc), held no standing for letter-vendor disclosures absent publicity to the public at large. Other circuits have not uniformly followed; *Quaglia v. NS193, LLC*, 96 F.4th 222 (2d Cir. 2024), accepted the *Hunstein* framework while leaving room for fact-specific contests.  
- **Contact with employer, family, neighbors.** Standing is more readily found because the communication targets specific third parties with whom the consumer has an interest in concealment. *Sayles v. Allied Interstate, LLC*, 433 F. Supp. 3d 211 (D. Conn. 2019) (workplace contact).  
  
### 4.2 § 1692d — Harassment / Abuse  
  
Emotional distress is the natural concrete harm for harassment claims. Courts generally find standing where the consumer pleads:  
  
- Sleep disturbance, anxiety, fear, embarrassment;  
- Avoidance of the phone (loss of legitimate calls);  
- Workplace consequences if calls reached the employer;  
- Specific time-of-day, frequency, or content of the calls.  
  
The intrusion-upon-seclusion analogue is well-established. *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 652B.  
  
**Sample brief:** > "Defendant's 47 calls over three weeks, several after 9 p.m. and several at plaintiff's workplace, inflict the precise harm that the intrusion-upon-seclusion tort has long redressed. Plaintiff alleges resulting anxiety, sleep loss, and workplace embarrassment — concrete injuries far beyond a bare statutory violation."  
  
### 4.3 § 1692e — False or Misleading Representations  
  
This is the most contested standing terrain.  
  
- ***Trichell v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.***, 964 F.3d 990 (11th Cir. 2020). Plaintiff who received a time-barred-debt letter but admitted he was not personally misled lacked standing. The court emphasized that the LSC standard governs liability but standing requires individual injury. Pre-*TransUnion* but consistent with it.  
- ***Pierre v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.***, 29 F.4th 934 (7th Cir. 2022). Vacated $350,000 class judgment for lack of standing where plaintiff alleged emotional distress, confusion, and time spent disputing a time-barred-debt letter but did not pay or act on the letter. The court held those allegations did not satisfy *TransUnion*. The dissent flagged a split with the Third, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits.  
- ***Wadsworth v. Kross, Lieberman & Stone, Inc.***, 12 F.4th 665 (7th Cir. 2021) (no standing for confusion); compare *Lupia v. Medicredit*, 8 F.4th 1184 (10th Cir. 2021) (standing for unwanted contact + distress).  
- ***Sayles v. Advanced Recovery Sys., Inc.***, 865 F.3d 246 (5th Cir. 2017). Risk of credit harm from § 1692e(8) violation (failure to mark disputed) sufficed for standing pre-*TransUnion*; standing remains viable post-*TransUnion* with careful pleading.  
  
**Defense brief language:**  
> "Plaintiff's § 1692e claim mirrors *Pierre*. She received a letter she believes was misleading, was not in fact misled, did not pay anything, and alleges only generalized distress and time spent disputing the letter. The Seventh Circuit en banc rejected exactly that injury theory under *TransUnion*. 29 F.4th at 938."  
  
**Plaintiff brief language:**  
> "Unlike *Pierre*, Plaintiff was personally misled by Defendant's misrepresentation: she paid $X believing the debt was owed (Compl. ¶ 22) and reasonably relied on Defendant's false statement of authority. Payment of money in reliance on misrepresentation is the paradigm common-law harm and amply satisfies *TransUnion*."  
  
### 4.4 § 1692f — Unfair / Unconscionable  
  
- **Unauthorized fee paid.** Payment of an unauthorized fee is concrete monetary harm; the conversion analogue applies. Standing routinely found.  
- **Unauthorized fee assessed but unpaid.** Some courts find standing based on liability exposure; others require actual payment. The cautious plaintiff pleads payment plus emotional distress.  
- **Other unfair-practice claims** (e.g., postcard collection, deceptive forms): standing assessment turns on whether actual harm flowed from the practice.  
  
### 4.5 § 1692g — Validation Notice  
  
Validation-notice claims are often the most vulnerable to standing dismissal because they are procedural by nature. The plaintiff should plead:  
  
- That she would have exercised dispute rights had they been properly disclosed;  
- That she paid money she would not have paid absent the validation-notice failure;  
- That she suffered concrete confusion or anxiety distinguishable from a bare statutory violation.  
  
Without such allegations, validation-notice claims face strong dismissal arguments. See *Nyanjom v. Hawker Beechcraft Corp.*, 641 F. App'x 795 (10th Cir. 2016) (procedural violation alone insufficient).  
  
### 4.6 § 1692b — Location Information  
  
Communications with third parties to obtain location information must comply with strict rules (e.g., disclose only that confirmation of location is sought; not state that consumer owes a debt). Standing for § 1692b claims is contested:  
  
- If the third party learns of the debt due to a § 1692b violation, the reputational/disclosure analogue supports standing.  
- If the violation is purely technical (e.g., failing to identify oneself by name without further consequence), standing is precarious.

## 5. Class Certification Standing  
  
### 5.1 *TransUnion*'s class implications  
  
The most consequential ruling in *TransUnion* for FDCPA practice: **every class member must have concrete injury — not just the named plaintiff.** "Every class member must have Article III standing in order to recover individual damages." 594 U.S. at 431.  
  
The Court held that class members whose information was internally flagged by TransUnion but never disseminated to third parties lacked standing, while those whose information was disclosed to creditors had standing.  
  
### 5.2 Implications for FDCPA classes  
  
- **Letter-content cases** (§ 1692e, 1692g): every class member received the same letter; standing analysis is uniform but vulnerable post-*Pierre*/*Trichell*.  
- **Letter-vendor cases** (§ 1692c(b)): post-*Hunstein*, standing is class-wide vulnerable.  
- **Harassment cases** (§ 1692d): individualized harm makes class certification difficult independent of standing.  
- **Unauthorized-fee cases** (§ 1692f(1)): class members who paid have standing; those who were merely billed may not.  
  
### 5.3 Narrowed class definitions  
  
Post-*TransUnion*, plaintiffs increasingly narrow classes to members with verifiable concrete harm:  
  
- "All persons who **paid** the unauthorized fee" (rather than "all persons charged");  
- "All persons whose credit report **was accessed** by a third party showing the disputed debt unmarked" (rather than "all persons whose accounts were reported");  
- "All persons who **received and read** the dunning letter" (with read-receipt evidence or attestation).  
  
### 5.4 Sample narrowed class definition  
  
> "All natural persons in [State] who, within one year prior to the filing of this action: (a) were sent the letter substantially in the form attached as Exhibit A; (b) **paid all or part of the $[X] collection fee** in response to that letter; and (c) were not in default of a business debt at the time of the underlying obligation."  
  
### 5.5 Standing-based MSJ  
  
Defendants increasingly raise standing not just at the pleading stage but also at class certification and summary judgment. Plaintiff should plead concrete harm in detail and develop the record to support certification.

## 6. Discovery on Standing  
  
### 6.1 Permissible scope  
  
Standing-focused discovery is generally permitted to develop the factual record:  
  
- Plaintiff's emotional-distress evidence (therapy records, journal entries, witness declarations);  
- Time-spent records (calendar entries, correspondence logs);  
- Money-paid evidence (bank statements, canceled checks);  
- Causation evidence (would-have-acted-differently testimony).  
  
Plaintiffs should be prepared to support standing allegations with discoverable evidence; speculative pleading invites *TransUnion* dismissal at MSJ.  
  
### 6.2 Standing-based MSJ  
  
After discovery, defense may move for summary judgment on standing under Rule 56. *Lujan* requires standing to be proved with evidence — not just allegations — by summary judgment. Where plaintiff cannot produce concrete-harm evidence beyond her own testimony, MSJ on standing remains a live risk.

## 7. Common-Law Analogues — Detailed Guide  
  
### 7.1 Defamation analogue  
  
**Elements:** false statement, publication to third party, damage to reputation. Defamation is well-recognized at common law and supports standing for FDCPA claims involving:  
  
- Furnishing false debt information to a CRA (§ 1692e(8));  
- Communicating false debt status to third parties;  
- "Publishing" debt lists (§ 1692d(3)).  
  
**Pleading template:**  
> "Defendant's furnishing of inaccurate information about Plaintiff's debt to the credit reporting agencies is closely analogous to common-law defamation — a publication of a false statement that has injured Plaintiff's reputation and creditworthiness, redressable at common law for centuries."  
  
### 7.2 Intrusion-upon-seclusion analogue  
  
**Elements:** intentional intrusion, upon the solitude or seclusion of another, that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person. *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 652B. Recognized at common law and embraced in *TransUnion* as a valid analogue. Supports standing for:  
  
- Harassing calls (§ 1692d);  
- Communications at unusual hours (§ 1692c(a)(1));  
- Continued contact after cease demand (§ 1692c(c));  
- Workplace contact (§ 1692c(a)(3)).  
  
**Pleading template:**  
> "Defendant's 47 calls over three weeks — including calls at 8:30 p.m., calls to Plaintiff's workplace after she informed Defendant of her employer's prohibition, and calls after Plaintiff's written cease demand — constitute the precise intrusion-upon-seclusion that the common law has long recognized. The tort 'consists solely of an intentional interference with [plaintiff's] interest in solitude or seclusion.' Restatement § 652B cmt. a."  
  
### 7.3 Conversion analogue  
  
**Elements:** wrongful exercise of dominion over plaintiff's property. Supports standing for:  
  
- Payment of unauthorized fees (§ 1692f(1));  
- Wrongful enforcement of security interests (§ 1692f(6));  
- Misapplication of payments.  
  
**Pleading template:**  
> "Plaintiff's payment of the unauthorized $375 collection fee, induced by Defendant's misrepresentation that the fee was lawful, is analogous to common-law conversion. Defendant exercised wrongful dominion over funds Plaintiff would not otherwise have surrendered."  
  
### 7.4 Public-disclosure-of-private-facts analogue  
  
**Elements:** publication, of private facts, that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person, and not of legitimate public concern. *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 652D. **The "publicity" element is restrictive** — *Hunstein* held that disclosure to a single mail vendor does not satisfy "publicity."  
  
Use this analogue with caution:  
  
- **Available** where the collector disclosed the debt to multiple persons, posted information publicly, or shared with employers/family/community.  
- **Limited** where disclosure was to a single contractor or intermediary (post-*Hunstein* analysis).  
  
### 7.5 False-light analogue  
  
**Elements:** publication, placing plaintiff in a false light, that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person. Useful for some § 1692e false-statement claims where the misrepresentation affects how third parties perceive the plaintiff.  
  
### 7.6 Fraud / misrepresentation analogue  
  
**Elements:** false statement, intent, reliance, damages. Useful for § 1692e claims where the consumer relied to her detriment. The reliance element supplies the "concrete harm" — the consumer would not have acted (paid, disclosed information, foregone defenses) absent the misrepresentation.

## 8. Concrete-Harm Sample Allegations by Fact Pattern  
  
### 8.1 Harassing-call fact pattern  
  
> "Between July 12 and August 31, 2024, Defendant placed at least 32 calls to Plaintiff's cell phone, including calls before 8 a.m. and after 9 p.m. on at least seven occasions. On August 14, Defendant's representative threatened Plaintiff with a lawsuit Defendant had no intent to file. As a result, Plaintiff suffered persistent anxiety, two weeks of insomnia treated with over-the-counter sleep aids, and twice missed scheduled work shifts to attend to her distress. She lost approximately $480 in wages. Plaintiff's harm is analogous to common-law intrusion upon seclusion."  
  
### 8.2 Letter-vendor fact pattern (in non-11th Cir.)  
  
> "Defendant transmitted Plaintiff's account information — her name, account balance, alleged creditor, and unique account number — to a third-party mail vendor, exposing private financial information beyond the parties to the underlying obligation. Plaintiff experienced anxiety on learning of the transmission and spent four hours investigating whether her information had been further redistributed. The harm resembles common-law publication of private facts, with the disclosure to commercial third parties constituting sufficient publicity to ground standing in this jurisdiction."  
  
### 8.3 Unauthorized-fee fact pattern  
  
> "On October 4, 2024, in response to Defendant's letter (Ex. A) demanding $1,275 — comprising the principal of $900 and a 'collection fee' of $375 — Plaintiff paid the full amount. The $375 fee was unauthorized by both the cardholder agreement (Ex. B § 7) and applicable state law. Plaintiff would not have paid the $375 absent Defendant's misrepresentation that the fee was lawful. Her injury is concrete monetary loss, analogous to common-law conversion or fraud-induced payment."  
  
### 8.4 Misreported-debt fact pattern  
  
> "After Plaintiff disputed the debt by letter dated June 1, 2024, Defendant continued to report the debt to Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion without marking it disputed in violation of § 1692e(8). Plaintiff's FICO score fell from 712 to 658 between June and September 2024. On September 15, Plaintiff applied for a refinance loan with [Lender] and was denied at the credit-decision stage citing the disputed tradeline (Ex. C). The harm — reputational injury manifested in adverse credit decisions — is the precise injury that common-law defamation has redressed for centuries."  
  
### 8.5 Time-barred-debt collection-letter fact pattern (post-*Pierre*)  
  
> "Defendant's letter (Ex. A) demanded payment of a debt that had been time-barred since 2018, but did not include the time-barred disclosure required by 12 C.F.R. § 1006.26. Plaintiff, an unsophisticated consumer who did not know the debt was unenforceable, believed the demand to be valid and on October 11, 2024, paid $250 from her tax refund. Her concrete injury is monetary loss caused by Defendant's misrepresentation — the textbook harm redressed at common law by the action for fraud."  
  
*Note:* This is the pattern that distinguishes a viable § 1692e claim from *Pierre*. The key is **payment in reliance**, not mere receipt of the letter.  
  
### 8.6 Cease-and-desist violation fact pattern  
  
> "On July 1, 2024, Plaintiff sent Defendant a written cease-communication notice under § 1692c(c). Defendant nevertheless called Plaintiff on July 11, July 18, August 3, and August 12, 2024. Each call caused Plaintiff anxiety and disrupted family time. On August 12, the call reached Plaintiff while she was driving and contributed to a near-collision. The harm is intrusion upon seclusion, recognized at common law since the early twentieth century."

## 9. Discovery-Stage Standing Considerations  
  
### 9.1 Document evidence for standing  
  
Plaintiffs should preserve and produce:  
  
- Therapy/medical records reflecting collection-related distress;  
- Personal journals or notes contemporaneous to the events;  
- Calendar entries showing time spent;  
- Phone records corroborating call counts and times;  
- Bank statements showing payments;  
- Credit reports showing reporting and score effects;  
- Lender correspondence showing adverse decisions.  
  
### 9.2 Standing depositions  
  
Defense counsel will depose the plaintiff on standing. Anticipate questions:  
  
- What was your physical and emotional state before/after the conduct?  
- How many hours did you actually spend disputing? (specific dates, activities)  
- Did you pay anything? Why did you pay?  
- Did you read the letter? Did you understand it? Did you act differently because of it?  
  
Prepare the plaintiff to answer concretely and consistently with the complaint.

## 10. Master Brief Outlines  
  
### 10.1 Defense Rule 12(b)(1) brief outline  
  
```  
INTRODUCTION  
  
LEGAL STANDARD  
 Rule 12(b)(1); standing as jurisdictional prerequisite;  
 TransUnion's "concrete harm + analogue" test;  
 plaintiff bears the burden  
  
ARGUMENT  
I. NO CONCRETE HARM IS ALLEGED  
 A. The complaint pleads only a procedural violation  
 B. Generic distress and time-spent are insufficient (Pierre)  
 C. Plaintiff was not personally affected (Trichell)  
II. NO CLOSE COMMON-LAW ANALOGUE  
 A. No defamation publication  
 B. No intrusion-upon-seclusion (no harassment alleged)  
 C. No publicity for disclosure tort (Hunstein)  
III. THE CLAIM SHOULD BE DISMISSED WITHOUT PREJUDICE  
 A. Without prejudice for state-court refiling  
 B. Alternatively, remand under § 1447(c) if removed  
  
CONCLUSION  
```  
  
### 10.2 Plaintiff opposition brief outline  
  
```  
INTRODUCTION  
  
LEGAL STANDARD  
 Standing at pleading stage requires only plausible allegations;  
 TransUnion does not heighten pleading; common-law analogues are  
 "instructive, not dispositive" (594 U.S. at 424)  
  
ARGUMENT  
I. PLAINTIFF PLEADS CONCRETE HARM  
 A. Emotional distress with specifics  
 B. Time spent disputing — quantified  
 C. Money paid — quantified  
 D. Reputational/credit harm — quantified  
II. EACH HARM HAS A CLOSE COMMON-LAW ANALOGUE  
 A. Defamation analogue (publication)  
 B. Intrusion-upon-seclusion analogue (harassment)  
 C. Conversion analogue (payment)  
 D. Fraud analogue (reliance)  
III. PIERRE AND TRICHELL ARE DISTINGUISHABLE  
 A. Unlike Pierre, plaintiff acted on the misrepresentation  
 B. Unlike Trichell, plaintiff was personally misled  
  
CONCLUSION  
```

## 11. Practitioner Notes — Tactical Considerations  
  
- **Federal-court access is not always preferred.** Plaintiff counsel should consider whether the case is better suited to state court (no standing requirement under most state constitutions). Filing in state court is sometimes strategically wiser than risking *TransUnion* dismissal.  
- **Removal calculus.** If plaintiff files in state court, the defense may decline to remove to avoid the *TransUnion* trap. Removed cases that lose standing must be remanded, not dismissed, under § 1447(c). Some defendants prefer remand; others prefer outright dismissal.  
- **Standing for declaratory and injunctive relief is different.** Past-injury allegations may not support prospective relief without a continuing or imminent harm. *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95 (1983).  
- **Class strategy.** Definitions must be tight enough that every member has standing. Subclasses by type of harm (paid vs. not paid; reported vs. not reported) are increasingly common.  
- **Pleading detail vs. trial proof.** *TransUnion* requires concrete *evidence* at MSJ and trial — not just allegations. Plaintiff should not plead more than she can prove.
