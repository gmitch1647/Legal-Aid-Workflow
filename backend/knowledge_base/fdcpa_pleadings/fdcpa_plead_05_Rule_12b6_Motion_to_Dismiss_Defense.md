## 1. The Rule 12(b)(6) Framework in FDCPA Cases  
  
Under *Twombly* and *Iqbal*, the complaint must plead enough non-conclusory facts to make each element of the claim "plausible." For FDCPA claims, this requires plausible allegations that:  
  
1. The plaintiff is a "consumer" under § 1692a(3);  
2. The obligation is a "debt" under § 1692a(5);  
3. The defendant is a "debt collector" under § 1692a(6);  
4. The defendant engaged in conduct that violates a specific subsection of the FDCPA (1692b–1692j); and  
5. The violation caused (for actual-damages claims) or constitutes (for statutory damages) a cognizable injury — overlapping with Article III standing.  
  
Defense counsel attacks the complaint section by section. The court "accept[s] all well-pleaded factual allegations as true" but rejects "[t]hreadbare recitals of the elements" and "legal conclusions couched as factual allegations." *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 678–79. Documents attached to or integral to the complaint (e.g., the dunning letter) may be considered without converting the motion to summary judgment. *In re Burlington Coat Factory*, 114 F.3d 1410, 1426 (3d Cir. 1997).

## 2. Argument A — Defendant Is Not a "Debt Collector"  
  
This is the most frequent and frequently successful Rule 12(b)(6) defense.  
  
### 2.1 Statutory framework  
  
15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) defines "debt collector" through two principal clauses:  
  
- **"Regularly collects" prong** — "any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, **or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another**."  
- **"Principal purpose" prong** — same sentence: "in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts."  
  
### 2.2 *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 582 U.S. 79 (2017)  
  
A unanimous Court (Gorsuch, J.) held that an entity that purchases defaulted debts and collects on its own behalf is not collecting "debts owed . . . another" and therefore is not a "regularly collects" debt collector. The Court expressly declined to decide whether such an entity might still qualify under the "principal purpose" prong.  
  
**Brief takeaway:** Where the complaint alleges the defendant purchased the debt and is collecting on its own account, the "regularly collects another's debt" prong fails as a matter of law.  
  
### 2.3 *Obduskey v. McCarthy & Holthus LLP*, 586 U.S. 466 (2019)  
  
A law firm engaged in nonjudicial foreclosure is not a "debt collector" subject to the full FDCPA. Only § 1692f(6) (enforcement of a security interest without right) applies. Courts confronting nonjudicial foreclosure FDCPA complaints routinely dismiss all counts except § 1692f(6). The decision left open whether a firm that "strays" from state-mandated foreclosure procedure could exceed the security-enforcement carve-out.  
  
### 2.4 The post-*Henson* principal-purpose framework  
  
Two Third Circuit cases set the dominant standard:  
  
- ***Tepper v. Amos Financial, LLC***, 898 F.3d 364 (3d Cir. 2018). A debt buyer whose sole business is purchasing defaulted debts and collecting on them qualifies under the "principal purpose" prong even after *Henson*.  
- ***Barbato v. Greystone Alliance, LLC***, 916 F.3d 260 (3d Cir. 2019). "As long as a business's *raison d'etre* is obtaining payment on the debts that it acquires, it is a debt collector. Who actually obtains the payment or how they do so is of no moment." A passive debt buyer that outsources collection still qualifies.  
  
**Defense argument when applicable:** plead specific facts showing the defendant has business lines beyond debt collection (e.g., loan origination, servicing performing accounts, sales). The mere fact that defendant acquires debts is insufficient if collection is not the company's *raison d'etre*.  
  
### 2.5 Original-creditor exception  
  
§ 1692a(6) excludes the creditor itself: a person who collects its own debt that was *not in default when acquired* is not a "debt collector." § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). Banks collecting their own performing-then-defaulted loans are not "debt collectors." See *Pollice v. Nat'l Tax Funding, L.P.*, 225 F.3d 379, 403 (3d Cir. 2000).  
  
### 2.6 In-house/affiliate exception  
  
§ 1692a(6)(B) excludes "any officer or employee of a creditor while, in the name of the creditor, collecting debts for such creditor." This shields in-house collection departments and employees engaged in *named* collection on behalf of the creditor. The exception is narrow: collection under a name other than the creditor's name may forfeit it. See § 1692a(6) flush language.  
  
### 2.7 Sample brief structure — debt-collector argument  
  
```  
ARGUMENT  
I. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO PLEAD THAT DEFENDANT IS A "DEBT COLLECTOR"  
 A. The FDCPA's two definitions of "debt collector"  
 B. Defendant is not a "regularly collects" debt collector  
 1. The complaint alleges defendant collects its own debt — Henson  
 2. The complaint does not plead "regularly" — frequency facts absent  
 C. Defendant is not a "principal purpose" debt collector  
 1. Pleading principal purpose requires more than acquisition  
 2. The complaint does not allege defendant's raison d'etre is collection  
 D. [If applicable] Defendant is a creditor (1692a(6)(F)(iii))  
 E. [If applicable] Defendant performed only nonjudicial foreclosure — Obduskey  
```  
  
### 2.8 Common counterarguments  
  
- Defendant is a *debt buyer* whose business is buying defaulted portfolios → *Barbato*/*Tepper* control.  
- Allegation that defendant "regularly attempts to collect debts" plus identification of the dunning letter, NMLS registration, or state licensing is often sufficient at the pleading stage. *Schlaf v. Safeguard Prop., LLC*, 899 F.3d 459 (7th Cir. 2018).  
- Status is fact-bound and often inappropriate for resolution on the pleadings. Courts frequently deny dismissal and require discovery on collection frequency and business mix.

## 3. Argument B — The Obligation Is Not a "Debt"  
  
### 3.1 Statutory hook  
  
§ 1692a(5) defines "debt" as an obligation "of a consumer to pay money arising out of a transaction in which the money, property, insurance, or services which are the subject of the transaction are **primarily for personal, family, or household purposes**."  
  
### 3.2 Business-purpose exclusion  
  
Obligations arising from commercial or business transactions fall outside the FDCPA. *Bloom v. I.C. System, Inc.*, 972 F.2d 1067 (9th Cir. 1992) (business loan). When the complaint or attached documents reveal a business purpose (sole-proprietor business account, commercial credit card, vendor invoice), the claim is subject to dismissal.  
  
### 3.3 Mixed-purpose debts  
  
When a single account was used for both personal and business purposes, courts look to the **primary purpose at the time the transaction was incurred** — not the use at the time of collection. *Slenk v. Transworld Sys., Inc.*, 236 F.3d 1072, 1075 (9th Cir. 2001). A defendant may argue that the documents (e.g., commercial account agreement, business name on the account) preclude an inference of primarily personal purpose. Conclusory "consumer debt" allegations are insufficient where the documents show otherwise.  
  
### 3.4 Sample brief language  
  
> "The complaint pleads only the conclusion that the obligation is a 'consumer debt.' The account statement (Ex. A) is in the name of [Business LLC], lists the merchant category as B2B services, and predates plaintiff's personal use. Under *Slenk*, the operative inquiry is purpose at origination, not at collection. The complaint pleads no plausible facts that the transaction was 'primarily for personal, family, or household purposes' under § 1692a(5)."

## 4. Argument C — § 1692c Communication Claims  
  
### 4.1 Statutory framework  
  
§ 1692c restricts communications with (a) the consumer at unusual time/place or when represented by counsel, (b) third parties, and (c) after written cease-demand. § 1692c(b) excepts communications "with the consumer, the creditor, the attorney of the debt collector, the attorney of the creditor, a consumer reporting agency if otherwise permitted by law, or with the express permission of a court."  
  
### 4.2 Statutory exceptions as MTD grounds  
  
- **Attorney-of-debt-collector exception.** Communications with the collector's own counsel are categorically permitted.  
- **CRA exception.** Furnishing data to a credit bureau in compliance with the FCRA is excepted.  
- **Court order.** Litigation filings and required service satisfy the "express permission of a court" exception. See *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291 (1995) (FDCPA covers litigation activity generally, but compliance with court process is excepted).  
- **Consumer consent.** Written authorization to contact third parties (often in the underlying contract or via § 1692b location request) eliminates liability.  
  
### 4.3 *Hunstein* — Eleventh Circuit en banc  
  
In *Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Mgmt. Servs., Inc.*, 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc), the Eleventh Circuit reversed a prior panel and held that disclosing account information to a third-party mail vendor for letter preparation does not inflict the concrete harm required by Article III standing, because public-disclosure-of-private-facts tort requires *publicity to the public at large*, not transmission to a single vendor.  
  
**Defense usage:** Although *Hunstein* is technically a standing ruling under Rule 12(b)(1), it is often paired with a 12(b)(6) merits argument — that letter-vendor transmission is not a "communication" within the statutory bar, or that it falls outside § 1692c's purposes. Outside the Eleventh Circuit, courts have split. The defense brief should pair standing (file 07) with a § 1692c(b) merits argument that the vendor is functionally an extension of the collector or that no actionable third-party "communication" occurred.  
  
### 4.4 Sample brief structure  
  
```  
II. THE § 1692c CLAIM FAILS  
 A. The communication falls within a statutory exception  
 1. Attorney-of-collector exception (1692c(b))  
 2. CRA exception (1692c(b))  
 3. Express consumer consent  
 B. Letter-vendor transmissions are not actionable third-party  
 communications (cite Hunstein and progeny)  
```

## 5. Argument D — § 1692e False / Misleading Representations  
  
### 5.1 Statutory framework  
  
§ 1692e bars "false, deceptive, or misleading representation[s]" in connection with debt collection and supplies sixteen non-exhaustive examples.  
  
### 5.2 Materiality requirement  
  
The Seventh Circuit's *Hahn v. Triumph Partnerships, LLC*, 557 F.3d 755, 757 (7th Cir. 2009), and broad consensus among circuits require that the alleged misrepresentation be **material** — i.e., capable of influencing a least-sophisticated consumer's decisions. Immaterial misstatements (e.g., trivial typographical errors, technical formatting deviations) do not give rise to § 1692e liability.  
  
**Sample brief:**  
> "Plaintiff alleges the letter listed the original creditor's name with a minor typographical error. Plaintiff does not — and cannot — allege the error could have caused him to act differently regarding the debt. As *Donohue v. Quick Collect, Inc.*, 592 F.3d 1027, 1033–34 (9th Cir. 2010), holds, immaterial misstatements are not actionable under § 1692e."  
  
### 5.3 Statement not made / mischaracterized  
  
Where the dunning letter is attached to the complaint, the court reads it itself. Defense counsel argues that the alleged statement is not contained in the letter, or that plaintiff's characterization is contradicted by the document. The Court need not credit allegations that contradict an integral exhibit. *In re Wachovia Equity Sec. Litig.*, 753 F. Supp. 2d 326, 348 (S.D.N.Y. 2011).  
  
### 5.4 Statement accurate under the least-sophisticated-consumer (LSC) standard  
  
The LSC test (or "unsophisticated consumer" in the 7th Circuit) protects against deception viewed from a hypothetical naive consumer's perspective, but it "protect[s] debt collectors from liability for bizarre or idiosyncratic interpretations." *Clomon v. Jackson*, 988 F.2d 1314, 1320 (2d Cir. 1993). When the letter, on its face, accurately describes the debt and includes the validation notice, the claim can be dismissed as a matter of law.  
  
### 5.5 Time-barred debt and *Midland Funding v. Johnson*  
  
*Midland Funding, LLC v. Johnson*, 581 U.S. 224 (2017), held that filing a proof of claim on a stale (time-barred but unextinguished) debt in bankruptcy is not "false, deceptive, misleading, unfair, or unconscionable" under §§ 1692e and 1692f. Pre-Regulation F, time-barred debt dunning letters generated frequent FDCPA suits. Regulation F § 1006.26 now requires specific time-barred-debt disclosures, but where the letter complies (or where the dispute was filed in bankruptcy), *Midland* supports dismissal.  
  
### 5.6 Sample brief structure  
  
```  
III. THE § 1692e CLAIM FAILS  
 A. The alleged statement is not material  
 B. The letter itself contradicts plaintiff's characterization  
 C. Under the LSC standard, the letter is accurate  
 D. [Time-barred debt context] Midland Funding controls  
```

## 6. Argument E — § 1692f Unfair / Unconscionable  
  
### 6.1 Statutory framework  
  
§ 1692f bars "unfair or unconscionable means" with eight enumerated examples, including § 1692f(1) (collection of amounts not authorized by the agreement or by law).  
  
### 6.2 The unauthorized fee or amount was actually permitted  
  
When the underlying contract or applicable law authorizes the fee (e.g., late fees in cardholder agreement, statutory interest, court-awarded costs), § 1692f(1) is not violated. The defense should attach the operative contract or statute. *Kojetin v. CU Recovery, Inc.*, 212 F.3d 1318 (8th Cir. 2000) (NSF fee authorized by state law).  
  
### 6.3 Conduct not "unfair" or "unconscionable" as a matter of law  
  
§ 1692f's "catch-all" prohibition is not boundless. Courts require something beyond the conduct already covered by §§ 1692c, 1692d, or 1692e. Where the plaintiff merely repackages a § 1692e theory under § 1692f, the latter count is duplicative and dismissible. See *Foster v. AllianceOne Receivables Mgmt., Inc.*, No. 15 C 11108, 2016 WL 1719824, at *5 (N.D. Ill. Apr. 29, 2016).  
  
### 6.4 Sample brief language  
  
> "Plaintiff's § 1692f claim merely restates her § 1692e theory. Courts routinely dismiss such duplicative § 1692f claims as failing to identify conduct independently unfair or unconscionable beyond the alleged misrepresentation."

## 7. Argument F — § 1692g Validation Notice  
  
### 7.1 Statutory framework  
  
§ 1692g(a) requires that within five days of the initial communication, the collector send a written notice containing the amount of the debt, name of the creditor, validation rights, and dispute deadlines. § 1692g(b) requires cessation of collection if the consumer disputes in writing within 30 days, until verification is mailed.  
  
### 7.2 Sufficiency as a matter of law  
  
When the attached letter contains the required disclosures verbatim or substantially, dismissal is appropriate. The notice need not be perfect; it must be reasonably calculated to convey the validation rights to the LSC. *Avila v. Rubin*, 84 F.3d 222, 226 (7th Cir. 1996).  
  
### 7.3 Regulation F safe harbor — 12 C.F.R. § 1006.34(d)  
  
Regulation F's **Model Form B-1** validation notice (effective Nov. 30, 2021) operates as a safe harbor. A debt collector that uses Model Form B-1 (or one substantially similar) is in compliance with § 1006.34(d)(1) as a matter of law. Defense brief should attach the letter alongside Model B-1 and show line-by-line correspondence.  
  
### 7.4 Overshadowing and contradiction  
  
Plaintiffs often plead that another statement in the letter "overshadows" or contradicts the validation notice. Where the letter is attached and discloses the dispute rights conspicuously, dismissal is proper. See *Durkin v. Equifax Check Servs., Inc.*, 406 F.3d 410 (7th Cir. 2005).  
  
### 7.5 Sample brief structure  
  
```  
IV. THE § 1692g CLAIM FAILS  
 A. The letter contains all five required disclosures  
 B. The letter is substantially similar to Reg F Model Form B-1  
 1. Safe harbor under 12 C.F.R. § 1006.34(d)  
 C. No statement in the letter overshadows the validation notice  
```

## 8. Argument G — § 1692d Harassment or Abuse  
  
### 8.1 Statutory framework  
  
§ 1692d bars conduct "the natural consequence of which is to harass, oppress, or abuse" any person. Examples include violence threats, obscene language, publication of debtor lists, and repeated/continuous phone calls "with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass."  
  
### 8.2 Pleading deficiencies  
  
- **Conduct not as alleged.** Where call logs are attached or judicially noticeable, mismatches between alleged frequency and the record support dismissal.  
- **Call frequency below "natural consequence" threshold.** Courts dismiss complaints alleging only modest call volume without other aggravating circumstances. See *Tucker v. CBE Group, Inc.*, 710 F. Supp. 2d 1301 (M.D. Fla. 2010) (57 calls over 2 months insufficient without intent allegations).  
- **No alleged "intent to annoy, abuse, or harass"** under § 1692d(5).  
  
### 8.3 Regulation F 7-in-7 rule — 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2)(i)  
  
Reg F creates a rebuttable presumption of compliance when a collector places no more than **seven calls within seven consecutive days** for a particular debt, and waits seven days after a conversation. Defense brief should plead and document that calls fall within 7-in-7 and supplement with the absence of other harassing conduct (the rule does not categorically immunize, but creates the presumption).  
  
### 8.4 Sample brief language  
  
> "Reg F § 1006.14(b)(2)(i) creates a presumption of compliance with § 1692d at the 7-in-7 call-frequency level. Plaintiff alleges five calls over seven days (Compl. ¶ 18), well below the threshold, and pleads no harassing language, time-of-day violations, or third-party disclosures. Plaintiff's bare invocation of the statute fails *Iqbal*."

## 9. Argument H — Bona Fide Error Defense  
  
§ 1692k(c) provides a complete affirmative defense where the collector proves by a preponderance of the evidence that (1) the violation was unintentional, (2) resulted from a bona fide error, and (3) occurred notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid such error. *Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010) (defense unavailable for legal-interpretation errors).  
  
The defense is rarely a winning MTD argument because procedures and intent are factual. It surfaces at MTD only when the complaint itself pleads facts establishing the elements (e.g., describes a clerical error and quotes the collector's corrective letter). Otherwise, this defense waits for MSJ.

## 10. Argument I — Article III Standing (Rule 12(b)(1))  
  
Although technically Rule 12(b)(1) rather than 12(b)(6), the standing motion is the most powerful tool in the post-*TransUnion v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), defense arsenal. The defense argument: a bare procedural violation, without "concrete harm" tied to a close common-law analogue, is not redressable in federal court.  
  
**See `fdcpa_plead_07_Rule_12b1_Standing_Motions.md`** for full coverage including:  
  
- *Hunstein* (letter-vendor transmissions lack the publicity analogue);  
- *Trichell v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 964 F.3d 990 (11th Cir. 2020) (no standing where plaintiff was not personally misled);  
- *Pierre v. Midland Credit Mgmt., Inc.*, 29 F.4th 934 (7th Cir. 2022) (emotional distress from time-barred dunning insufficient);  
- *Sayles v. Advanced Recovery Sys., Inc.*, 865 F.3d 246 (5th Cir. 2017) (risk of credit harm sufficient).  
  
Defendants should consider whether to dismiss with prejudice (12(b)(6) on merits) or without prejudice (12(b)(1) standing — remand back to state court). Many sophisticated defendants prefer 12(b)(1) when the state-court alternative is favorable; others want a merits judgment to bind future actions.

## 11. Argument J — Statute of Limitations  
  
§ 1692k(d) sets a **one-year limitations period** running from "the date on which the violation occurs." In *Rotkiske v. Klemm*, 589 U.S. 8 (2019), the Supreme Court (Thomas, J., 8-1) rejected a general "discovery rule" toll. The clock starts on the violation date, not when plaintiff learned of it.  
  
### 11.1 Sample brief  
  
> "The letter at issue (Ex. A) is dated April 10, 2024. The complaint was filed June 3, 2025 — fourteen months later. Under *Rotkiske v. Klemm*, the one-year clock began on April 10, 2024 with no discovery-rule toll. The action is time-barred."  
  
### 11.2 Reservations  
  
*Rotkiske* left open the equitable-tolling and fraud-specific tolling questions. If plaintiff pleads concealment, defense should preserve the time-bar but anticipate factual discovery.  
  
### 11.3 Each-violation rule  
  
For repeated violations (e.g., monthly collection letters), each letter or call is a separate violation triggering its own one-year clock. *Solomon v. HSBC Mortg. Corp.*, 395 F. App'x 494, 497–98 (10th Cir. 2010). Defense should isolate dismissable counts (those outside the one-year window) and litigate the remainder.

## 12. Argument K — Iqbal/Twombly Insufficiency  
  
When the complaint pleads only the elements as legal conclusions without factual content — e.g., "Defendant is a debt collector"; "Defendant used false statements"; "Defendant harassed Plaintiff" — *Iqbal*/*Twombly* require dismissal.  
  
### 12.1 Common deficiencies  
  
- **No identification of which subsection of § 1692e is violated, or which statement violates it.**  
- **Generic "harassment" allegations** without call dates, times, frequency, or content.  
- **Conclusory communication allegations** without identifying the third party, content, or context.  
- **No facts supporting "debt collector" status** beyond the legal label.  
  
### 12.2 Sample brief  
  
> "Plaintiff alleges Defendant 'violated § 1692e by making false statements.' He does not identify the statement, the letter, the speaker, or the date. This is the precise kind of '[t]hreadbare recital[] of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory statements,' that *Iqbal* prohibits. 556 U.S. at 678."  
  
### 12.3 Leave to amend  
  
Many courts grant leave to amend on first dismissal under *Iqbal*. Defense should consider whether to seek dismissal with prejudice (e.g., on uncurable defects such as not-a-debt-collector status or SOL) versus without prejudice (on pleading-only defects).

## 13. Master Brief Outline — Defense Rule 12(b)(6) Motion  
  
```  
INTRODUCTION  
 Three-paragraph summary of dismissal grounds  
  
STATEMENT OF FACTS (drawn from complaint and attached exhibits)  
  
LEGAL STANDARD  
 Twombly / Iqbal; documents integral to complaint; FDCPA threshold elements  
  
ARGUMENT  
I. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO PLEAD A "DEBT COLLECTOR" (§ 1692a(6))  
II. THE OBLIGATION IS NOT A "DEBT" (§ 1692a(5))  
III. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO STATE A § 1692c CLAIM  
IV. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO STATE A § 1692e CLAIM  
V. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO STATE A § 1692f CLAIM  
VI. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO STATE A § 1692g CLAIM  
VII. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO STATE A § 1692d CLAIM  
VIII. THE CLAIMS ARE TIME-BARRED UNDER § 1692k(d) / ROTKISKE  
IX. [If applicable] STANDING UNDER RULE 12(B)(1)  
  
CONCLUSION  
 Specify with or without prejudice; identify which claims are not curable by amendment  
```

## 14. Common Counterarguments — Quick Reference  
  
| Defense Argument | Most Common Plaintiff Counter | Strength |  
|---|---|---|  
| Not a "debt collector" — *Henson* | Defendant qualifies under *Barbato*/*Tepper* principal-purpose prong | Fact-bound; often denied without prejudice |  
| Not a "debt" — business purpose | Mixed-purpose; pre-default consumer use | Fact-bound unless documents are conclusive |  
| Letter-vendor — *Hunstein* | Pre-*Hunstein* law in non-11th Circuits | Strong in 11th Cir.; split elsewhere |  
| § 1692e immateriality | Statement was material to LSC's decision | Often fact-bound; reserved for MSJ |  
| § 1692g — Reg F safe harbor | Letter deviates from Model B-1 | Strong if letter matches Model B-1 |  
| § 1692d — Reg F 7-in-7 | Aggravating conduct beyond call count | Rebuttable presumption only |  
| § 1692k(d) SOL — *Rotkiske* | Equitable tolling / fraud concealment | Strong unless concealment pleaded |  
| *Iqbal*/*Twombly* | Sufficient factual content alleged | Often grants leave to amend |  
| 12(b)(1) standing — *TransUnion* | Plaintiff suffered concrete harm | See file 07 |

## 15. Practitioner Notes  
  
- **Choose 12(b)(1) vs. 12(b)(6) deliberately.** A 12(b)(1) standing dismissal is without prejudice and can be re-filed in state court. A 12(b)(6) merits dismissal binds future federal litigation but may not bar state-court re-filing under state UDAP statutes.  
- **Attach the dunning letter.** Most successful FDCPA MTDs turn on the court reading the letter and finding plaintiff's characterization unsupportable. The letter is "integral" to the complaint and properly considered.  
- **Don't waste pages on bona fide error at MTD.** Save for MSJ.  
- **Plead alternative grounds.** Argue 12(b)(1) and 12(b)(6) together. If the court finds standing, it can still reach the merits; if it dismisses for lack of standing, the merits arguments preserve the record for appeal.
