# FDCPA Class Action Complaint Drafting  
  
**Topic:** Rule 23 class action complaints under the FDCPA  
**FRCP Cross-References:** Rules 8(a), 10(c), 23(a), 23(b)(2), 23(b)(3), 23(c)(1)(B), 23(g)  
**FDCPA Cross-References:** 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a, 1692e, 1692f, 1692g, 1692k(a)(2)(B), 1692k(b)(2)  
**Regulation F Cross-References:** 12 C.F.R. §§ 1006.18, 1006.26, 1006.34, 1006.38  
**Last Updated:** May 16, 2026  
**Sources:** Public-domain primary materials only (FRCP, FDCPA statute, Regulation F, published federal opinions, CFPB/FTC public guidance)  
  
---  
  
## 1. Introduction  
  
FDCPA class actions are typically built around **form letters** or **uniform call practices** — the kind of conduct that produces near-identical violations across hundreds or thousands of consumers. The statutory damages cap and uniform-conduct fact patterns make Rule 23(b)(3) classes a natural fit. This document covers drafting the class allegations and surveys the doctrinal landmines that determine whether a class will be certified.  
  
For underlying claim-by-claim drafting, see `fdcpa_plead_02`. For full sample class complaints, see `fdcpa_plead_04`.  
  
---  
  
## 2. Why FDCPA Cases Are Suited to Rule 23  
  
- **Form-letter uniformity.** Identical letter language sent to a defined recipient population creates classwide common questions.  
- **Statutory damages.** § 1692k(a)(2)(B) caps classwide statutory damages at 1% of net worth or $500,000, whichever is less — capping but standardizing recovery.  
- **No individualized reliance.** Most FDCPA provisions impose strict liability and use objective standards (least-sophisticated consumer), so individual reliance issues that defeat predominance in fraud cases are largely absent.  
- **Negative-value claims.** Small per-consumer recoveries make class certification the only practical vehicle.

## 3. Rule 23(a) Prerequisites  
  
### 3.1 Numerosity — Rule 23(a)(1)  
  
The class must be so numerous that joinder is impracticable. Form-letter cases satisfy numerosity easily.  
  
**Sample allegation:**  
  
> 70. Upon information and belief, Defendant mailed the form letter attached as Exhibit A (the "Form Letter") to hundreds, if not thousands, of consumers in [State] during the one-year period preceding the filing of this Complaint. The exact number is presently within Defendant's knowledge and exclusively maintained in Defendant's business records. Joinder of all class members is impracticable.  
  
Bare assertions ("upon information and belief, the class numbers in the thousands") generally survive pleading review where the conduct is a form letter; concrete numbers come in discovery.  
  
### 3.2 Commonality — Rule 23(a)(2)  
  
After *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), commonality requires "a common contention . . . capable of classwide resolution — which means that determination of its truth or falsity will resolve an issue that is central to the validity of each one of the claims in one stroke." *Id.* at 350.  
  
Form-letter cases ordinarily satisfy commonality: every recipient received the same allegedly unlawful language, and whether that language violates the FDCPA is a single legal question.  
  
**Sample allegation:**  
  
> 72. Common questions of law and fact include:  
>  
> a. Whether Defendant is a "debt collector" within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6);  
>  
> b. Whether the Form Letter is an "initial communication" within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1692g and 12 C.F.R. § 1006.34(a)(1);  
>  
> c. Whether the Form Letter's failure to disclose [the itemization date / the original creditor / the validation rights] violates 15 U.S.C. § 1692g(a) and 12 C.F.R. § 1006.34(c);  
>  
> d. Whether the Form Letter overshadows or is inconsistent with the validation notice in violation of 15 U.S.C. § 1692g(b);  
>  
> e. Whether the Form Letter contains false, deceptive, or misleading representations in violation of 15 U.S.C. § 1692e;  
>  
> f. The appropriate amount of statutory damages under 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(2)(B);  
>  
> g. Whether Defendant's net worth limits classwide statutory damages under 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(2)(B).  
  
### 3.3 Typicality — Rule 23(a)(3)  
  
The named plaintiff's claims must be typical of the class. The named plaintiff must have received the same form communication.  
  
**Sample allegation:**  
  
> 74. Plaintiff received the Form Letter on or about [date]. The Form Letter sent to Plaintiff was substantially identical to the form letter sent to all Class members, with only individualized fields (name, address, account number, balance) varying. Plaintiff's claims are typical of the claims of the Class.  
  
### 3.4 Adequacy — Rule 23(a)(4)  
  
The named plaintiff must fairly and adequately protect class interests, and counsel must be qualified (Rule 23(g)).  
  
**Sample allegation:**  
  
> 76. Plaintiff will fairly and adequately protect the interests of the Class. Plaintiff has retained competent counsel experienced in consumer-protection class litigation. Plaintiff has no interests antagonistic to those of the Class.  
>  
> 77. Plaintiff's counsel has prosecuted numerous FDCPA and consumer-protection class actions and has the resources and experience necessary to litigate this matter.

## 4. Rule 23(b)(3) Predominance and Superiority  
  
### 4.1 Predominance  
  
Questions of law or fact common to class members must predominate over questions affecting individual members. *Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591 (1997).  
  
In FDCPA form-letter cases, predominance is typically satisfied because:  
- The letter's lawfulness is a single legal question;  
- Liability does not require individualized reliance under the least-sophisticated-consumer standard;  
- Statutory damages do not require individualized proof.  
  
**Sample allegation:**  
  
> 80. Common questions of law and fact predominate over any individual issues. Defendant's liability turns on the content of the Form Letter, which was uniform as to all Class members. Statutory damages under 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(2)(B) are determined by reference to Defendant's net worth, not individual class member characteristics, and therefore do not introduce individualized issues.  
  
### 4.2 *Comcast* Damages Methodology  
  
In *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27 (2013), the Court required that damages methodology track plaintiffs' theory of liability for purposes of predominance. FDCPA classes generally satisfy *Comcast* because statutory damages are computed by formula (1% of net worth / class size) and require no individualized damages model.  
  
### 4.3 *Tyson Foods* and Representative Proof  
  
*Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo*, 577 U.S. 442 (2016), permits representative or statistical evidence in class actions where each class member could have relied on such evidence to prove an individual claim. For FDCPA form-letter cases, the letter itself is the representative evidence — no statistical sampling is needed.  
  
### 4.4 Superiority  
  
A class action must be superior to other available methods. **Sample allegation:**  
  
> 82. A class action is superior to other available methods for the fair and efficient adjudication of this controversy. Individual litigation of each class member's FDCPA claim is impracticable given the small statutory damages available and the cost of litigation. Concentration of these claims in a single forum will conserve judicial resources and avoid inconsistent rulings on the lawfulness of the Form Letter.

## 5. Ascertainability  
  
### 5.1 The Split — *Carrera* and *Byrd*  
  
In *Carrera v. Bayer Corp.*, 727 F.3d 300 (3d Cir. 2013), the Third Circuit required "administrative feasibility" of identifying class members, defeating certification where consumer purchases could not be verified from defendant's records. The Third Circuit subsequently softened this in *Byrd v. Aaron's Inc.*, 784 F.3d 154 (3d Cir. 2015), clarifying that the class need only be defined by objective criteria and identifiable in a reliable, administratively feasible way without significant individualized inquiry.  
  
Most other circuits (Second, Sixth, Seventh, Eighth, Ninth, Eleventh) reject a heightened ascertainability requirement; an objective class definition suffices. See, e.g., *Briseno v. ConAgra Foods, Inc.*, 844 F.3d 1121 (9th Cir. 2017).  
  
### 5.2 Why FDCPA Form-Letter Cases Avoid the Problem  
  
Defendant's mailing records identify recipients. Class members are typically ascertainable from defendant's account-management system or vendor mailing logs.  
  
**Sample allegation:**  
  
> 84. The Class is readily ascertainable through Defendant's business records, including Defendant's account-management system, mailing logs, and records maintained by Defendant's letter vendor (if any), all of which identify the consumers to whom the Form Letter was sent during the Class Period.

## 6. Class Definition Drafting  
  
Class definitions must be:  
- **Objective** — capable of being applied without merits adjudication;  
- **Bounded** — limited by time (Class Period), geography (state), conduct (specific letter), and recipient type (consumer);  
- **Not "fail-safe"** — must not be defined by reference to ultimate liability.  
  
### 6.1 Sample Class Definition  
  
> 85. Plaintiff brings this action on behalf of herself and the following Class pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(a) and 23(b)(3):  
>  
> *All persons with addresses in [State] to whom Defendant mailed, or caused to be mailed, a letter in the form of Exhibit A in connection with the collection of a consumer debt, during the period beginning one year prior to the filing of this Complaint and continuing through the date of class certification.*  
>  
> 86. Excluded from the Class are: (a) Defendant, its officers, directors, employees, and counsel; (b) the Court, court personnel, and members of their immediate families; (c) persons who timely opt out; (d) persons whose claims have been resolved by prior settlement or judgment.  
  
### 6.2 Avoiding Fail-Safe Problems  
  
A fail-safe class is one defined to include only those persons who would prevail. Example to avoid: *"All persons to whom Defendant sent an unlawful letter."* The corrected definition uses objective letter-content criteria and lets the merits determine whether the content violates the statute.

## 7. § 1692k(a)(2)(B) — Classwide Damages Cap  
  
### 7.1 Statutory Text  
  
> *"(B) in the case of a class action, (i) such amount for each named plaintiff as could be recovered under subparagraph (A) [up to $1,000], and (ii) such amount as the court may allow for all other class members, without regard to a minimum individual recovery, not to exceed the lesser of $500,000 or 1 per centum of the net worth of the debt collector . . . ."* 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(2)(B).  
  
### 7.2 Pleading the Cap  
  
**Sample allegations:**  
  
> 90. The Class is entitled to statutory damages under 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(2)(B), in an aggregate amount equal to the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of Defendant's net worth.  
>  
> 91. Upon information and belief, Defendant's net worth exceeds [or does not exceed] $50,000,000, making the applicable statutory damages cap [$500,000 / 1% of approximately $___].  
>  
> 92. Plaintiff, as named class representative, additionally seeks statutory damages of up to $1,000 pursuant to 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(2)(A) and (B)(i).  
  
### 7.3 § 1692k(b)(2) — Factors  
  
In determining the amount of damages in class actions, the court considers, among other relevant factors, the frequency and persistence of noncompliance, the nature of the noncompliance, the resources of the debt collector, the number of persons adversely affected, and the extent to which the noncompliance was intentional. 15 U.S.C. § 1692k(b)(2). Plead facts addressing each factor.

## 8. Post-*TransUnion* Class Standing  
  
### 8.1 The Holding's Effect on FDCPA Classes  
  
*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), held that **every** class member — not only the named plaintiff — must have Article III standing for purposes of statutory damages recovery in federal court. The Court rejected the argument that uninjured class members could collect statutory damages.  
  
Practical implication: a federal FDCPA class must be defined such that membership entails concrete injury, or the case will face certification or final-judgment problems.  
  
### 8.2 Pleading Strategies  
  
**Option 1 — Define class around an injury-conferring event.** Limit class membership to consumers who received the letter and either paid the debt, disputed the debt, or contacted counsel.  
  
**Option 2 — Plead the form letter itself as the injury-conferring event when analogous to a traditional tort.** Form misrepresentation analogous to defamation; harassing calls analogous to intrusion upon seclusion.  
  
**Option 3 — File in state court.** State courts are not bound by Article III. State analog statutes (Rosenthal Act in California, NY GBL § 601, FCCPA in Florida) may afford state-court standing for the same conduct.  
  
### 8.3 Sample Standing Allegations  
  
> 95. Each member of the Class suffered concrete harm sufficient to satisfy Article III. The Form Letter falsely represented [the amount / the legal status / etc.] of consumer debt. Receipt of such a misrepresentation in the U.S. Mail is closely analogous to the common-law tort of misrepresentation. Such misrepresentation caused each Class member to face the same risk of payment of amounts not owed, dispute confusion, and credit-history confusion. See *TransUnion*, 594 U.S. at 425.

## 9. Class Allegations Checklist  
  
- [ ] Class definition (objective, bounded, non-fail-safe)  
- [ ] Class period (within one-year FDCPA limitations)  
- [ ] Exclusions (defendants, court personnel, prior settlements)  
- [ ] Numerosity allegation  
- [ ] Commonality — enumerated common questions  
- [ ] Typicality — named plaintiff received same letter/conduct  
- [ ] Adequacy — named plaintiff + counsel  
- [ ] Predominance — single legal question on letter lawfulness  
- [ ] Superiority — small claims, judicial economy  
- [ ] Ascertainability — defendant's mailing records  
- [ ] § 1692k(a)(2)(B) cap allegation  
- [ ] § 1692k(b)(2) factors pleaded  
- [ ] *TransUnion* standing addressed for absent class members  
- [ ] Class counsel qualifications (Rule 23(g))

## 10. Anchor Cases — Quick Reference  
  
- **Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes**, 564 U.S. 338 (2011) — commonality requires a common contention capable of classwide resolution.  
- **Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor**, 521 U.S. 591 (1997) — predominance and superiority framework.  
- **Comcast Corp. v. Behrend**, 569 U.S. 27 (2013) — damages methodology must track liability theory.  
- **Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo**, 577 U.S. 442 (2016) — representative proof permissible where each class member could have used it individually.  
- **Carrera v. Bayer Corp.**, 727 F.3d 300 (3d Cir. 2013) — heightened ascertainability (administrative feasibility).  
- **Byrd v. Aaron's Inc.**, 784 F.3d 154 (3d Cir. 2015) — relaxing *Carrera*'s standard.  
- **TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez**, 594 U.S. 413 (2021) — concrete-harm standing for every class member.  
- **Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins**, 578 U.S. 330 (2016) — predicate concrete-injury rule.

## 11. Settlement and Notice Drafting Considerations  
  
Although outside the four corners of the complaint, counsel should consider at the pleading stage:  
  
- Rule 23(c)(2)(B) notice — manageable for form-letter classes via defendant's address list;  
- Settlement-class certifiability — *Amchem* requires the same Rule 23 analysis (minus manageability) for settlement classes;  
- Cy pres allocation — for unclaimed funds from statutory damages cap;  
- Attorney's fees petition under § 1692k(a)(3) and Rule 23(h).  
  
---  
  
## 12. Common Reasons FDCPA Class Certification Fails  
  
- Class includes non-consumers (debts not "consumer debts");  
- Class definition reaches multiple form letters with material differences;  
- Named plaintiff received a non-form, individualized communication;  
- Named plaintiff lacks standing (no concrete harm);  
- *TransUnion* problem with absent class members;  
- Adequacy challenge based on counsel's prior conduct or plaintiff's atypical claim.  
  
Address each of these in the complaint where possible.
