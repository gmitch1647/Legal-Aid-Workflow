# FDCPA Pleadings 06 — Rule 12(b)(6) Plaintiff Opposition  
  
| Field | Value |  
|---|---|  
| **Topic** | Plaintiff opposition to Rule 12(b)(6) motions to dismiss FDCPA claims |  
| **Procedural Rule** | Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6), 12(d), 15(a) (leave to amend) |  
| **Statute** | Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692–1692p |  
| **Regulation** | 12 C.F.R. Part 1006 (Regulation F) |  
| **Pleading Standard** | *Twombly*/*Iqbal* — plausibility, not probability |  
| **Last Updated** | May 16, 2026 |

## 1. Plaintiff-Side *Iqbal*/*Twombly* Framework  
  
The defense will overreach by demanding probability-level pleading. *Iqbal* requires only **plausibility**: "[a] claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable." *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 678. Plausibility "is not akin to a probability requirement." *Twombly*, 550 U.S. at 556.  
  
Three core opposition themes:  
  
1. **Take the well-pleaded facts as true and draw all reasonable inferences in plaintiff's favor.** *Erickson v. Pardus*, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007) (per curiam).  
2. **FDCPA is a remedial statute construed broadly** to protect consumers. *Hamilton v. United Healthcare of La., Inc.*, 310 F.3d 385, 392 (5th Cir. 2002).  
3. **Status questions (debt collector / debt) are fact-bound** and rarely resolvable on the pleadings without discovery.  
  
### 1.1 Sample boilerplate opening  
  
> "On a motion to dismiss, the Court accepts as true all well-pleaded factual allegations in the complaint and draws all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 678. *Iqbal* does not 'demand[] specific factual allegations' supporting each element; it requires only enough factual content to render the claim plausible. *Id.* The FDCPA, a remedial statute, is liberally construed in favor of consumers. *Hamilton*, 310 F.3d at 392. Defendant's motion misapplies the standard by demanding evidentiary specificity inappropriate at the pleading stage."

## 2. Opposing "Not a Debt Collector" Arguments  
  
### 2.1 Identifying the prong  
  
The FDCPA defines "debt collector" two ways under § 1692a(6): (a) any business whose "principal purpose" is the collection of any debts; and (b) any person who regularly collects debts "owed or due . . . another." Most successful complaints plead **both** prongs in the alternative.  
  
### 2.2 Responding to *Henson*  
  
*Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 582 U.S. 79 (2017), held that a debt buyer collecting its own debts is not a "regularly collects another's debt" collector — but expressly left open principal-purpose liability. Plaintiff's opposition:  
  
> "Defendant relies on *Henson* to argue it cannot be a debt collector because it owns the debt at issue. *Henson* answered only the 'regularly collects another's debt' question, expressly leaving principal-purpose liability for another day. 582 U.S. at 89. The Third Circuit and others have since held that debt buyers whose business *raison d'etre* is collection on acquired debts squarely fit the principal-purpose definition. *Barbato v. Greystone Alliance, LLC*, 916 F.3d 260, 267 (3d Cir. 2019); *Tepper v. Amos Fin., LLC*, 898 F.3d 364, 370 (3d Cir. 2018). The complaint alleges defendant's principal business is purchasing and collecting on defaulted accounts (Compl. ¶¶ 8–12). That is enough at the pleading stage."  
  
### 2.3 Responding to *Obduskey*  
  
Where defense argues *Obduskey* immunizes nonjudicial-foreclosure conduct, plaintiff should:  
  
- **Confine the holding.** *Obduskey* protects only "businesses engaged in no more than" state-mandated nonjudicial foreclosure activities. 586 U.S. at 481. If the firm did anything beyond (sent dunning letters, demanded payment outside foreclosure procedure, called the borrower), the full FDCPA applies.  
- **Preserve § 1692f(6).** *Obduskey* explicitly leaves § 1692f(6) (enforcing a security interest without right) applicable to all foreclosure conduct.  
  
### 2.4 Pleading principal-purpose facts  
  
Sufficient pleading includes any of:  
  
- Defendant's business description on its website or NMLS filing identifies debt collection as its primary line.  
- Defendant's revenue is derived primarily from collection activity.  
- Defendant is registered/bonded as a debt collector under state law.  
- Defendant's prior litigation history shows it is regularly sued as a debt collector.  
  
The complaint need not prove these facts with documents at the MTD stage; well-pleaded allegations on information and belief are sufficient if supported by some contextual basis. *Arista Records, LLC v. Doe 3*, 604 F.3d 110, 120 (2d Cir. 2010).  
  
### 2.5 Status is generally a fact question  
  
Courts routinely deny MTDs on collector-status grounds and reserve the question for summary judgment. *Goldstein v. Hutton, Ingram, Yuzek, Gainen, Carroll & Bertolotti*, 374 F.3d 56, 62–63 (2d Cir. 2004) (whether attorney "regularly" collects is fact-intensive).

## 3. Opposing "Not a Debt" Arguments  
  
### 3.1 Primary purpose at origination  
  
The defense will argue the obligation is commercial. Plaintiff responds with the consumer-purpose pleading framework:  
  
- **Specifically allege purchase use.** "Plaintiff used the credit card to purchase household groceries, family clothing, and gasoline for the family vehicle."  
- **Address mixed-use directly.** "The account was used primarily for personal purchases; any incidental business use was de minimis."  
- ***Slenk v. Transworld Sys., Inc.***, 236 F.3d 1072 (9th Cir. 2001), looks at primary purpose at origination — useful when the consumer originally opened the account for personal use even if business use developed later.  
  
### 3.2 Document defenses are limited at MTD  
  
If defense attaches commercial-account paperwork, plaintiff can:  
  
- Argue the document is not "integral" to the complaint and falls outside the 12(b)(6) record.  
- Argue the document does not foreclose the inference of consumer purpose (e.g., the merchant category code is non-determinative).  
- Request 12(d) conversion to MSJ with discovery under Rule 56(d).  
  
### 3.3 Sample brief  
  
> "Defendant attaches an account agreement bearing a business name but cannot show consumer-purpose pleading is implausible. The complaint alleges plaintiff used the card to buy groceries, medical supplies, and household goods (Compl. ¶ 15). *Slenk* requires primary-purpose analysis at the time of the transaction — a fact-intensive inquiry inappropriate for resolution on the pleadings."

## 4. Opposing § 1692c Communication Arguments  
  
### 4.1 Statutory-exception responses  
  
- **Attorney-of-debt-collector exception:** narrowly construed; communications with outside counsel for litigation purposes are protected, but transmission to vendors or non-attorney agents is not. *Cunningham v. Credit Mgmt., L.P.*, 2010 WL 3791104 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 28, 2010).  
- **CRA exception:** applies only to transmissions to a "consumer reporting agency" under the FCRA — not to mail vendors, scrub services, or other intermediaries.  
- **Court-process exception:** strict — only filings literally permitted by court order. Service of process is excepted; voluntary mailings to third parties are not.  
- **Consumer consent:** must be **direct, prior, express, written** consent under § 1692c(a). Consent given to the original creditor does not automatically transfer.  
  
### 4.2 Responding to *Hunstein*  
  
*Hunstein v. Preferred Collection & Mgmt. Servs., Inc.*, 48 F.4th 1236 (11th Cir. 2022) (en banc), is a **standing** decision, not a merits decision. Plaintiff distinctions:  
  
- **Outside the Eleventh Circuit, *Hunstein* is not binding.** The Second, Third, Sixth, and Seventh Circuits have considered the issue with varying results. See, e.g., *Quaglia v. NS193, LLC*, 96 F.4th 222 (2d Cir. 2024) (collecting cases).  
- **Even in the Eleventh Circuit, the merits question survives.** *Hunstein* held only that public-disclosure-of-private-facts requires "publicity to the public at large"; § 1692c(b)'s text bars "communications with third parties" without an embedded publicity element.  
- **Standing harm beyond letter-vendor transmission.** Plead specific concrete harm — emotional distress, time lost, fees paid — alongside the bare communication.  
  
### 4.3 Sample brief language  
  
> "Defendant invokes *Hunstein*, but that decision rests on Eleventh Circuit Article III analysis. This Court sits in the [X] Circuit, where *Hunstein* is not controlling. Even on its own terms, *Hunstein* assessed standing through the public-disclosure tort analogue; the complaint here pleads independent concrete harm — namely, plaintiff's emotional distress and the time she spent disputing the unauthorized communication."

## 5. Opposing § 1692e False / Misleading Arguments  
  
### 5.1 Materiality is the LSC's perspective, not the court's  
  
Defense will argue immateriality. Plaintiff responses:  
  
- **The LSC standard is generous to consumers.** The least-sophisticated consumer is "uninformed, naive, and trusting." *Clomon v. Jackson*, 988 F.2d 1314, 1318 (2d Cir. 1993).  
- **Materiality is a question for the trier of fact** in most cases. *Brown v. Card Serv. Ctr.*, 464 F.3d 450, 455 (3d Cir. 2006) (whether statements mislead is "ordinarily a question of fact").  
- **A statement can be misleading even if technically true.** *Wood v. M&J Recovery LLC*, 2007 WL 1771488 (E.D.N.Y. June 18, 2007).  
  
### 5.2 Letter-as-exhibit two-step  
  
Defense often attaches the letter and argues "the letter speaks for itself." Plaintiff response:  
  
- The letter is read **as the LSC would read it** — not as a lawyer or judge reads it. *Russell v. Equifax A.R.S.*, 74 F.3d 30, 34 (2d Cir. 1996).  
- Where the letter could plausibly mislead, the question of whether it *did* mislead is generally not appropriate for resolution on the pleadings.  
- The complaint should attach or describe **the surrounding context** — prior letters, oral statements, account confusion — that informs the LSC's reading.  
  
### 5.3 Time-barred debt and *Midland v. Johnson*  
  
*Midland Funding* applies only to **proofs of claim in bankruptcy**, not collection letters. *Midland Funding* expressly relied on the bankruptcy-court oversight context. Outside bankruptcy, time-barred debt dunning that fails to disclose the stale status remains actionable under §§ 1692e and 1692f. *McMahon v. LVNV Funding, LLC*, 744 F.3d 1010 (7th Cir. 2014) (pre-Reg F); see also Reg F § 1006.26 (codifying disclosures required for time-barred debts).  
  
### 5.4 Sample brief structure  
  
```  
III. THE § 1692e CLAIM IS ADEQUATELY PLEADED  
 A. The least-sophisticated-consumer standard is generous  
 B. Materiality is ordinarily a fact question  
 C. The letter, read as an LSC would read it, is misleading  
 D. Midland v. Johnson is limited to bankruptcy POCs and is inapposite  
```

## 6. Opposing § 1692f Unfair / Unconscionable Arguments  
  
### 6.1 The "duplicative" objection has limits  
  
Defense will argue § 1692f duplicates § 1692e. Plaintiff response:  
  
- § 1692f's catch-all extends beyond misrepresentation to encompass *practices* that are unfair regardless of truth or falsity. *LeBlanc v. Unifund CCR Partners*, 601 F.3d 1185, 1200 (11th Cir. 2010).  
- The same conduct can simultaneously violate multiple sections; that is not duplicative — it is comprehensive.  
- Pleading in the alternative is permitted under Rule 8(d)(2).  
  
### 6.2 Unauthorized-fee claim — § 1692f(1)  
  
Defense will argue the fee was permitted. Plaintiff response:  
  
- **Plead specifically which fee was unauthorized and why.** Identify the cardholder-agreement provision or applicable law that fails to authorize it.  
- **Misapplication of permitted fees is itself unauthorized.** A fee that is authorized in principle but imposed in violation of contract terms (wrong amount, premature, after waiver) is unauthorized.  
  
### 6.3 Sample brief  
  
> "Defendant argues the $25 collection fee was authorized by the cardholder agreement. But that agreement (Ex. A § 7) permits collection costs only 'reasonably incurred,' and the complaint alleges the $25 charge was a flat assessment unrelated to actual costs (Compl. ¶ 23). At the pleading stage, that is enough."

## 7. Opposing § 1692g Validation-Notice Arguments  
  
### 7.1 Sufficiency of the notice is often fact-bound  
  
Defense will argue the letter "contains all required disclosures." Plaintiff responses:  
  
- **Overshadowing.** Even a technically complete notice can be overshadowed by other letter content. *Durkin v. Equifax Check Servs., Inc.*, 406 F.3d 410, 415–16 (7th Cir. 2005). Plead specifically: deadline tension, conflicting payment demands, prominent threats.  
- **Contradiction.** Statements like "pay within 10 days to avoid further action" can contradict the 30-day validation period from the LSC's perspective.  
- **Form vs. substance.** Listing the required disclosures in fine print at the back can fail § 1692g where the LSC would not perceive them. *Swanson v. Southern Oregon Credit Service, Inc.*, 869 F.2d 1222, 1225 (9th Cir. 1988).  
  
### 7.2 Responding to the Reg F safe harbor  
  
12 C.F.R. § 1006.34(d) provides a safe harbor for use of Model Form B-1, but:  
  
- The safe harbor applies only to § 1006.34's **content and form** requirements — not to overshadowing or contradiction in other parts of the letter.  
- Deviations from Model B-1 forfeit the safe harbor; defense must show line-by-line correspondence.  
- Use of Model B-1 does not insulate violations of other FDCPA sections (e.g., § 1692e statements appearing elsewhere in the letter).  
  
### 7.3 Sample brief  
  
> "Defendant invokes the Reg F § 1006.34(d) safe harbor, but its letter departs from Model Form B-1 in [identify deviations]. The safe harbor is unavailable. Moreover, the letter's prominent 'Pay now to avoid escalation' language overshadows the validation rights — a question for the trier of fact under *Durkin*."

## 8. Opposing § 1692d Harassment Arguments  
  
### 8.1 Conduct beyond call frequency  
  
Defense will invoke the Reg F 7-in-7 presumption. Plaintiff responses:  
  
- **The 7-in-7 rule creates a rebuttable presumption.** 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2)(ii). Aggravating conduct — abusive language, false threats, calls to family members, late-night calls — defeats the presumption.  
- **§ 1692d is non-exhaustive.** § 1692d(5)'s call-frequency example is one of several harassment categories. Plaintiff should plead harassing **content**, not just frequency.  
- **Intent** under § 1692d(5) — "intent to annoy, abuse, or harass" — can be inferred from a pattern of calls after the consumer demands cessation. *Akalwadi v. Risk Mgmt. Alternatives, Inc.*, 336 F. Supp. 2d 492, 506 (D. Md. 2004).  
  
### 8.2 Sample brief  
  
> "Defendant invokes Reg F § 1006.14(b)(2)(i)'s presumption of compliance based on call count. But the rule creates only a *rebuttable* presumption. The complaint alleges defendant called plaintiff after she demanded in writing that calls stop (Compl. ¶ 22), called her workplace despite knowing her employer prohibited such calls (¶ 25), and used profane language during the August 14 call (¶ 28). Those allegations defeat the presumption and state a § 1692d claim."

## 9. Opposing Bona Fide Error at MTD  
  
The bona fide error defense is an **affirmative defense** for which the collector bears the burden by a preponderance. *Reichert v. Nat'l Credit Sys., Inc.*, 531 F.3d 1002, 1006 (9th Cir. 2008). At MTD:  
  
- The defense is rarely available because intent, procedures, and reasonable adaptation are factual.  
- *Jerman v. Carlisle*, 559 U.S. 573 (2010), forecloses the defense for legal-interpretation errors.  
- Plaintiff should object to dismissal on bona fide error grounds and reserve the issue for summary judgment after discovery into the collector's procedures.  
  
> "The bona fide error defense is unavailable at the pleading stage. *Jerman* forecloses the defense for legal errors, and the procedures inquiry is factual. Plaintiff is entitled to discovery into defendant's intake, screening, training, and supervisory procedures before this defense can be adjudicated."

## 10. Opposing Standing Motions  
  
Standing is covered in `fdcpa_plead_07_Rule_12b1_Standing_Motions.md`. Plaintiff's pleading-stage strategy:  
  
- **Plead specific concrete harm** in every complaint — emotional distress (sleep loss, anxiety), time spent disputing, money paid as a result of the unlawful conduct, reputational harm, exposure to identity-theft risk.  
- **Identify the common-law analogue** — defamation (false statement publication), intrusion upon seclusion (harassing calls), conversion (unauthorized fee), public disclosure (only with caution post-*Hunstein*).  
- **Distinguish bare procedural violations from substantive ones.** Substantive violations causing actual harm satisfy *TransUnion*; procedural notice violations may not.

## 11. Opposing Statute-of-Limitations Arguments  
  
### 11.1 *Rotkiske* did not bar equitable doctrines  
  
*Rotkiske v. Klemm*, 589 U.S. 8 (2019), rejected a *general* discovery rule but expressly preserved:  
  
- **Equitable tolling** based on extraordinary circumstances;  
- **Fraud-specific tolling** ("an equitable, fraud-specific discovery rule"), where the violation was self-concealing or the defendant actively concealed it.  
  
### 11.2 Plead concealment with specificity  
  
If the complaint relies on tolling, plead:  
  
- The specific facts showing concealment (false identity, suppressed mail, intentional misdirection);  
- The date of plaintiff's actual discovery;  
- Diligent investigation between violation and discovery.  
  
### 11.3 Each-violation accrual  
  
Each communication or act can be its own violation with its own one-year clock. Even if some letters are time-barred, others within the year remain actionable.  
  
### 11.4 Sample brief  
  
> "Defendant relies on *Rotkiske* to argue all claims are time-barred. *Rotkiske* expressly preserved equitable tolling and a fraud-specific discovery rule. 589 U.S. at 14. The complaint alleges defendant sent letters under a fictitious name and concealed its identity until plaintiff retained counsel (Compl. ¶¶ 34–38). Discovery is needed before SOL is resolved."

## 12. Opposing *Iqbal*/*Twombly* Sufficiency Challenges  
  
### 12.1 Plausibility, not probability  
  
The defense often demands evidentiary detail that *Iqbal* does not require. Plaintiff response:  
  
- Plausibility is a low bar — it requires only "enough facts to raise a reasonable expectation that discovery will reveal evidence" of liability. *Twombly*, 550 U.S. at 556.  
- The complaint need not identify every employee, every call time, or every internal procedure at the pleading stage.  
  
### 12.2 Allegations on information and belief  
  
Allegations on information and belief are appropriate where information is within defendant's control. *Arista Records*, 604 F.3d at 120; *Soo Park v. Thompson*, 851 F.3d 910, 928 (9th Cir. 2017).  
  
### 12.3 Leave to amend  
  
Where the court finds pleading deficient, plaintiff should request leave to amend under Rule 15(a)(2). Leave is "freely give[n] when justice so requires," and denial requires a showing of undue delay, bad faith, futility, or prejudice. *Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962).  
  
> "Even if the Court finds any allegation imperfect, leave to amend is freely given. Plaintiff respectfully requests an opportunity to amend any pleading deemed insufficient before dismissal with prejudice."

## 14. Sample Language Library  
  
### 14.1 Pleading-standard recital  
  
> "On a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the court must accept all well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draw all reasonable inferences in plaintiff's favor. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 678; *Erickson*, 551 U.S. at 94. Plausibility is not probability; it requires only enough facts to raise the right to relief above the speculative level. *Twombly*, 550 U.S. at 555."  
  
### 14.2 FDCPA remedial-construction principle  
  
> "The FDCPA is a remedial statute that 'should be construed broadly, so as to effect its purpose.' *Hamilton v. United Healthcare of La., Inc.*, 310 F.3d 385, 392 (5th Cir. 2002); accord *Johnson v. Riddle*, 305 F.3d 1107, 1117 (10th Cir. 2002)."  
  
### 14.3 LSC standard  
  
> "The least-sophisticated-consumer test asks how an 'uninformed, naive, [or] trusting' consumer would read the communication. *Clomon v. Jackson*, 988 F.2d 1314, 1318–19 (2d Cir. 1993). It protects vulnerable debtors while screening out bizarre interpretations."  
  
### 14.4 Fact-bound nature of debt-collector status  
  
> "Whether defendant qualifies as a debt collector is 'a quintessentially factual question' inappropriate for resolution at the pleading stage. *Goldstein*, 374 F.3d at 62. Discovery into defendant's business mix, collection volume, and revenue sources is required."  
  
### 14.5 Materiality reservation  
  
> "Materiality under § 1692e is 'ordinarily a question of fact' for trial. *Brown v. Card Serv. Ctr.*, 464 F.3d 450, 455 (3d Cir. 2006). Defendant's argument that the misstatement is immaterial as a matter of law cannot be sustained on the pleadings alone."

## 15. Tactical Practitioner Notes  
  
- **Do not concede the standing issue while opposing the merits.** Address both 12(b)(1) and 12(b)(6) arguments squarely; concession on one can imperil the other.  
- **Attach supporting authority.** Many districts permit limited additional exhibits with opposition (the dunning letter is usually already in the record). Include side-by-side comparisons of the letter with Reg F Model B-1 if the safe harbor is asserted.  
- **Preserve the record for appeal.** Where dismissal is granted, ensure the order identifies which grounds — *Iqbal*/*Twombly* dismissals without prejudice are reviewed differently than merits dismissals with prejudice.  
- **Request leave to amend by motion** if dismissal seems likely; do not rely on the court's *sua sponte* discretion.  
- **Watch the Rule 12(d) trigger.** If defendant attaches matters outside the complaint, plaintiff can request 12(d) conversion to MSJ and Rule 56(d) discovery — often a tactical advantage.
